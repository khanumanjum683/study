# Endocrine System Physiology

The endocrine system consists of glands and specialized cells that release hormones into blood or extracellular fluid. Hormones act on target cells with appropriate receptors and coordinate metabolism, growth, reproduction, stress responses, fluid balance, calcium balance, immunity, and biological rhythms.

## 1. Classification of endocrine glands

### Major endocrine glands

- **Hypothalamus:** releasing and inhibiting hormones; ADH and oxytocin synthesis.
- **Pituitary:** anterior and posterior hormones controlling growth, thyroid, adrenal cortex, gonads, lactation, water balance, and uterine/mammary function.
- **Thyroid:** T3, T4, and calcitonin.
- **Parathyroids:** parathyroid hormone (PTH).
- **Pancreatic islets:** insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, and pancreatic polypeptide.
- **Adrenal cortex:** cortisol, aldosterone, and adrenal androgens.
- **Adrenal medulla:** adrenaline and noradrenaline.
- **Pineal gland:** melatonin.
- **Thymus:** thymic hormones supporting T-cell maturation.

Other endocrine organs include the kidneys (renin, erythropoietin, calcitriol), heart (ANP/BNP), liver (IGF-1), gastrointestinal tract, placenta, adipose tissue, and skin.

### Endocrine, paracrine, and autocrine signalling

- **Endocrine:** hormone travels through blood to a distant target.
- **Paracrine:** mediator acts on nearby cells.
- **Autocrine:** mediator acts on the secreting cell itself.
- **Neuroendocrine:** a neuron releases a hormone into blood, as with hypothalamic neurons.

## 2. Hormone chemistry and receptors

### Peptide and protein hormones

Examples include insulin, glucagon, ADH, oxytocin, growth hormone, ACTH, PTH, and TSH. They are synthesized as preprohormones, processed in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi, stored in secretory vesicles, and released by exocytosis. They are generally water-soluble, circulate freely or partly bound, have short half-lives, and act through cell-surface receptors.

Common receptor pathways include:

- **Gs -> adenylyl cyclase -> cAMP -> protein kinase A**
- **Gq -> phospholipase C -> IP3/DAG -> intracellular calcium/protein kinase C**
- **Receptor tyrosine kinase:** insulin and IGF-1 receptors; activates phosphorylation cascades.
- **JAK-STAT:** growth hormone, prolactin, and several cytokines.

### Steroid hormones

Cortisol, aldosterone, adrenal androgens, oestrogen, progesterone, and testosterone are cholesterol-derived. They are synthesized on demand, poorly soluble in water, carried by plasma proteins, and usually have longer half-lives. They cross cell membranes and bind intracellular cytoplasmic or nuclear receptors. The hormone-receptor complex binds DNA hormone-response elements and changes gene transcription, so effects are slower but longer lasting.

### Amino-acid-derived hormones

- **Catecholamines:** adrenaline and noradrenaline; water-soluble and membrane-receptor mediated.
- **Thyroid hormones:** T3 and T4; lipid-soluble, protein-bound, and nuclear-receptor mediated.
- **Melatonin:** indoleamine derived from tryptophan; membrane and intracellular actions.

### Hormone action

The biological response depends on hormone concentration, receptor number and affinity, post-receptor signalling, target-cell enzymes, and interactions with other hormones. Permissiveness occurs when one hormone enables another's effect, synergy when hormones produce a greater combined effect, and antagonism when one opposes another.

## 3. Feedback regulation of hormones

### Negative feedback

Most endocrine axes use negative feedback. A hypothalamic hormone stimulates pituitary hormone, which stimulates a peripheral gland; the final hormone inhibits both hypothalamus and pituitary.

Example:

`hypothalamic TRH -> pituitary TSH -> thyroid T3/T4 -> inhibition of TRH and TSH`

Negative feedback stabilizes hormone levels and prevents excessive responses. Long-loop feedback is from the peripheral hormone to hypothalamus/pituitary; short-loop feedback is pituitary hormone inhibition of hypothalamus; ultrashort feedback is local hypothalamic regulation.

### Positive feedback

Positive feedback amplifies a process until a defined endpoint. Examples are oestrogen-induced LH surge before ovulation and oxytocin during labour. Positive feedback is uncommon because it is not inherently stabilizing.

### Other control patterns

Hormone release may be pulsatile, circadian, seasonal, or linked to the level of a regulated variable. Receptor down-regulation reduces sensitivity during prolonged high hormone exposure; up-regulation increases sensitivity during low exposure.

## 4. Anterior pituitary hormones

The anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis) contains endocrine cells controlled by hypothalamic hormones delivered through the hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal system.

| Hormone | Main stimulus | Main functions |
|---|---|---|
| GH | GHRH, sleep, exercise, hypoglycaemia; inhibited by somatostatin | Growth through IGF-1, protein synthesis, lipolysis, and reduced glucose uptake |
| Prolactin | Suckling; normally inhibited by dopamine | Mammary development and milk production |
| TSH | TRH; inhibited by T3/T4 | Thyroid growth and T3/T4 synthesis |
| ACTH | CRH, stress; inhibited by cortisol | Adrenal cortisol and androgen secretion |
| FSH | GnRH | Follicle growth and oestrogen synthesis; Sertoli-cell support and spermatogenesis |
| LH | GnRH | Ovulation and corpus luteum; Leydig-cell testosterone secretion |

### Growth hormone

GH stimulates the liver and other tissues to produce IGF-1. IGF-1 promotes cartilage, bone, and protein growth. GH also increases lipolysis and reduces glucose uptake, raising blood glucose. Secretion is pulsatile, highest during deep sleep, and inhibited by somatostatin and high IGF-1.

- **GH excess before epiphyseal closure:** gigantism.
- **GH excess after closure:** acromegaly, with enlarged hands/feet, facial changes, insulin resistance, hypertension, and organ enlargement.
- **GH deficiency in childhood:** proportionate short stature; adult deficiency may reduce muscle mass and energy.

### Prolactin

Prolactin promotes milk synthesis after breast development. Suckling reduces hypothalamic dopamine, allowing prolactin release; it also stimulates oxytocin release for milk ejection. High prolactin suppresses GnRH and may cause amenorrhoea, infertility, reduced libido, or galactorrhoea. Causes include pituitary adenoma, hypothalamic disease, pregnancy, and dopamine-blocking drugs.

### TSH, ACTH, and gonadotropins

TSH stimulates thyroid hormone synthesis and growth. TSH excess may produce hyperthyroidism; low TSH may result from pituitary or hypothalamic disease.

ACTH stimulates cortisol and adrenal androgen secretion and maintains adrenal cortical trophic support. ACTH excess can produce hypercortisolism and hyperpigmentation; low ACTH causes secondary adrenal insufficiency, usually with preserved aldosterone.

FSH and LH regulate ovarian and testicular function. Disruption causes delayed puberty, menstrual dysfunction, infertility, low testosterone, or impaired spermatogenesis.

## 5. Posterior pituitary hormones

The posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) stores and releases hormones synthesized in hypothalamic supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. Hormones travel down axons in the hypothalamic-hypophyseal tract.

### Antidiuretic hormone

ADH (vasopressin) is released when plasma osmolality rises or blood volume/pressure falls. It acts on:

- **V2 receptors** in collecting-duct principal cells through cAMP, inserting aquaporin-2 and increasing water reabsorption.
- **V1 receptors** on vascular smooth muscle through IP3/DAG, causing vasoconstriction at high concentrations.

- **Central diabetes insipidus:** inadequate ADH production/release; large volumes of dilute urine and intense thirst.
- **Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus:** renal resistance to ADH; causes include lithium, hypercalcaemia, and kidney disease.
- **SIADH:** excessive ADH causing water retention, low plasma sodium, low plasma osmolality, and inappropriately concentrated urine.

### Oxytocin

Oxytocin causes uterine contraction during labour and myoepithelial contraction for milk ejection. Cervical stretch and nipple stimulation produce positive feedback. Its effects are mediated through Gq-linked receptors and intracellular calcium.

## 6. Thyroid hormones

The thyroid contains follicles filled with colloid. Follicular cells produce T4 and T3; parafollicular C cells produce calcitonin.

### Synthesis and secretion

1. TSH stimulates iodide uptake through the sodium-iodide symporter.
2. Thyroid peroxidase oxidizes iodide and attaches iodine to tyrosine residues in thyroglobulin.
3. Iodination produces MIT and DIT; coupling of two DIT molecules forms T4, and MIT plus DIT forms T3.
4. Thyroglobulin is stored in colloid.
5. TSH stimulates endocytosis and proteolysis of thyroglobulin, releasing T3 and T4.
6. Most circulating hormone is T4; peripheral tissues convert T4 to the more active T3 or reverse T3.

T3 and T4 circulate mainly bound to thyroxine-binding globulin, transthyretin, and albumin. Free hormone enters cells and binds nuclear thyroid receptors, altering gene transcription.

### Functions

Thyroid hormones increase basal metabolic rate, oxygen consumption, heat production, carbohydrate and lipid turnover, and permissive responses to catecholamines. They are essential for CNS development, skeletal growth, cardiovascular function, gastrointestinal motility, and normal reproductive function.

### Disorders

- **Hypothyroidism:** fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, bradycardia, and slow reflexes. Causes include autoimmune thyroid destruction, iodine deficiency, thyroid surgery, or pituitary disease.
- **Hyperthyroidism:** weight loss, heat intolerance, sweating, tremor, tachycardia, anxiety, diarrhoea, and increased reflexes. Graves disease is caused by antibodies stimulating the TSH receptor.
- **Goitre:** thyroid enlargement due to stimulation, iodine deficiency, inflammation, or nodular disease.
- **Cretinism/congenital hypothyroidism:** untreated deficiency in infancy impairs brain and skeletal development.
- **Thyroid storm and myxoedema coma:** severe, life-threatening extremes of thyroid excess or deficiency.

## 7. Parathyroid hormone and calcium homeostasis

Parathyroid chief cells secrete PTH when ionized plasma calcium falls. Calcium-sensing receptors detect the change.

### PTH actions

- **Bone:** stimulates osteoblast signalling to increase osteoclast activity, releasing calcium and phosphate.
- **Kidney:** increases calcium reabsorption in the distal nephron, decreases phosphate reabsorption in the PCT, and stimulates calcitriol formation.
- **Intestine:** indirectly increases calcium and phosphate absorption through calcitriol.

The net effect is increased plasma calcium and reduced plasma phosphate. PTH secretion is inhibited by restored calcium and by calcitriol.

### Calcitriol and calcitonin

Calcitriol increases intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption and supports bone mineralization when minerals are adequate. Calcitonin from thyroid C cells lowers osteoclast activity and may reduce plasma calcium, but its role in day-to-day adult calcium control is modest.

### Calcium disorders

- **Hypocalcaemia:** tingling, muscle cramps, tetany, seizures, and prolonged QT; causes include hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, renal failure, and pancreatitis.
- **Hypercalcaemia:** weakness, constipation, polyuria, kidney stones, confusion, shortened QT, and arrhythmias; causes include hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, and excess vitamin D.
- **Rickets/osteomalacia:** defective mineralization due to vitamin D or phosphate disturbance.
- **Osteoporosis:** reduced bone mass with relatively preserved mineralization and increased fracture risk.

## 8. Pancreatic hormones

The endocrine pancreas consists of islets:

- **Beta cells:** insulin and amylin.
- **Alpha cells:** glucagon.
- **Delta cells:** somatostatin.
- **PP cells:** pancreatic polypeptide.

### Insulin

Insulin is secreted when blood glucose rises, especially after meals. Amino acids, incretins such as GLP-1 and GIP, and parasympathetic activity enhance release. Sympathetic alpha-2 activity inhibits it.

Insulin binds a receptor tyrosine kinase and promotes:

- GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue;
- glycogenesis in liver and muscle;
- glycolysis and glucose oxidation;
- triglyceride synthesis and storage;
- amino-acid uptake and protein synthesis.

It inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, lipolysis, ketogenesis, and protein breakdown.

- **Type 1 diabetes:** autoimmune beta-cell destruction and absolute insulin deficiency; prone to ketosis.
- **Type 2 diabetes:** insulin resistance with progressive beta-cell dysfunction; associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome.
- **Hypoglycaemia:** sweating, tremor, palpitations, confusion, seizures, and coma from excessive insulin, drugs, fasting, or endocrine disease.

### Glucagon

Glucagon is secreted by alpha cells during hypoglycaemia, fasting, exercise, and increased amino acids. It acts mainly on the liver through a Gs-cAMP pathway to stimulate glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, fatty-acid oxidation, and ketogenesis. It opposes insulin and protects against falling blood glucose.

Somatostatin inhibits insulin, glucagon, and many GI secretions. Pancreatic polypeptide influences pancreatic secretion, gall-bladder activity, and appetite.

## 9. Adrenal cortex

The adrenal cortex has three zones:

- **Zona glomerulosa:** mineralocorticoids, mainly aldosterone.
- **Zona fasciculata:** glucocorticoids, mainly cortisol.
- **Zona reticularis:** adrenal androgens, mainly DHEA and androstenedione.

All are steroid hormones synthesized from cholesterol and act through intracellular receptors.

### Glucocorticoids: cortisol

ACTH from the pituitary stimulates cortisol secretion through CRH. Cortisol follows a circadian rhythm, usually peaking in the early morning, and rises during stress.

Functions include:

- increases hepatic gluconeogenesis and maintains blood glucose;
- promotes protein breakdown in some tissues and lipolysis;
- permits vascular responsiveness to catecholamines;
- suppresses inflammation and immune activity;
- reduces capillary permeability and cytokine production;
- supports fetal lung maturation and helps the stress response.

- **Cushing syndrome:** excess cortisol causes central obesity, muscle wasting, thin skin, bruising, hypertension, glucose intolerance, osteoporosis, infection risk, and mood changes.
- **Addison disease/primary adrenal insufficiency:** low cortisol and aldosterone cause weakness, weight loss, hypotension, hyperkalaemia, hyponatraemia, and hyperpigmentation.
- **Secondary adrenal insufficiency:** low ACTH causes low cortisol but usually preserved aldosterone and no marked hyperpigmentation.

### Mineralocorticoids: aldosterone

Aldosterone acts on principal cells in the late distal tubule and collecting duct through intracellular mineralocorticoid receptors. It increases ENaC and Na+/K+-ATPase activity, causing sodium and water retention and potassium secretion. It also increases hydrogen secretion by intercalated cells.

Its major regulators are angiotensin II and plasma potassium; ACTH has a minor role. Aldosterone raises extracellular volume and blood pressure.

- **Primary hyperaldosteronism:** hypertension, hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis, and low renin.
- **Hypoaldosteronism:** salt wasting, hypotension, hyperkalaemia, and acidosis.

### Adrenal androgens

DHEA and related androgens contribute to pubic and axillary hair, libido, and androgen effects, particularly in females. Excess may cause virilization; congenital adrenal hyperplasia can impair cortisol synthesis and increase androgen production.

## 10. Adrenal medulla

Chromaffin cells are modified sympathetic postganglionic neurons. Preganglionic sympathetic fibres release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors, causing adrenaline and noradrenaline secretion.

Catecholamines act through alpha and beta receptors:

- beta-1: increased heart rate and contractility;
- beta-2: bronchodilation and skeletal-muscle vasodilation;
- alpha-1: vasoconstriction and increased blood pressure;
- beta-3: lipolysis.

They produce the rapid fight-or-flight response: increased cardiac output, blood flow to muscle, glucose availability, bronchodilation, pupil dilation, and reduced GI activity.

**Phaeochromocytoma** is a catecholamine-secreting tumour causing episodic or sustained hypertension, headache, sweating, palpitations, tremor, and weight loss.

## 11. Thymus and pineal gland

### Thymus

The thymus is prominent in childhood and involutes after puberty. Thymic epithelial cells produce thymosin, thymopoietin, and related factors that support T-lymphocyte maturation and selection. It establishes central immune tolerance by removing strongly self-reactive T cells. Its function links endocrine development and adaptive immunity.

### Pineal gland

The pineal gland secretes **melatonin**, synthesized from serotonin. Retinal light information reaches the suprachiasmatic nucleus, then the sympathetic pathway to the pineal. Darkness increases melatonin; light suppresses it.

Melatonin helps synchronize circadian rhythms, promotes sleep timing, and signals seasonal changes. Jet lag and shift work disturb the light-melatonin-clock relationship.

## 12. Integrated endocrine correlations

1. **Hypothalamus-pituitary-peripheral axes:** hypothalamic signals control pituitary hormones, which control thyroid, adrenal, and gonadal glands; peripheral hormones provide negative feedback.
2. **Metabolism:** thyroid hormone sets metabolic rate, insulin stores nutrients, glucagon mobilizes them, cortisol maintains glucose during stress, and adrenaline provides rapid fuel.
3. **Stress response:** hypothalamic CRH-ACTH-cortisol provides slower metabolic and anti-inflammatory support, while sympathetic-adrenal catecholamines provide immediate cardiovascular and metabolic responses.
4. **Blood pressure and volume:** aldosterone retains sodium, ADH retains water, cortisol permits vascular catecholamine action, and PTH/calcitriol regulate minerals needed for muscle and vascular function.
5. **Kidney-endocrine integration:** kidneys activate vitamin D, release EPO and renin, and are major targets of ADH and aldosterone. Renal failure therefore causes anaemia, bone-mineral disease, hypertension, and acid-base disturbance.
6. **Calcium and neuromuscular function:** PTH, calcitriol, and calcitonin maintain calcium for bone, neurotransmitter release, muscle contraction, coagulation, and cardiac electrical stability.
7. **GI-pancreas-liver axis:** nutrients stimulate insulin, glucagon, incretins, and somatostatin; the liver then stores, distributes, and transforms absorbed nutrients.
8. **Growth:** GH and IGF-1 require adequate thyroid hormone, insulin, nutrition, and sex steroids. Cortisol excess antagonizes growth and bone formation.
9. **Immune-endocrine links:** cortisol suppresses inflammation, thymus supports T-cell maturation, thyroid and metabolic hormones influence immune activity, and cytokines can alter endocrine axes during illness.
10. **Circadian coordination:** the pineal and suprachiasmatic nucleus influence sleep and hormone timing; cortisol, GH, melatonin, insulin, and body temperature follow daily patterns.
11. **Clinical localization:** primary gland disease changes the target hormone and feedback signal in opposite directions from secondary pituitary disease. Measuring both helps identify the level of dysfunction.

## High-yield summary

Endocrine physiology is organized around chemical communication and feedback. Peptide and catecholamine hormones act mainly through membrane receptors and second messengers, while steroid and thyroid hormones act mainly through intracellular receptors and gene transcription. The pituitary coordinates peripheral glands, the thyroid sets metabolism, parathyroid hormone controls calcium, pancreas controls fuel storage and mobilization, adrenals coordinate stress and volume, thymus supports immune maturation, and pineal melatonin coordinates biological time. Their effects overlap continuously to maintain homeostasis.
