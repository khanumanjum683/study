# Reproductive System Physiology

The reproductive system produces gametes, enables fertilization, supports development of a new individual, and secretes hormones that influence growth, metabolism, behaviour, bone, muscle, and reproductive cycles. Its central control is the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis:

`hypothalamic GnRH -> pituitary FSH/LH -> gonads -> gametes + sex steroids + inhibin`

Sex steroids and inhibin usually provide negative feedback. Sustained high oestrogen before ovulation produces positive feedback and the LH surge.

## 1. Introduction and physiological functions

### Male functions

- Production of spermatozoa
- Secretion of testosterone and other androgens
- Transport and deposition of sperm
- Production of seminal fluid
- Sexual differentiation, libido, erection, and ejaculation

### Female functions

- Production of ova
- Secretion of oestrogens, progesterone, inhibin, and other hormones
- Reception and transport of sperm
- Support of fertilization, implantation, pregnancy, birth, and lactation
- Cyclic preparation of the reproductive tract

Reproduction is closely linked to endocrine, nervous, cardiovascular, renal, nutritional, and immune systems. Adequate energy, blood flow, hormone receptors, and a functioning hypothalamus are necessary for fertility.

## 2. Sex differentiation

### Chromosomal and genetic sex

At fertilization, the ovum contributes an X chromosome and the sperm contributes X or Y. XX generally produces an ovarian developmental pathway, while XY generally produces a testicular pathway. Variations such as XO, XXY, and differences in sex development demonstrate that chromosomal, gonadal, internal, external, and phenotypic sex are related but distinct levels.

### Gonadal differentiation

The early gonad contains a medulla and cortex and can develop as either testis or ovary. The SRY gene on the Y chromosome usually initiates testis formation through testis-determining pathways. Sertoli cells then produce anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH), and Leydig cells produce testosterone.

Without testicular hormones, the indifferent gonad follows ovarian development, the paramesonephric (Mullerian) ducts form female internal structures, and the mesonephric (Wolffian) ducts regress.

### Internal and external differentiation

- Sertoli-cell AMH causes Mullerian duct regression in a typical male pathway.
- Testosterone maintains and differentiates Wolffian ducts into epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and ejaculatory ducts.
- Dihydrotestosterone (DHT), formed from testosterone by 5-alpha-reductase, supports prostate, penis, scrotum, and male external genital development.
- In the typical female pathway, absence of AMH allows Mullerian structures to form the uterine tubes, uterus, cervix, and upper vagina. Absence of high androgen action permits female external development.

## 3. Puberty

Puberty begins when pulsatile GnRH secretion increases after childhood suppression. GnRH stimulates FSH and LH, which activate the gonads.

### Changes in both sexes

- Growth spurt and maturation of bones
- Development of secondary sexual characteristics
- Increased gonadal steroid secretion
- Changes in body composition, skin, hair, mood, and sexual interest
- Maturation of reproductive capacity

### Male puberty

LH stimulates Leydig-cell testosterone production. FSH acts on Sertoli cells and, with testosterone, supports spermatogenesis. Testicular enlargement usually precedes rapid penile growth, pubic and axillary hair, voice deepening, increased muscle mass, facial hair, and increased erythropoiesis.

### Female puberty

FSH promotes follicular growth and oestrogen secretion; LH supports theca-cell androgen production, ovulation, and luteal function. Breast development, widening of the pelvis, female fat distribution, pubic/axillary hair, and menarche occur. Early cycles may be anovulatory because the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis is still maturing.

## 4. Male reproductive system

### Structure

The testes lie in the scrotum, where a temperature slightly below core temperature supports sperm production. Each testis contains seminiferous tubules surrounded by interstitial Leydig cells.

The sperm pathway is:

`seminiferous tubules -> rete testis -> efferent ductules -> epididymis -> vas deferens -> ejaculatory duct -> urethra`

Accessory glands are the seminal vesicles, prostate, and bulbourethral glands. The penis deposits semen in the female reproductive tract.

### Functions of testosterone

Testosterone is produced mainly by Leydig cells under LH stimulation. It supports fetal male differentiation, puberty, libido, spermatogenesis, muscle and bone growth, male body hair, sebaceous activity, and erythropoiesis. It is converted to DHT in some tissues and to oestradiol in others.

Testosterone and inhibin B suppress GnRH/FSH/LH through negative feedback. In the male, high intratesticular testosterone is essential for spermatogenesis even when blood testosterone is normal.

### Semen

Seminal vesicles produce fructose-rich fluid with prostaglandins and proteins. The prostate contributes citrate, enzymes, zinc, and alkaline fluid. Bulbourethral glands secrete mucus. Semen nourishes sperm, buffers the vaginal environment, and supports transport.

## 5. Spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis occurs in seminiferous tubules from puberty onward and takes about 64–74 days, followed by epididymal maturation.

1. **Spermatogonia:** diploid stem cells undergo mitosis.
2. **Primary spermatocytes:** enter meiosis I.
3. **Secondary spermatocytes:** haploid cells produced after meiosis I; rapidly undergo meiosis II.
4. **Spermatids:** haploid products of meiosis II.
5. **Spermiogenesis:** spermatids become spermatozoa by forming an acrosome, condensing the nucleus, developing a flagellum, and shedding excess cytoplasm.
6. **Spermiation:** mature spermatozoa are released into the tubule lumen and gain motility and fertilizing capacity in the epididymis.

Sertoli cells nourish germ cells, form the blood-testis barrier, secrete androgen-binding protein, inhibin B, AMH in fetal life, and support release of sperm. FSH acts mainly through Sertoli cells; LH acts on Leydig cells. Sperm production requires FSH, high local testosterone, normal temperature, nutrition, and an intact seminiferous epithelium.

## 6. Female reproductive system

### Structure

The ovaries contain follicles and produce ova and hormones. The uterine tubes receive the ovulated oocyte and are the usual site of fertilization. The uterus supports implantation and pregnancy; its body and fundus are mainly muscular, while the cervix produces mucus and forms the lower canal. The vagina receives the penis, allows menstrual flow, and forms the birth canal.

The external genitalia include the vulva, labia, clitoris, and vestibular structures. Mammary glands are accessory reproductive organs specialized for lactation.

### Oestrogen

Oestrogens, especially oestradiol, are produced by developing follicles and the corpus luteum; the placenta becomes an important source during pregnancy.

Functions include growth of female reproductive organs, breast duct development, proliferation of endometrium, maintenance of bone, favourable lipid effects, increased skin vascularity, and development of secondary sexual characteristics. Oestrogen increases progesterone receptors in the endometrium and, when sustained at a high level, produces positive feedback leading to the LH surge.

### Progesterone

Progesterone is produced mainly by the corpus luteum after ovulation and by the placenta during pregnancy. It converts the proliferative endometrium into a secretory endometrium, supports implantation, reduces uterine contractility, thickens cervical mucus, raises basal body temperature, and promotes lobuloalveolar breast development. It provides negative feedback on GnRH, FSH, and LH.

## 7. Oogenesis

Oogenesis begins before birth. Oogonia multiply and become primary oocytes, which enter meiosis I and arrest in prophase I. At puberty, each cycle recruits follicles; usually one dominant follicle completes meiosis I to form a secondary oocyte and a small first polar body.

The secondary oocyte begins meiosis II and arrests at metaphase II. It completes meiosis II only after sperm entry, producing the mature ovum and second polar body. Unlike spermatogenesis, cytokinesis is unequal, preserving cytoplasm in one large ovum. The ovarian reserve declines with age, and meiotic errors become more common later in reproductive life.

## 8. Ovulation and menstrual cycle

The ovarian and uterine cycles are coordinated by FSH, LH, oestrogen, progesterone, inhibin, and local ovarian signals. A typical cycle is approximately 28 days, but normal cycles vary.

### Follicular/proliferative phase

Early in the cycle, low oestrogen and progesterone allow FSH to rise. FSH stimulates follicular growth. Granulosa cells convert theca-cell androgens to oestrogen through aromatase. Oestrogen causes endometrial proliferation and cervical mucus becomes thin and watery. Inhibin B reduces FSH as the dominant follicle develops.

### Ovulation

Sustained high oestradiol changes feedback from negative to positive. The resulting LH surge causes completion of meiosis I, follicular wall breakdown, and release of the secondary oocyte. Ovulation usually occurs about 14 days before the next menstruation, not necessarily on cycle day 14.

### Luteal/secretory phase

The ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone, oestrogen, and inhibin A. Progesterone makes the endometrium secretory and receptive, thickens cervical mucus, and raises basal temperature. If fertilization and implantation do not occur, the corpus luteum regresses, steroid levels fall, and menstruation begins.

### Menstruation

Withdrawal of ovarian steroids causes spiral-artery constriction, endometrial ischemia, tissue breakdown, and bleeding. Prostaglandins contribute to uterine contractions and menstrual pain. The cycle then begins again as FSH rises.

## 9. Fertilization and early pregnancy

Fertilization usually occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube. Sperm undergo capacitation, bind to the zona pellucida, and undergo the acrosome reaction. Fusion with the oocyte triggers a cortical reaction that blocks polyspermy. The zygote divides while travelling to the uterus, forms a blastocyst, and implants in the endometrium.

The early trophoblast secretes **human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)**. hCG maintains the corpus luteum until the placenta produces sufficient progesterone and oestrogen. Later, placental hormones take over support of the endometrium.

## 10. Physiological changes during pregnancy

### Hormonal and reproductive changes

Progesterone maintains uterine quiescence and breast development. Oestrogen promotes uterine growth, breast duct growth, and increased blood flow. hCG supports the corpus luteum early. Human placental lactogen changes maternal metabolism to increase glucose availability for the fetus and mobilize maternal fat.

### Cardiovascular changes

Plasma volume, heart rate, stroke volume, and cardiac output increase. Systemic vascular resistance often falls because of placental and hormonal vasodilation. A benign flow murmur may occur. Supine late pregnancy can compress the inferior vena cava and reduce venous return.

### Respiratory changes

Progesterone increases ventilatory drive. Tidal volume and minute ventilation rise, producing mild respiratory alkalosis with renal bicarbonate compensation. Oxygen consumption increases, especially later in pregnancy.

### Renal and metabolic changes

Renal blood flow and GFR increase. Glucose handling changes and mild glycosuria may occur. Maternal insulin resistance increases later in pregnancy, while the pancreas increases insulin secretion; failure to compensate produces gestational diabetes.

### Gastrointestinal and blood changes

Smooth-muscle relaxation may cause reflux and constipation. Plasma volume expands more than red-cell mass, producing physiological dilutional anaemia. Clotting factors and venous stasis increase thromboembolic risk.

## 11. Pregnancy tests

Pregnancy tests detect hCG or its beta subunit.

- **Urine immunoassay:** convenient and usually positive after a missed period, but dilute urine or testing too early can give a false negative.
- **Serum beta-hCG:** more sensitive and can be quantified; serial measurements help assess early pregnancy development.
- **Ultrasound:** does not detect hCG directly but confirms location, gestational development, and viability.

## 12. Parturition

Parturition is childbirth. Near term, increased uterine stretch, changes in progesterone/oestrogen balance, prostaglandins, fetal cortisol, and oxytocin receptors make the uterus more excitable.

1. **Cervical ripening and dilation:** the cervix softens and the uterus develops coordinated contractions.
2. **Expulsion:** stronger contractions push the fetus through the birth canal.
3. **Placental stage:** continued contractions separate and expel the placenta and reduce bleeding.

The Ferguson reflex is positive feedback: fetal head and cervical stretch increase hypothalamic oxytocin release; oxytocin increases myometrial contraction, causing more stretch. Prostaglandins amplify contractions and cervical ripening.

## 13. Lactation

### Mammary preparation

Oestrogen promotes duct growth; progesterone promotes lobuloalveolar development. Prolactin supports milk synthesis, but high oestrogen and progesterone during pregnancy inhibit full secretion.

### Milk production and ejection

After delivery, the fall in placental oestrogen and progesterone permits prolactin action. Suckling reduces hypothalamic dopamine and increases prolactin, maintaining milk production. Suckling also stimulates oxytocin, which contracts myoepithelial cells and produces milk ejection.

Colostrum is the early, antibody-rich secretion, especially rich in secretory IgA. Mature milk contains water, lactose, fat, proteins, vitamins, minerals, growth factors, and immune components. Prolactin mainly produces milk; oxytocin ejects it.

## 14. Male contraceptive methods

- **Condoms:** block sperm deposition and also reduce transmission of many sexually transmitted infections.
- **Vasectomy:** interrupts the vas deferens; sperm production continues but sperm do not enter semen. It is not immediately effective until stored sperm are cleared.
- **Withdrawal:** unreliable because timing is difficult and pre-ejaculatory fluid may contain sperm.
- **Periodic abstinence/fertility awareness:** effectiveness depends on accurate cycle and fertile-window assessment.
- **Hormonal and non-hormonal male methods:** testosterone-based combinations suppress GnRH, FSH, and LH and reduce spermatogenesis; availability varies and side effects/return of fertility must be considered.

## 15. Female contraceptive methods

- **Combined oral, transdermal, or vaginal hormonal methods:** oestrogen plus progestin suppress FSH/LH and ovulation, thicken cervical mucus, and alter endometrium.
- **Progestin-only pills, injections, implants, and hormonal intrauterine systems:** suppress ovulation variably, thicken mucus, and thin endometrium.
- **Copper intrauterine device:** releases copper, producing a local sperm-toxic inflammatory response; it is also effective for emergency contraception when inserted promptly.
- **Barrier methods:** condoms, diaphragms, and cervical caps prevent sperm entry; condoms also reduce STI transmission.
- **Fertility-awareness methods:** identify the fertile interval using cycle timing, cervical mucus, basal temperature, or urine hormones.
- **Emergency contraception:** levonorgestrel or ulipristal tablets delay ovulation; a copper IUD is highly effective when suitable.
- **Tubal occlusion:** prevents sperm and ovum from meeting and is intended as permanent contraception.

Contraceptive choice depends on effectiveness, safety, reversibility, access, medical conditions, age, fertility goals, and STI risk. Only condoms provide substantial STI protection.

## 16. Correlation of reproductive physiology

1. **The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis coordinates the system:** pulsatile GnRH controls FSH/LH, FSH supports gamete cells, LH stimulates steroid-producing cells, and sex steroids/inhibin provide feedback.
2. **Gametogenesis requires both hormones and local support:** testosterone acts inside the testis with FSH and Sertoli cells; oocyte maturation depends on follicular granulosa/theca cooperation and pituitary cycles.
3. **Sex steroids link reproduction to the whole body:** testosterone supports muscle, bone, erythropoiesis, and libido; oestrogen supports bone and vascular function; progesterone coordinates uterus, temperature, and breasts.
4. **The menstrual cycle is a coordinated ovarian-uterine cycle:** follicular oestrogen proliferates endometrium, the LH surge ovulates, and luteal progesterone creates a secretory implantation-ready lining.
5. **Pregnancy changes every major system:** placental hormones alter cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, metabolic, blood, and GI physiology to support maternal and fetal needs.
6. **The placenta is an endocrine organ:** hCG maintains early luteal function, then placental oestrogen/progesterone maintain pregnancy; placental lactogen changes maternal fuel use.
7. **Parturition and lactation use positive feedback:** cervical stretch produces oxytocin and stronger contractions; suckling produces prolactin for milk synthesis and oxytocin for milk ejection.
8. **Fertility depends on timing:** contraception works by preventing ovulation, sperm transport, fertilization, implantation, or sperm production, and therefore targets different points in the reproductive sequence.
9. **Nutrition and stress matter:** low energy availability, excessive exercise, severe illness, hyperprolactinaemia, thyroid disease, and high cortisol can suppress GnRH and impair fertility.
10. **Reproductive and calcium systems interact:** oestrogen and testosterone preserve bone, pregnancy increases calcium transfer to the fetus, and lactation shifts calcium handling through PTH-related peptide and renal/gut adaptations.
11. **Reproductive and cardiovascular systems interact:** erection requires parasympathetic vasodilation, pregnancy increases cardiac output, and sexual hormones influence vascular and thrombotic risk.
12. **Clinical localization uses feedback:** low gonadal hormone with high FSH/LH suggests primary gonadal failure; low hormone with low or inappropriately normal FSH/LH suggests hypothalamic or pituitary disease.

## High-yield summary

Reproductive physiology is a continuous sequence from sex differentiation and puberty to gamete production, ovulation or spermatogenesis, fertilization, implantation, pregnancy, birth, and lactation. GnRH, FSH, LH, testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, inhibin, hCG, prolactin, and oxytocin act at different stages but form one coordinated system. Feedback, timing, nutrition, blood flow, and the condition of target organs determine reproductive function.
