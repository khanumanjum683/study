# Renal System and Body Temperature Physiology

## 1. Functions of the kidneys

The kidneys are paired retroperitoneal organs that maintain the composition and volume of the internal environment. Each kidney contains about one million nephrons.

### Excretory and regulatory functions

- Excrete nitrogenous wastes: urea, uric acid, creatinine, and drug metabolites.
- Regulate extracellular fluid volume and osmolality.
- Regulate sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphate, chloride, and bicarbonate balance.
- Maintain acid-base balance by secreting hydrogen ions and producing/reabsorbing bicarbonate.
- Regulate arterial pressure through sodium-water balance and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.
- Produce urine with an appropriate concentration and composition.

### Non-excretory functions

- **Endocrine:** release renin, erythropoietin, and renal prostaglandins.
- **Vitamin D activation:** convert 25-hydroxyvitamin D to active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), which supports intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption.
- **Glucose metabolism:** perform gluconeogenesis during prolonged fasting.
- **Hormone metabolism:** degrade and clear several peptide hormones and biologically active substances.
- **Osmoreceptor and volume responses:** participate in the body's response to dehydration, haemorrhage, and changes in salt intake.

The kidneys therefore connect the cardiovascular, endocrine, respiratory, skeletal, and nervous systems. A change in renal function affects blood pressure, blood volume, pH, bone mineral balance, red-cell production, and temperature-related fluid conservation.

## 2. Structure of the nephron

The **nephron** is the functional unit of the kidney. It consists of a renal corpuscle and renal tubule.

### Renal corpuscle

- **Glomerulus:** a tuft of fenestrated capillaries supplied by the afferent arteriole and drained by the efferent arteriole.
- **Bowman's capsule:** double-walled capsule surrounding the glomerulus. The visceral layer contains podocytes; the parietal layer forms the outer wall.
- **Bowman's space:** receives the ultrafiltrate.

The glomerular filtration barrier has fenestrated endothelium, a negatively charged basement membrane, and podocyte slit diaphragms. It allows water and small solutes to pass but normally retains cells and most plasma proteins.

### Renal tubule

1. **Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT):** highly folded epithelium with a brush border and many mitochondria; bulk reabsorption.
2. **Loop of Henle:** descending and ascending limbs create the medullary osmotic gradient.
3. **Distal convoluted tubule (DCT):** selective sodium, chloride, calcium, and acid-base regulation.
4. **Connecting tubule and collecting duct:** final regulation of sodium, potassium, hydrogen ions, urea, and water.

The collecting ducts pass through the cortex and medulla and drain into papillary ducts, minor calyces, renal pelvis, ureter, bladder, and urethra.

### Types of nephrons

- **Cortical nephrons:** about 80–85%; have short loops of Henle extending only into the outer medulla. They perform most filtration and routine tubular processing.
- **Juxtamedullary nephrons:** about 15–20%; have long loops extending deep into the medulla and long vasa recta. They are essential for producing concentrated urine.

## 3. Juxtaglomerular apparatus

The juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA) lies where the thick ascending limb/early DCT contacts the vascular pole of its own glomerulus.

### Structure

- **Macula densa:** specialized DCT cells that sense tubular sodium chloride delivery and flow.
- **Juxtaglomerular or granular cells:** modified smooth-muscle cells in the afferent arteriole that store and secrete renin; they also act as pressure sensors.
- **Extraglomerular mesangial cells:** support the apparatus and help transmit signals between macula densa and arterioles.

### Functions

1. **Tubuloglomerular feedback:** high NaCl delivery causes macula densa signalling that constricts the afferent arteriole and reduces GFR. Low NaCl delivery promotes afferent dilation and renin release.
2. **Renin release:** low renal perfusion pressure, sympathetic beta-1 stimulation, or low macular NaCl activates renin.
3. **Blood pressure regulation:** renin initiates angiotensin II and aldosterone formation, increasing vascular tone and sodium-water retention.
4. **GFR stabilization:** feedback helps prevent excessive changes in glomerular filtration when arterial pressure changes.

## 4. Glomerular filtration rate

### Definition and normal value

**Glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** is the volume of filtrate formed by all glomeruli per unit time. Normal adult GFR is approximately **125 mL/min**, or about **180 L/day**, when adjusted for a standard body surface area of 1.73 m². It is usually somewhat lower in women and declines with age.

The filtrate resembles plasma without cells and most proteins. Despite producing about 180 L/day of filtrate, the kidneys excrete only about 1–2 L/day of urine because most water and useful solutes are reabsorbed.

### Filtration forces

Net filtration pressure is determined by Starling forces:

$$NFP=(P_{GC}-P_{BS})-(\pi_{GC}-\pi_{BS})$$

where $P_{GC}$ is glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure, $P_{BS}$ is Bowman's-space hydrostatic pressure, $\pi_{GC}$ is glomerular capillary oncotic pressure, and $\pi_{BS}$ is Bowman's-space oncotic pressure, normally near zero.

GFR also depends on the filtration coefficient:

$$GFR=K_f\times NFP$$

$K_f$ depends on filtration surface area and hydraulic permeability.

### Factors affecting GFR

**Increase GFR:** moderate afferent dilation, moderate efferent constriction, increased glomerular capillary pressure, increased filtration surface area/permeability, and reduced plasma oncotic pressure.

**Decrease GFR:** afferent constriction, severe efferent constriction, efferent dilation, increased Bowman's-space pressure from urinary obstruction, increased plasma oncotic pressure, reduced renal perfusion, glomerular disease, and loss of functioning nephrons.

Sympathetic activation constricts renal vessels and lowers GFR during severe stress or haemorrhage. Prostaglandins and nitric oxide help preserve renal blood flow by dilating vessels. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs can reduce prostaglandin-mediated afferent dilation, especially when renal perfusion is already compromised.

### Autoregulation

Within a moderate arterial-pressure range, renal blood flow and GFR remain relatively stable through:

- **Myogenic response:** increased pressure stretches afferent arteriolar muscle, causing constriction; reduced pressure causes relaxation.
- **Tubuloglomerular feedback:** macula densa adjusts afferent tone and renin release according to distal NaCl delivery.

## 5. Tubular reabsorption

Tubular reabsorption is movement of water and useful solutes from tubular fluid into peritubular capillaries or vasa recta. It prevents loss of substances that the body needs.

### Mechanisms

- **Active transport:** direct ATP use, especially basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase.
- **Secondary active transport:** sodium gradient drives cotransport or countertransport, for example sodium-glucose and sodium-amino-acid cotransport.
- **Passive diffusion:** movement down electrochemical or concentration gradients.
- **Osmosis:** water follows reabsorbed solute, mainly through aquaporins or paracellular pathways.
- **Endocytosis:** filtered proteins are taken up in the proximal tubule.
- **Solvent drag:** solutes move with water through paracellular pathways.

The basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase maintains low intracellular sodium and supplies the main driving force for proximal and distal transport.

### Sites and substances reabsorbed

#### Proximal convoluted tubule

The PCT reabsorbs approximately 65–70% of filtered sodium and water, about 80–90% of bicarbonate, nearly all glucose and amino acids under normal conditions, and large amounts of chloride, potassium, phosphate, and uric acid. It also reabsorbs filtered small proteins.

Glucose and amino acids enter through sodium-linked cotransporters and leave across the basolateral membrane. Hydrogen secretion supports bicarbonate reclamation. Water follows solute through aquaporin-1 and paracellular routes, so PCT fluid remains approximately iso-osmotic.

When the filtered glucose load exceeds transport maximum, glucose appears in urine. This produces osmotic diuresis, as in uncontrolled diabetes mellitus.

#### Loop of Henle

- **Thin descending limb:** highly permeable to water and relatively less permeable to solutes; water leaves into the hyperosmotic medulla.
- **Thin ascending limb:** relatively impermeable to water and may permit passive NaCl movement.
- **Thick ascending limb:** actively reabsorbs Na+, K+, and 2Cl- through NKCC2; it is impermeable to water and dilutes tubular fluid. It is called the diluting segment.

#### Distal convoluted tubule

The early DCT reabsorbs NaCl through NCC and is relatively impermeable to water. Calcium reabsorption is stimulated by parathyroid hormone. The late DCT and connecting tubule respond to aldosterone and other hormones.

#### Collecting duct

Principal cells reabsorb sodium through ENaC and secrete potassium under aldosterone influence. ADH inserts aquaporin-2 channels in the luminal membrane, increasing water reabsorption. Intercalated cells regulate acid-base balance: alpha-intercalated cells secrete hydrogen and reabsorb bicarbonate, while beta-intercalated cells secrete bicarbonate.

## 6. Tubular secretion

Tubular secretion is movement from peritubular blood into tubular fluid. It adds substances to the filtrate and helps eliminate them or regulate plasma composition.

### Sites and substances

- **PCT:** hydrogen ions, ammonium, creatinine, uric acid, bile salts, drugs, toxins, and organic acids/bases such as para-aminohippurate.
- **Distal tubule and collecting duct:** potassium secretion by principal cells; hydrogen secretion by alpha-intercalated cells; ammonia/ammonium handling.
- **Collecting duct:** final hydrogen and potassium secretion, regulated by aldosterone, plasma potassium, acid-base status, and flow.

### Mechanisms

Secretion may occur by active transporters, organic anion/cation transport systems, Na+/H+ exchange, H+-ATPase, H+/K+-ATPase, and diffusion of ammonia. Secretion is important for potassium balance, acid-base regulation, and rapid elimination of protein-bound drugs that are not freely filtered.

The phrase “tubular reabsorption” applies to movement from tubule to blood; secretion is movement from blood to tubule.

## 7. Countercurrent mechanism and concentration of urine

The kidney produces concentrated urine by creating and preserving a hyperosmotic medullary interstitium. This requires the loop of Henle, vasa recta, collecting ducts, urea recycling, and ADH.

### Countercurrent multiplier

The thick ascending limb actively pumps NaCl into the medullary interstitium but does not allow water to follow. This creates a small transverse osmotic difference. The descending limb is water-permeable, so water leaves into the increasingly concentrated interstitium. Fluid then moves into the ascending limb, where more NaCl is pumped out. Repetition along the length of the loop multiplies the gradient from cortex to inner medulla.

### Countercurrent exchanger

The vasa recta are hairpin-shaped capillaries. Slow blood flow and countercurrent exchange allow them to supply the medulla without washing away its osmotic gradient. Solute enters descending vasa recta and leaves ascending vasa recta; water movement occurs in the opposite directions.

### Urea recycling

ADH increases urea permeability in the inner medullary collecting duct. Urea enters the medullary interstitium and some enters the thin limb, then returns to the collecting duct. Urea contributes substantially to inner-medullary osmolality.

### Role of ADH

When plasma osmolality rises or blood volume falls, ADH increases aquaporin-2 insertion in collecting-duct cells. Water leaves the duct into the hyperosmotic medulla, producing concentrated urine. Without ADH, the collecting duct remains relatively water-impermeable and dilute urine is excreted.

## 8. Obligatory and facultative water reabsorption

- **Obligatory reabsorption:** water reabsorbed independently of ADH, mainly in the PCT and descending limb. About 80–85% of filtered water is reabsorbed in these segments.
- **Facultative reabsorption:** variable water reabsorption in the late distal tubule and collecting ducts under ADH control. It adjusts final urine volume and concentration according to hydration and volume status.

Thirst and ADH act together. ADH conserves water through the kidney, while thirst increases intake. A fall in blood volume can stimulate ADH even if plasma osmolality is low.

## 9. Micturition reflex

Micturition is the coordinated emptying of the urinary bladder.

1. Urine enters the bladder and stretches the detrusor wall.
2. Stretch receptors send afferents through pelvic nerves to the sacral spinal cord, mainly S2–S4.
3. Parasympathetic pelvic nerves contract the detrusor and relax the internal urethral sphincter.
4. The external urethral sphincter is skeletal muscle supplied by the pudendal nerve and is under voluntary control.
5. Pontine and cortical centres coordinate storage and voiding. In infancy the reflex is mainly spinal; mature control permits postponement or initiation.

During storage, sympathetic activity relaxes the detrusor and contracts the bladder neck, while pudendal activity maintains the external sphincter. During voiding, parasympathetic activity dominates.

## 10. Diuretics

Diuretics increase urine formation by reducing tubular sodium reabsorption, so water remains in the tubular fluid.

| Class | Main site/mechanism | Important effects or uses |
|---|---|---|
| Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors | PCT; reduce bicarbonate reabsorption | Mild diuresis; metabolic acidosis may occur |
| Osmotic diuretics | Filtered but poorly reabsorbed solutes retain water, mainly PCT/descending limb | Reduce intracranial or intraocular pressure; expand extracellular fluid initially |
| Loop diuretics | Thick ascending limb; inhibit NKCC2 | Powerful diuresis; can cause hypokalaemia, hypocalcaemia, and ototoxicity |
| Thiazides | Early DCT; inhibit NCC | Hypertension; can cause hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia, and increased calcium retention |
| Potassium-sparing diuretics | Collecting duct; block ENaC or aldosterone receptors | Limit potassium loss; hyperkalaemia risk |
| ADH antagonists | Collecting duct; reduce ADH effect | Water diuresis in selected hyponatraemic states |

Diuretics may alter volume, potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, acid-base balance, and blood pressure. Their effects must be interpreted with the nephron segment they block.

## 11. Artificial kidney and dialysis

An artificial kidney replaces some functions of failed kidneys. **Haemodialysis** pumps blood through a semipermeable dialyser against dialysate. Diffusion removes small wastes such as urea and excess electrolytes; ultrafiltration removes water through a pressure gradient.

**Peritoneal dialysis** uses the peritoneal membrane as the exchange surface. Dialysate is placed in the peritoneal cavity, allowed to equilibrate, and then drained. Glucose or another osmotic agent draws water across the membrane.

Dialysis can correct uraemia, hyperkalaemia, acidosis, and fluid overload, but it does not fully replace endocrine functions such as EPO production or vitamin D activation. Long-term renal replacement may require transplantation.

## 12. Renal function tests and clearance

### Clearance principle

Clearance is the virtual volume of plasma completely cleared of a substance per unit time:

$$C_x=\frac{U_x\times V}{P_x}$$

where $U_x$ is urine concentration, $V$ is urine flow per minute, and $P_x$ is plasma concentration.

### Important tests

- **Inulin clearance:** ideal measure of GFR because inulin is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted. It is accurate but impractical for routine use.
- **Creatinine clearance:** estimates GFR. Creatinine is produced by muscle and is filtered with a small amount of tubular secretion, so it slightly overestimates GFR.
- **Estimated GFR:** calculated from serum creatinine with age, sex, and other variables; useful for staging chronic kidney disease.
- **PAH clearance:** estimates effective renal plasma flow because PAH is filtered and strongly secreted in one renal pass.
- **Filtration fraction:** $FF=\frac{GFR}{renal\ plasma\ flow}$, normally about 20%.
- **Urinalysis:** detects protein, glucose, blood, ketones, cells, casts, specific gravity, pH, and infection markers.
- **Concentration tests:** urine osmolality and response to water deprivation or desmopressin assess concentrating ability.
- **Protein and albumin excretion:** detect glomerular barrier damage.

Clearance interpretation:

- clearance equal to GFR: filtered only;
- clearance less than GFR: net reabsorption;
- clearance greater than GFR: net secretion;
- clearance near renal plasma flow: strong extraction from plasma.

## 13. Skin: structure and functions

### Structure

- **Epidermis:** keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. It contains keratinocytes, melanocytes, immune cells, and sensory-associated cells.
- **Dermis:** connective tissue containing blood vessels, nerves, sensory receptors, hair follicles, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands.
- **Subcutaneous tissue:** fat and loose connective tissue that insulate, cushion, and anchor skin.

### Functions

- Physical barrier against trauma, chemicals, pathogens, and water loss
- Temperature regulation through blood flow, sweating, and insulation
- Sensation: touch, pressure, pain, temperature, and itch
- Vitamin D synthesis after ultraviolet exposure
- Immune defence through skin cells and resident immune cells
- Excretion of small amounts of water, salts, and urea in sweat
- Storage of fat and blood in cutaneous vessels

## 14. Measurement of body temperature

Body temperature reflects the balance between heat production and heat loss. Core temperature is approximately 37°C, with normal circadian variation of about 0.5–1°C.

### Measurement sites

- **Rectal:** close to core temperature and often slightly higher than oral.
- **Oral:** convenient but affected by food, breathing, and technique.
- **Axillary:** convenient but less accurate and often lower.
- **Tympanic:** estimates temperature near the hypothalamus through infrared measurement; technique matters.
- **Temporal artery:** non-invasive infrared estimate of core temperature.

Factors affecting readings include time of day, exercise, meals, environmental temperature, age, menstrual cycle, illness, medications, and measurement method. A single reading should be interpreted with clinical context.

## 15. Heat production and heat loss

### Heat production

- Basal metabolism of liver, brain, heart, and other organs
- Skeletal-muscle activity and exercise
- Shivering thermogenesis
- Non-shivering thermogenesis, especially brown adipose tissue in infants
- Sympathetic catecholamines and thyroid hormones
- Food-induced thermogenesis

### Heat loss

- **Radiation:** transfer of infrared heat to cooler surroundings without direct contact.
- **Conduction:** direct transfer to objects or fluids touching the body.
- **Convection:** heat carried away by moving air or water.
- **Evaporation:** heat used to convert sweat or respiratory water into vapour; the only effective major route when environmental temperature exceeds skin temperature.

The skin controls heat loss through vasoconstriction, vasodilation, and sweating. The respiratory tract also loses heat and water, while the kidneys conserve or excrete water and electrolytes according to volume status.

## 16. Regulation of body temperature

Thermoregulation is a negative-feedback system involving thermoreceptors, hypothalamic integration, autonomic and endocrine responses, and behavioural responses.

### Physical mechanisms

When body temperature rises:

- skin arterioles dilate, increasing heat transfer to the environment;
- eccrine sweat glands secrete sweat for evaporative cooling;
- respiration may increase modestly;
- behaviour seeks shade, cool air, reduced clothing, or water.

When temperature falls:

- cutaneous vasoconstriction reduces heat loss;
- piloerection traps a small insulating air layer in humans;
- shivering increases skeletal-muscle heat production;
- non-shivering thermogenesis and metabolic activity increase;
- behaviour seeks warmth, shelter, food, or additional clothing.

### Chemical mechanisms

Chemical thermogenesis changes metabolic heat production. Sympathetic catecholamines increase metabolism; thyroid hormone supports longer-term metabolic heat production. Shivering produces heat through rapid involuntary muscle contraction. Brown fat uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, releasing energy as heat rather than ATP.

### Nervous mechanisms and hypothalamus

Peripheral cold and warm receptors send information to the spinal cord and brain. The anterior/preoptic hypothalamus contains temperature-sensitive neurons and coordinates heat-loss responses. The posterior hypothalamus integrates signals and activates heat-conserving and heat-producing responses when core temperature falls.

The hypothalamus compares body temperature with a regulated set point. It coordinates autonomic activity, endocrine responses, somatic motor output, and behaviour. The cortex contributes conscious temperature sensation and voluntary actions, but hypothalamic responses continue without conscious control.

## 17. Hypothermia

Hypothermia is a core temperature below approximately 35°C. It occurs when heat loss exceeds heat production, especially with cold exposure, wetness, wind, immersion, impaired consciousness, endocrine disease, or drugs.

### Effects

- Early: peripheral vasoconstriction, shivering, tachycardia, and increased catecholamines.
- Progressive: confusion, poor coordination, bradycardia, slowed conduction, reduced ventilation, and impaired reflexes.
- Severe: arrhythmias, coma, respiratory failure, and cardiac arrest.

Rewarming may be passive for mild cases or active external/internal for severe cases. Handling a severely hypothermic patient gently is important because the cold myocardium is irritable.

## 18. Fever

Fever is a regulated rise in body temperature because the hypothalamic set point is increased. It differs from hyperthermia, in which temperature rises despite an unchanged set point because heat production or gain exceeds heat-loss capacity.

Microbial products stimulate immune cells to release cytokines such as interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumour necrosis factor. These increase hypothalamic prostaglandin E2, which raises the set point.

### Stages of fever

1. **Onset:** the set point rises; the body perceives its current temperature as too cold. Vasoconstriction and shivering occur.
2. **Plateau:** body temperature reaches the new set point; the person may feel warm.
3. **Defervescence:** the set point returns to normal; vasodilation and sweating dissipate heat.

Moderate fever may inhibit some pathogens and enhance immune reactions, but high or prolonged fever increases metabolic demand, oxygen consumption, fluid loss, and cardiovascular stress. Antipyretics reduce prostaglandin production and lower the set point; they do not directly correct uncontrolled hyperthermia.

## 19. Renal and temperature regulation: physiological correlations

1. **The kidney controls the water needed for sweating.** Heat causes sweating and water loss; ADH increases collecting-duct water reabsorption, and thirst increases intake. Without these responses, dehydration reduces plasma volume and impairs skin heat loss.
2. **Temperature affects renal and cardiovascular perfusion.** Heat-induced skin vasodilation can reduce central venous return and blood pressure. The sympathetic system and renal salt-water retention help defend circulation.
3. **The hypothalamus coordinates multiple systems.** It changes sweating, skin blood flow, shivering, thirst, ADH release, and autonomic activity as one integrated response.
4. **GFR depends on perfusion.** Dehydration and heat-related volume loss reduce renal perfusion and may reduce GFR. Severe hypovolaemia activates sympathetic tone, RAAS, and ADH, prioritizing blood pressure over urine production.
5. **Urine concentration depends on the medulla and ADH.** The countercurrent gradient and collecting-duct water permeability allow water conservation during fever, exercise, or heat exposure.
6. **Fever increases renal workload indirectly.** Sweating, tachycardia, increased metabolism, and reduced intake can cause fluid deficit; kidney conservation limits loss but cannot replace adequate intake.
7. **Acid-base regulation is shared.** The lungs change CO2 rapidly, while kidneys excrete hydrogen and regenerate bicarbonate more slowly. Fever, exercise, shock, and renal failure can alter this balance.
8. **Endocrine links connect the organs.** Renin affects pressure and volume; ADH affects water; aldosterone affects sodium and potassium; calcitriol affects calcium; EPO affects oxygen delivery. Temperature stress changes these systems through volume and metabolic demands.
9. **Skin and kidney both regulate internal stability.** Skin adjusts heat and small solute loss externally; kidneys adjust fluid, electrolytes, osmolality, pH, and waste internally.
10. **Disease localizes to a step in the chain.** Glomerular injury reduces filtration, tubular injury alters reabsorption/secretion, ADH failure produces dilute urine, dehydration reduces perfusion, hypothalamic injury disrupts temperature control, and excessive fever increases metabolic and fluid demands.

## High-yield summary

The nephron filters plasma and then selectively reabsorbs and secretes substances to form urine. The JGA stabilizes GFR and controls renin release. The loop of Henle, vasa recta, urea, and ADH create concentrated urine, while micturition removes it. The skin provides a physical barrier, sensation, and heat exchange. The hypothalamus integrates temperature signals and coordinates vascular, sweating, metabolic, muscular, behavioural, renal, and hormonal responses. Together, the kidney and skin maintain volume, osmolality, acid-base balance, waste removal, blood pressure, and body temperature.
