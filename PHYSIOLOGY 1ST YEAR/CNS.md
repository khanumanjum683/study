# Central Nervous System Physiology

The central nervous system (CNS) consists of the brain and spinal cord. It receives sensory information, interprets it, stores experience, generates movement, regulates autonomic and endocrine functions, and supports consciousness, language, emotion, and behaviour.

The basic sequence is:

`stimulus -> receptor -> afferent pathway -> synaptic integration -> perception or reflex -> descending command -> effector response`

## 1. Introduction to the CNS

The brain is protected by the skull, meninges, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and the blood-brain barrier. The spinal cord is protected by the vertebral column and contains ascending sensory tracts, descending motor tracts, and spinal reflex circuits.

### Major divisions

- **Cerebrum:** conscious sensation, voluntary movement, language, memory, reasoning, and behaviour.
- **Diencephalon:** thalamus and hypothalamus; sensory relay, homeostasis, endocrine and autonomic regulation.
- **Brainstem:** midbrain, pons, and medulla; conduction, cranial nerve nuclei, arousal, cardiovascular and respiratory control.
- **Cerebellum:** balance, posture, coordination, timing, and motor learning.
- **Spinal cord:** communication between body and brain and rapid reflex activity.

Gray matter contains neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses, and glia. White matter contains myelinated axons organized into tracts. A CNS nucleus is a collection of neuronal cell bodies; a peripheral ganglion is the corresponding collection outside the CNS.

## 2. Sensory receptors

A receptor converts a physical or chemical stimulus into a receptor potential. If threshold is reached, action potentials travel in an afferent neuron toward the CNS.

### Classification by adequate stimulus

- **Mechanoreceptors:** touch, pressure, vibration, stretch, hearing, and balance.
- **Thermoreceptors:** warmth and cold.
- **Nociceptors:** potentially damaging mechanical, thermal, and chemical stimuli.
- **Photoreceptors:** light in the retina.
- **Chemoreceptors:** taste, smell, blood gases, pH, and osmolality.
- **Osmoreceptors:** effective plasma osmolality, especially in the hypothalamus.

### Classification by location

- **Exteroceptors:** detect the external environment.
- **Interoceptors or visceroceptors:** detect internal organ stretch, chemistry, and pressure.
- **Proprioceptors:** detect muscle length, tendon tension, and joint position.

### Classification by structure

- **Free nerve endings:** pain, temperature, itch, and crude touch.
- **Encapsulated endings:** pressure, vibration, stretch, and specialized touch.
- **Separate receptor cells:** hair cells, retinal rods/cones, taste cells, and chemoreceptor cells synapse with afferent neurons.

### Properties of receptors

1. **Specificity:** each receptor responds best to its adequate stimulus.
2. **Transduction:** stimulus energy becomes an electrical receptor potential.
3. **Receptive field:** body region that changes one sensory neuron's activity.
4. **Adaptation:** response declines during a sustained stimulus. Rapidly adapting receptors detect change and vibration; slowly adapting receptors signal continuing pressure, stretch, or tissue damage.
5. **Intensity coding:** stronger stimuli increase receptor-potential size, firing frequency, and recruitment of receptors.
6. **Labeled-line coding:** the CNS identifies sensation partly from the pathway activated.
7. **Lateral inhibition:** strongly activated pathways inhibit neighbours and sharpen localization.

## 3. Synapses

A synapse is a junction where a neuron communicates with another neuron or effector cell.

### Classification

- **Chemical synapse:** neurotransmitter crosses a synaptic cleft; it permits excitation, inhibition, and modulation.
- **Electrical synapse:** gap junctions directly pass current; it is rapid and can synchronize cells.
- **Axodendritic, axosomatic, and axoaxonic:** classified by the postsynaptic site.
- **Excitatory or inhibitory:** classified by the postsynaptic effect.

### Chemical transmission

1. An action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal.
2. Voltage-gated calcium channels open.
3. Calcium triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release.
4. Neurotransmitter binds postsynaptic receptors.
5. Ion channels or second messengers produce an EPSP or IPSP.
6. Neurotransmitter is removed by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, diffusion, or glial uptake.

### Synaptic properties

- One-way conduction because release is presynaptic and receptors are postsynaptic
- Synaptic delay
- Spatial and temporal summation
- Convergence of many neurons onto one neuron
- Divergence from one neuron to many neurons
- Facilitation, inhibition, and synaptic fatigue
- Plasticity, including long-term potentiation and depression, underlying learning and memory

EPSPs commonly involve sodium or calcium entry and depolarization. IPSPs commonly involve chloride entry or potassium exit and hyperpolarization. The axon initial segment integrates all inputs and generates an action potential if threshold is reached.

## 4. Sensory pathways

Most conscious somatic sensory pathways use a three-neuron chain: first-order neuron from receptor to spinal cord or brainstem, second-order neuron that usually crosses and ascends to the thalamus, and third-order neuron from thalamus to cortex.

### Anterior spinothalamic tract

This tract carries crude touch and pressure.

1. First-order fibres enter the dorsal horn.
2. Second-order neurons cross through the anterior white commissure, usually within a few segments.
3. Fibres ascend contralaterally in the anterior funiculus.
4. They relay mainly in the ventral posterior lateral (VPL) thalamus.
5. Third-order fibres pass through the internal capsule to the primary somatosensory cortex.

Localization is less precise than fine touch because receptive fields are larger and pathways have more bilateral overlap.

### Posterior column-medial lemniscus pathway

This pathway carries fine/discriminative touch, vibration, conscious proprioception, pressure, and two-point discrimination.

1. First-order neurons enter the dorsal columns and ascend ipsilaterally.
2. Lower-body fibres use fasciculus gracilis; upper-body fibres use fasciculus cuneatus.
3. They synapse in gracile and cuneate nuclei in the medulla.
4. Second-order fibres cross as internal arcuate fibres and ascend as the medial lemniscus.
5. They relay in the VPL thalamus.
6. Third-order fibres reach the primary somatosensory cortex.

### Lateral spinothalamic tract

This tract carries pain and temperature.

1. Nociceptor and thermoreceptor fibres enter through dorsal roots.
2. They synapse in the dorsal horn, including substantia gelatinosa.
3. Second-order neurons cross in the anterior white commissure within one or two segments.
4. They ascend contralaterally in the anterolateral system.
5. Fibres relay in thalamic nuclei and also in reticular and midbrain regions.
6. Projections reach somatosensory, insular, and cingulate areas for localization and affective pain.

## 5. Pain and referred pain

### Types of pain

- **Fast pain:** sharp, pricking, and well localized; mainly A-delta fibres.
- **Slow pain:** dull, aching, burning, or throbbing; mainly C fibres and associated with autonomic/emotional responses.
- **Somatic pain:** from skin, muscle, joints, and body wall; usually well localized.
- **Visceral pain:** from organs; poorly localized, deep, and often associated with nausea.
- **Referred pain:** visceral pain perceived in a somatic area supplied by the same spinal segment.

Visceral and somatic afferents converge on common dorsal-horn neurons. The brain is more familiar with somatic input, so it may mislocalize visceral activity. Cardiac ischemia may be felt in the chest, left arm, neck, or jaw; diaphragmatic irritation may refer to the shoulder through phrenic segments.

The periaqueductal gray, rostroventral medulla, and dorsal horn form descending analgesic systems. Endogenous opioids, serotonin, and noradrenaline inhibit nociceptive transmission. Non-painful touch can also reduce pain transmission through spinal interneurons.

## 6. Thalamus: nuclei and functions

The thalamus is the major relay and processing centre between subcortical structures and the cortex. Nearly all sensory information except olfaction relays through it.

- **VPL:** somatic sensation from the body to somatosensory cortex.
- **VPM:** facial sensation and taste.
- **Lateral geniculate body:** visual relay to occipital cortex.
- **Medial geniculate body:** auditory relay to temporal cortex.
- **Anterior nuclei:** limbic memory and emotion; linked with hippocampus and cingulate cortex.
- **Mediodorsal nucleus:** emotion, motivation, memory, and prefrontal association.
- **Ventral anterior and ventral lateral nuclei:** basal-ganglia and cerebellar motor relays to motor cortex.
- **Intralaminar nuclei:** arousal, attention, and pain modulation.
- **Reticular nucleus:** regulates activity of other thalamic nuclei and attention.

The thalamus filters and organizes information rather than passing it unchanged. Lesions may cause sensory loss, thalamic pain, altered consciousness, or movement abnormalities.

## 7. Reflexes

A reflex is a rapid, automatic, relatively stereotyped response to a stimulus. Its arc contains a receptor, afferent neuron, CNS integration, efferent neuron, and effector.

### Classification

- Innate or acquired/conditioned
- Spinal or cranial
- Somatic or autonomic/visceral
- Monosynaptic or polysynaptic
- Superficial, deep tendon, protective, postural, or visceral

### Monosynaptic stretch reflex

The stretch reflex maintains muscle length and posture. Stretch activates primary endings of muscle spindles. Ia afferents enter the spinal cord and make a direct excitatory synapse on alpha motor neurons supplying the same muscle. The muscle contracts and opposes the stretch. The knee jerk is a classic example.

### Muscle spindle

Muscle spindles are encapsulated receptors containing intrafusal fibres arranged parallel to extrafusal muscle. Nuclear bag and nuclear chain fibres detect muscle length and rate of change. Ia endings respond strongly to dynamic stretch; II endings signal static length. Gamma motor neurons contract intrafusal ends and keep the spindle sensitive during muscle shortening. Alpha-gamma coactivation maintains posture and movement accuracy.

### Inverse stretch reflex

The inverse stretch reflex is mediated by Golgi tendon organs arranged in series with muscle fibres. Excessive tension activates Ib afferents, which excite inhibitory interneurons that reduce alpha motor activity to the same muscle and may facilitate antagonists. It protects against excessive tension and also informs the CNS about force.

### Polysynaptic withdrawal reflex

Nociceptor activation produces flexion away from a painful stimulus. Afferents enter the dorsal horn and activate interneurons that excite flexor motor neurons and inhibit extensors. The crossed-extensor reflex activates contralateral extensors to maintain balance. It has longer latency and produces a graded, widespread response.

## 8. Motor pathways

### Pyramidal/corticospinal pathway

The corticospinal pathway controls skilled voluntary movement, especially precise distal-limb movement.

1. Upper motor neurons arise mainly from primary motor, premotor, and supplementary motor cortex.
2. Axons pass through the corona radiata and posterior limb of the internal capsule.
3. They descend through cerebral peduncles, pons, and medullary pyramids.
4. About 85–90% cross at the pyramidal decussation and form the lateral corticospinal tract.
5. Remaining fibres descend anteriorly and usually cross near their spinal termination.
6. Lower motor neurons in the anterior horn or cranial motor nuclei activate skeletal muscle.

The corticobulbar tract controls cranial motor nuclei for face, jaw, tongue, pharynx, and larynx. Basal ganglia, cerebellum, brainstem pathways, and spinal circuits refine cortical commands.

### Upper motor neuron lesion

An UMN lesion affects cortex or descending pathways above the anterior horn cell or cranial motor nucleus. It causes weakness, spasticity, increased tone, hyperreflexia, clonus, extensor plantar response (Babinski sign), and relatively little early wasting. Fine voluntary control is impaired.

### Lower motor neuron lesion

An LMN lesion affects anterior horn cells, cranial motor neurons, peripheral nerves, or axons. It causes flaccid weakness, reduced tone, hyporeflexia/areflexia, fasciculations, marked denervation atrophy, and possible fibrillation potentials.

## 9. Cerebral cortex

### Sensory cortex

The primary somatosensory cortex lies in the postcentral gyrus. It receives VPL/VPM input and has a contralateral somatotopic map. Face, hand, and tongue occupy large areas because they require high discrimination. Association cortex interprets size, shape, texture, and body position.

Visual cortex is in the occipital lobe; auditory cortex in the superior temporal lobe; gustatory cortex in the insula and frontal operculum; olfactory cortex in medial temporal regions. Lesions may cause cortical sensory loss, impaired two-point discrimination, astereognosis, or neglect.

### Motor cortex

The primary motor cortex lies in the precentral gyrus and controls contralateral voluntary movement. Premotor and supplementary motor areas plan posture, learned sequences, and complex actions. Frontal eye fields control voluntary eye movement. Dominant frontal and temporal-parietal regions support language production and comprehension.

The cortex uses continuous sensory feedback to update motor commands. Motor maps are somatotopic but distorted according to functional importance.

## 10. Medulla and pons

### Medulla

- Contains pyramidal decussation and major ascending/descending pathways.
- Contains nuclei for cranial nerves IX, X, XI, and XII.
- Regulates cardiovascular activity, vasomotor tone, and basic respiratory rhythm.
- Coordinates swallowing, vomiting, coughing, sneezing, and gagging.
- Contains gracile/cuneate nuclei and the inferior olivary nucleus.

### Pons

- Bridges cortex, cerebellum, and spinal cord through pontine nuclei.
- Contains nuclei of cranial nerves V, VI, VII, and VIII.
- Modifies respiratory rhythm and the transition between inspiration and expiration.
- Participates in sleep, arousal, facial sensation and movement, hearing, balance, and gaze.

## 11. Cerebellum

The cerebellum compares intended movement with sensory feedback and corrects errors.

### Functions

- Coordinates timing and force of movement
- Maintains posture and balance
- Regulates muscle tone
- Coordinates eye movements and speech
- Supports motor learning and adaptation

The vestibulocerebellum controls balance and eye movements. The spinocerebellum regulates posture and ongoing limb movement. The cerebrocerebellum plans skilled movement and supports motor learning.

### Disorders

Cerebellar lesions cause ipsilateral ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, decomposition of movement, hypotonia, nystagmus, and broad-based gait. Strength may be normal but coordination is poor; speech may become scanning or irregular.

## 12. Basal ganglia

The basal ganglia include the caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, and substantia nigra. They form loops with cortex and thalamus.

### Functions

- Initiate desired movement and suppress competing movement
- Regulate muscle tone and posture
- Scale movement amplitude and speed
- Support procedural learning and habits
- Participate in motivation and cognitive loops

The direct pathway facilitates selected movement, while the indirect pathway suppresses unwanted movement. Dopamine from substantia nigra pars compacta promotes movement through D1 effects and reduces inhibitory braking through D2-related effects.

### Disorders

- **Parkinson disease:** loss of dopaminergic neurons; bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, postural instability, and reduced facial expression.
- **Huntington disease:** striatal degeneration; chorea, behavioural change, and cognitive decline.
- **Hemiballismus:** subthalamic nucleus lesion; violent flinging movements on the opposite side.
- **Dystonia and dyskinesias:** abnormal sustained or involuntary movements from circuit dysfunction.

## 13. Hypothalamus and limbic system

### Hypothalamus

The hypothalamus links nervous and endocrine systems and maintains homeostasis. It regulates temperature, thirst, osmolality, hunger, satiety, autonomic activity, pituitary hormones, reproductive behaviour, circadian rhythms, stress, and sleep. The supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei synthesize ADH and oxytocin. The anterior hypothalamus favours heat loss, while posterior regions support heat conservation.

### Limbic system

The limbic system includes the hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate cortex, parahippocampal regions, septal areas, and hypothalamic connections.

- **Hippocampus:** formation and consolidation of new declarative memories and spatial navigation.
- **Amygdala:** emotional significance, fear conditioning, aggression, and autonomic responses to threat.
- **Cingulate cortex:** emotion, attention, motivation, and pain affect.
- **Hypothalamic connections:** translate emotion into endocrine and autonomic responses.

The limbic system explains why fear, reward, pain, and memory alter heart rate, sweating, hormone secretion, and behaviour.

## 14. CSF and lumbar puncture

CSF is produced mainly by choroid plexuses. It flows:

`lateral ventricles -> interventricular foramina -> third ventricle -> cerebral aqueduct -> fourth ventricle -> apertures -> subarachnoid space -> arachnoid granulations -> dural venous sinuses`

CSF provides buoyancy and mechanical protection, maintains a stable chemical environment, removes metabolic waste, distributes signalling molecules, and contributes to intracranial pressure regulation. It is normally clear, low in protein, and contains very few cells.

Lumbar puncture samples CSF from the lumbar subarachnoid space, usually below the end of the spinal cord. It helps assess infection, subarachnoid haemorrhage, inflammatory disease, malignancy, and pressure. It must be avoided or carefully considered when a mass lesion and raised intracranial pressure could cause herniation.

## 15. Sleep and EEG

An EEG records summed electrical activity of cortical neurons through scalp electrodes.

- **Alpha:** about 8–13 Hz; relaxed awake state with eyes closed.
- **Beta:** above about 13 Hz; alertness and active thinking.
- **Theta:** about 4–7 Hz; drowsiness and early sleep, also normal in children.
- **Delta:** below about 4 Hz; deep sleep; excessive waking delta suggests dysfunction.

### Sleep stages

- **NREM N1:** transition from wakefulness; theta activity.
- **NREM N2:** sleep spindles and K-complexes.
- **NREM N3:** slow-wave delta sleep with high arousal threshold.
- **REM:** rapid eye movements, vivid dreaming, cortical activation, and skeletal-muscle atonia except respiratory and ocular muscles.

Sleep cycles repeat about every 90 minutes. The suprachiasmatic nucleus uses light input to coordinate circadian timing, while melatonin signals darkness. Sleep supports memory consolidation, endocrine rhythms, immune function, and metabolic recovery. EEG is useful in epilepsy, sleep studies, encephalopathy, and brain-death assessment alongside clinical findings.

## 16. Autonomic nervous system

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) controls smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands, and visceral organs. It includes sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric divisions.

### General organization

Autonomic pathways usually contain a preganglionic neuron from the CNS to an autonomic ganglion and a postganglionic neuron to the effector. All preganglionic fibres release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors. Most parasympathetic postganglionic fibres release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors; most sympathetic postganglionic fibres release noradrenaline onto alpha/beta receptors.

### Sympathetic division

The sympathetic thoracolumbar outflow arises from T1-L2. Ganglia lie near the vertebral column or in prevertebral plexuses. Preganglionic fibres are usually short and postganglionic fibres long.

Fight-or-flight effects include:

- increased heart rate and contractility;
- vasoconstriction in skin, kidney, and GI tract through alpha-1 receptors;
- beta-2 vasodilation in skeletal muscle;
- bronchodilation;
- pupil dilation;
- sweating through sympathetic cholinergic fibres;
- piloerection;
- reduced GI motility and secretion;
- bladder relaxation and sphincter contraction;
- ejaculation.

The adrenal medulla is a sympathetic neuroendocrine organ releasing adrenaline and noradrenaline into blood.

### Parasympathetic division

The craniosacral outflow arises from brainstem nuclei of cranial nerves III, VII, IX, and X and sacral segments S2-S4. Ganglia lie in or near target organs, so preganglionic fibres are long and postganglionic fibres short.

Rest-and-digest effects include:

- slowing the heart and AV conduction;
- pupil constriction and accommodation;
- increased salivation and lacrimation;
- increased GI motility and secretion with sphincter relaxation;
- bladder detrusor contraction and internal-sphincter relaxation;
- erection through nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation;
- facilitation of defecation.

Most blood vessels have little direct parasympathetic innervation; vascular tone is mainly controlled by sympathetic and local metabolic mechanisms.

## 17. How the CNS topics are correlated

1. **Sensation becomes perception through pathways:** receptors encode stimuli, spinal/brainstem tracts carry them, the thalamus filters and relays them, and the cortex gives conscious meaning.
2. **Touch and pain use different routes:** fine touch remains ipsilateral in the dorsal columns until the medulla, while pain and temperature cross early in the spinal cord.
3. **Reflexes provide rapid protection:** spinal circuits can respond before cortical awareness; the cortex later modifies avoidance, posture, and voluntary behaviour.
4. **Motor control is hierarchical:** cortex plans skilled action, basal ganglia select and scale it, cerebellum compares intention with feedback, brainstem maintains posture, and spinal LMNs activate muscle.
5. **UMN and LMN signs localize lesions:** descending-pathway damage produces spastic signs, while anterior-horn or peripheral motor damage produces flaccid denervation signs.
6. **Pain is sensory and emotional:** spinothalamic pathways identify the stimulus, while thalamic, insular, cingulate, amygdala, and hypothalamic networks generate affective and autonomic responses.
7. **The hypothalamus integrates body systems:** temperature, thirst, feeding, endocrine axes, circadian rhythms, autonomic activity, and reproduction are coordinated together.
8. **The ANS converts brain state into organ responses:** fear activates amygdala-hypothalamic sympathetic output; rest and digestion favour parasympathetic activity; visceral reflexes adjust pressure, bladder, gut, and airway function.
9. **CSF protects the circuits that regulate behaviour:** pressure, composition, and clearance influence neuronal excitability, consciousness, and motor function.
10. **Sleep reorganizes CNS function:** EEG rhythms reflect changing thalamocortical activity; NREM supports restoration and memory processing, while REM supports emotional and procedural processing.
11. **Cerebellum and basal ganglia refine movement:** lesions may leave strength intact but disrupt coordination, initiation, scaling, or suppression of movement.
12. **The complete loop is continuous:** internal and external stimuli alter receptor activity, the CNS selects a response, descending motor/autonomic pathways act, and sensory feedback corrects the result.

## High-yield summary

The CNS is an integrated information-processing system. Receptors detect change, synapses combine and transform signals, sensory tracts carry information to thalamus and cortex, reflex circuits provide rapid responses, and motor pathways generate voluntary and automatic action. Cerebellum, basal ganglia, hypothalamus, limbic system, brainstem, CSF, sleep networks, and autonomic divisions cooperate to maintain movement, consciousness, homeostasis, and behaviour.
