# General Physiology

## 1. Introduction to Cell Physiology

Cell physiology is the branch of physiology that deals with how cells function, maintain themselves, and interact with their environment. The human body is made up of trillions of cells, and each cell performs life-sustaining activities.

A cell must:
- Take in nutrients and oxygen
- Produce energy
- Remove waste products
- Maintain its internal environment
- Communicate with other cells
- Repair itself and reproduce when needed

### Major components of the cell
- Cell membrane: separates intracellular and extracellular environments
- Cytoplasm: contains organelles and site of chemical reactions
- Nucleus: controls cell activity and contains DNA
- Mitochondria: produce ATP (energy)
- Ribosomes: synthesize proteins
- Endoplasmic reticulum: protein and lipid synthesis
- Golgi apparatus: modifies and packages substances
- Lysosomes: digest cellular waste

The cell is not isolated; it constantly exchanges substances with surrounding fluid and responds to signals. This exchange is fundamental to cell survival and to the overall function of the body.

## 2. Transport Across the Cell Membrane

The cell membrane is a selectively permeable barrier. It allows certain substances to pass while restricting others. This selectively allows the cell to maintain internal composition.

### Structure of the cell membrane
- Phospholipid bilayer
- Proteins embedded in membrane
- Carbohydrates and cholesterol

### Functions
- Protects the cell
- Regulates exchange between intracellular and extracellular fluid
- Maintains ionic gradients
- Helps in cell signaling
- Maintains membrane potential

### Types of transport

## A. Passive transport
Passive transport does not require ATP (energy). Movement occurs from higher to lower concentration or along an electrochemical gradient.

### 1. Simple diffusion
Movement of molecules from an area of high concentration to low concentration due to random molecular motion.

Examples:
- Oxygen diffuses from blood to tissue cells
- Carbon dioxide diffuses from cells to blood

Factors affecting diffusion:
- Concentration gradient
- Membrane permeability
- Surface area
- Temperature
- Molecular size
- Distance

### 2. Facilitated diffusion
Movement of substances through membrane proteins without energy expenditure.

Types:
- Channel proteins
- Carrier proteins

Examples:
- Glucose transport into cells
- Ion movement through ion channels

This process is specific and can be saturated.

### 3. Osmosis
Movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane from low solute concentration to high solute concentration.

Water moves according to osmotic gradients. This is essential for:
- Cell volume regulation
- Maintenance of blood volume
- Distribution of extracellular and intracellular fluid

### 4. Filtration
Movement of water and solutes due to hydrostatic pressure.

Example:
- Filtration in kidney glomeruli

---

## B. Active transport
Active transport requires ATP and moves substances against a concentration gradient (from low to high concentration).

### 1. Primary active transport
Direct use of ATP.

Example:
- Na⁺/K⁺ pump

#### Sodium-potassium pump
- Pumps 3 Na⁺ out of the cell
- Pumps 2 K⁺ into the cell
- Uses ATP

Importance:
- Maintains intracellular potassium and extracellular sodium
- Controls cell volume
- Maintains resting membrane potential
- Provides energy for secondary active transport

### 2. Secondary active transport
Uses energy stored in an ion gradient, usually Na⁺ gradient created by Na⁺/K⁺ pump.

Types:
- Symport: both substances move in the same direction
- Antiport: substances move in opposite directions

Examples:
- Na⁺-glucose transport
- Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchange

---

## C. Vesicular transport
Movement of large particles or large amounts of materials using vesicles.

### Endocytosis
Movement of substances into the cell.

Types:
- Phagocytosis: engulfment of large particles
- Pinocytosis: intake of fluid
- Receptor-mediated endocytosis: selective uptake

### Exocytosis
Movement of substances out of the cell.

Examples:
- Hormone secretion
- Neurotransmitter release
- Enzyme secretion

---

## 3. Tonicity and Cell Volume

Tonicity is the ability of a solution to change cell volume.

### Isotonic solution
Same effective osmotic pressure as the cell.

- Cell volume unchanged
- Example: normal saline

### Hypotonic solution
Lower solute concentration than inside the cell.

- Water enters cell
- Cell swells
- May rupture

### Hypertonic solution
Higher solute concentration than inside the cell.

- Water leaves cell
- Cell shrinks

This is important in IV fluid therapy and in understanding fluid imbalance.

## 4. Homeostasis

Homeostasis means maintaining a stable internal environment despite changes in the external environment.

The body keeps internal conditions within a narrow range for:
- Body temperature
- Blood pressure
- Blood glucose
- pH
- Water and electrolyte levels
- Oxygen and carbon dioxide concentration

### Components of homeostatic control
1. Stimulus: change in internal or external environment
2. Receptor: detects the change
3. Control center: interprets and compares with normal range
4. Effector: produces corrective response
5. Response: restores balance

### Negative feedback
Most common mechanism in the body.

It opposes the original change.

Examples:
- Regulation of body temperature
- Regulation of blood glucose
- Regulation of blood pressure

Example: temperature regulation
- If body temperature rises, sweat glands activate and blood vessels dilate
- Heat loss increases
- Temperature returns to normal

### Positive feedback
Enhances or amplifies the original change.

Examples:
- Blood clotting
- Childbirth contractions
- Action potential generation

This is less common because it moves away from normal until a specific event stops it.

## 5. Body Fluid Compartments

The total body water is distributed into compartments.

### Total body water
Approximately 60% of body weight in an adult.

For a 70 kg adult:
- Total body water ≈ 42 L

### Main compartments
1. Intracellular fluid (ICF): fluid inside cells
2. Extracellular fluid (ECF): fluid outside cells

### Extracellular fluid includes:
- Plasma: fluid part of blood
- Interstitial fluid: fluid surrounding cells
- Transcellular fluid: fluid in specific cavities (CSF, synovial fluid, pleural fluid, etc.)

### Approximate distribution
- ICF ≈ 2/3 of total body water
- ECF ≈ 1/3 of total body water

For a 70 kg adult:
- ICF ≈ 28 L
- ECF ≈ 14 L

Of the ECF:
- Plasma ≈ 3 L
- Interstitial fluid ≈ 11 L

---

## 6. Composition of Body Fluids

### Intracellular fluid
High in:
- Potassium
- Magnesium
- Phosphate
- Proteins

### Extracellular fluid
High in:
- Sodium
- Chloride
- Bicarbonate

These differences are vital for cell function.

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains these ionic differences across the membrane.

---

## 7. Measurement of Body Fluid Compartments

Body fluid compartments are measured using indicator dilution methods.

### Principle
A known amount of indicator is injected into the body compartment, then concentration is measured after mixing.

Volume is calculated as:

Volume = Amount of indicator / Concentration

### Common indicators
- Total body water: deuterium oxide, tritiated water
- Extracellular fluid: inulin, mannitol
- Plasma volume: Evans blue, radioiodinated albumin

### Examples
If total body water = 42 L and ECF = 14 L:
- ICF = 42 - 14 = 28 L

If ECF = 14 L and plasma = 3 L:
- Interstitial fluid = 14 - 3 = 11 L

### Importance
Measurement helps in:
- Diagnosis of dehydration
- Edema assessment
- Electrolyte disorders
- Fluid therapy planning

## 8. Correlation Between These Topics

All these topics are deeply interrelated.

### Cell membrane + transport
The membrane controls movement of substances between cells and their surroundings. This determines:
- intracellular composition
- cell volume
- electrical activity
- nutrient supply
- waste elimination

### Body fluids + transport
Cells live in extracellular fluid. Transport processes regulate exchange between:
- plasma and interstitial fluid
- interstitial fluid and intracellular fluid

This maintains the fluid environment necessary for life.

### Homeostasis + body fluids
The body regulates water and electrolyte balance using:
- kidneys
- lungs
- endocrine system
- nervous system
- cardiovascular system

If body fluid volume or composition changes, homeostatic mechanisms restore normal ranges.

### Example: dehydration
- Water loss leads to increased ECF osmolarity
- Water shifts from ICF to ECF
- Cells shrink
- Thirst increases
- ADH is released
- Kidneys conserve water
- Body fluid balance is restored

This shows how transport, body fluids, and homeostasis work together.

### Example: sodium imbalance
- Too much sodium increases extracellular osmolarity
- Water moves out of cells
- Cells shrink
- This can affect brain function and circulation

### Example: blood pressure regulation
- Blood volume depends on body fluid volume
- Body fluid volume depends on water and sodium balance
- Transport across membranes determines sodium and water movement
- Homeostatic mechanisms regulate blood pressure

---

## 9. Final Summary

- Cell physiology studies how cells function and maintain life.
- The cell membrane is a selective barrier controlling movement of substances.
- Transport across membranes includes diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion, active transport, and vesicular transport.
- Homeostasis is the maintenance of stable internal conditions.
- Body fluids are distributed into intracellular and extracellular compartments.
- The composition of these compartments is maintained by membrane transport and regulatory systems.
- Measurement of fluid compartments helps in understanding physiology and clinical disorders.

The whole system is interconnected:

Cell membrane transport → body fluid distribution → cell function → homeostasis → normal body function

This is the core foundation of physiology.
