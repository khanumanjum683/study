# Nerve and Muscle Physiology

Nerve and muscle are **excitable tissues**. Both respond to stimulation by changing their membrane potential. A nerve usually conducts information, while a muscle converts excitation into force and movement. Their common language is the action potential, and their essential messenger is calcium.

## 1. Neuron structure

A neuron is a specialized cell that receives, integrates, and transmits information.

- **Cell body (soma):** contains the nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, Nissl substance, and metabolic machinery. It maintains the neuron.
- **Dendrites:** branched receptive processes that receive synaptic input. They usually conduct graded potentials toward the soma.
- **Axon hillock and initial segment:** the trigger zone. Excitatory and inhibitory inputs are integrated here, and an action potential begins when threshold is reached.
- **Axon:** conducts action potentials away from the soma. It may be very short or more than a metre long.
- **Myelin sheath:** a lipid-rich electrical insulator around many axons.
- **Nodes of Ranvier:** short unmyelinated gaps containing a high density of voltage-gated sodium channels.
- **Axon terminals:** terminal branches that release neurotransmitter at a synapse.

Materials synthesized in the soma are transported along the axon by **axoplasmic transport**. Kinesin generally carries material anterogradely toward the terminal, while dynein carries material retrogradely toward the soma. Retrograde transport also allows some toxins and viruses to reach the CNS.

## 2. Types of neurons

### Functional classification

1. **Sensory or afferent neurons:** conduct information from receptors to the CNS. Their cell bodies are often in dorsal-root or cranial sensory ganglia.
2. **Motor or efferent neurons:** conduct commands from the CNS to skeletal muscle, smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, or glands.
3. **Interneurons:** connect neurons within the CNS and perform analysis, integration, inhibition, and coordination.

### Structural classification

- **Multipolar:** one axon and many dendrites; typical of motor neurons and most CNS neurons.
- **Bipolar:** one dendrite and one axon; found in the retina, olfactory pathway, and vestibulocochlear pathways.
- **Pseudounipolar:** one process leaves the cell body and divides into peripheral and central branches; typical of general sensory neurons.
- **Unipolar:** a single process; common in invertebrates and uncommon in mature humans.

## 3. Neuroglia

Neuroglia support, nourish, insulate, defend, and regulate neurons. Unlike neurons, most glial cells can divide.

### Central nervous system glia

- **Astrocytes:** maintain the extracellular ionic environment, take up neurotransmitters, provide metabolic support, help form the blood-brain barrier, regulate synapses, and participate in scar formation.
- **Oligodendrocytes:** form myelin around several CNS axons. Damage causes conduction failure, as in demyelinating disease.
- **Microglia:** resident macrophage-like immune cells that remove debris and respond to infection or injury.
- **Ependymal cells:** line the ventricles and central canal; specialized ependymal cells contribute to cerebrospinal fluid production.

### Peripheral nervous system glia

- **Schwann cells:** each myelinating Schwann cell forms one segment of one peripheral axon. They also guide peripheral axonal regeneration.
- **Satellite cells:** surround neuron cell bodies in peripheral ganglia and control their local chemical environment.

## 4. Nerve-fibre classification

### Erlanger and Gasser classification

| Fibre | Features and functions |
|---|---|
| A-alpha | Largest and fastest; skeletal muscle motor fibres, proprioception, muscle spindle and Golgi tendon information |
| A-beta | Touch, pressure, vibration, and some joint sensation |
| A-gamma | Motor fibres to intrafusal muscle-spindle fibres |
| A-delta | Fast, sharp pain; cold; some crude touch |
| B | Small myelinated autonomic preganglionic fibres |
| C | Unmyelinated; slow pain, warmth, itch, and postganglionic sympathetic fibres |

### Sensory group classification

- **Group I (Ia and Ib):** largest fibres; muscle spindle primary endings and Golgi tendon organs.
- **Group II:** secondary muscle-spindle endings and touch/pressure receptors.
- **Group III:** thinly myelinated fibres carrying fast pain and cold.
- **Group IV:** unmyelinated fibres carrying slow pain, warmth, itch, and visceral sensations.

Conduction velocity increases with axon diameter and myelination. Myelin increases membrane resistance and decreases membrane capacitance, so current travels farther and the action potential is regenerated at each node. This is **saltatory conduction**.

## 5. Properties of nerve fibres

### Excitability

Excitability is the ability to respond to a stimulus by producing an electrical change. A stimulus must reach threshold to produce an action potential. The minimum strength of a long-duration stimulus that excites a fibre is the **rheobase**. The minimum time required for a stimulus of twice rheobase strength is the **chronaxie**.

### Conductivity

Conductivity is the ability to transmit an action potential along the membrane. In a healthy axon, the impulse is regenerated rather than progressively weakened.

### All-or-none response

An individual axon produces a full action potential or none. A stronger stimulus does not make one action potential larger; it recruits more axons and may increase firing frequency.

### Refractory periods

- **Absolute refractory period:** a second action potential cannot be generated, regardless of stimulus strength, because voltage-gated sodium channels are inactivated.
- **Relative refractory period:** a stronger-than-normal stimulus can generate an action potential while potassium conductance remains elevated.

Refractoriness ensures one-way propagation and limits the maximum firing frequency.

### Adaptation and accommodation

Some sensory receptors reduce their response during a sustained stimulus; this is adaptation. A slowly rising stimulus may fail to excite a fibre because sodium channels inactivate while the membrane is depolarizing; this is accommodation.

### Degeneration and regeneration

An axon cut or severely injured outside the cell body undergoes Wallerian degeneration distally. Regeneration is better in the PNS than CNS because Schwann cells and endoneurial tubes can guide new growth.

## 6. Resting membrane potential

The **resting membrane potential (RMP)** is the voltage difference across an excitable cell membrane when it is not producing an action potential. A typical neuron is about **-70 mV**, and a skeletal muscle fibre is about **-90 mV**.

The inside is negative because:

1. Potassium concentration is high inside and sodium concentration is high outside.
2. The resting membrane is much more permeable to potassium than sodium because of potassium leak channels.
3. Potassium diffuses outward, leaving behind negatively charged proteins and other anions.
4. The sodium-potassium pump transports 3 Na+ out for every 2 K+ in. It maintains the gradients and makes a small direct contribution to negativity.

The equilibrium potential for an ion is the voltage at which its electrical and concentration forces balance. The resting voltage is closest to the potassium equilibrium potential but is influenced by sodium, chloride, and other ions. The Nernst equation expresses the equilibrium potential for one ion; the Goldman equation considers several permeant ions.

### Factors affecting RMP

Changes in extracellular potassium have a strong effect. Hyperkalaemia initially depolarizes the membrane but may inactivate sodium channels and produce weakness or conduction failure. Hypokalaemia hyperpolarizes the membrane and reduces excitability. Changes in calcium alter the threshold: low extracellular calcium increases neuromuscular excitability and can cause tetany, while high calcium generally reduces excitability.

## 7. Action potential

An action potential is a rapid, transient, all-or-none reversal of membrane potential.

1. **Resting state:** voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels are closed; sodium channels are available.
2. **Threshold:** a depolarizing stimulus opens enough sodium channels to begin positive feedback.
3. **Depolarization:** sodium channels open rapidly and Na+ enters. The membrane potential becomes less negative and briefly positive.
4. **Overshoot:** the inside becomes positive relative to the outside.
5. **Repolarization:** sodium channels inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open; K+ leaves.
6. **After-hyperpolarization:** potassium conductance remains high briefly, making the membrane more negative than its resting value.
7. **Recovery:** sodium channels return from inactivated to closed-but-available state and ion gradients are maintained.

The action potential does not decrease as it travels. Its amplitude is independent of stimulus strength, but information intensity is coded by action-potential frequency and by the number of active fibres. Myelinated fibres conduct by saltation; unmyelinated fibres conduct continuously and more slowly.

## 8. Wallerian degeneration

Wallerian degeneration is degeneration of the axon and myelin distal to a nerve injury.

### Sequence

1. The axon is interrupted and distal axoplasm loses connection with the cell body.
2. The distal axon fragments and myelin breaks into debris.
3. Schwann cells and macrophages clear myelin and axonal debris.
4. Schwann cells proliferate and form regeneration tubes within the endoneurium.
5. The proximal axon forms sprouts. One sprout may grow through the tube toward the target at roughly 1–3 mm/day.
6. Reinnervation may restore function, but recovery is incomplete if the tube is disrupted, the gap is large, or the neuron dies.

In the CNS, oligodendrocyte myelin, inhibitory molecules, glial scarring, and poor guidance make regeneration limited. The cell body may also show chromatolysis, with swelling and dispersion of Nissl substance, after axonal injury.

## 9. Neuromuscular junction (NMJ)

The neuromuscular junction is a chemical synapse between a somatic motor-neuron terminal and a skeletal muscle fibre.

### Structure

- **Presynaptic terminal:** contains acetylcholine vesicles, mitochondria, voltage-gated calcium channels, and active zones.
- **Synaptic cleft:** approximately 20–50 nm wide and contains basal lamina with acetylcholinesterase.
- **Motor end plate:** specialized muscle membrane with junctional folds and many nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.

### Transmission

1. A motor-neuron action potential arrives at the terminal.
2. Voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open and calcium enters the terminal.
3. Calcium activates synaptotagmin and SNARE proteins, causing acetylcholine release.
4. Acetylcholine binds nicotinic Nm receptors and opens ligand-gated cation channels.
5. Na+ entry produces an end-plate potential.
6. If threshold is reached, voltage-gated sodium channels in adjacent muscle membrane generate a muscle action potential.
7. Acetylcholinesterase rapidly breaks acetylcholine into acetate and choline. Choline is taken back into the nerve terminal to resynthesize acetylcholine.

The NMJ normally has a safety factor: one nerve impulse releases enough acetylcholine to exceed the threshold for a muscle action potential.

### NMJ blockers and toxins

- **Curare-like non-depolarizing blockers:** competitively block nicotinic receptors and cause flaccid paralysis. They are used clinically with anaesthesia.
- **Succinylcholine:** depolarizing blocker. Persistent end-plate depolarization first causes fasciculations and then prevents further action potentials.
- **Botulinum toxin:** prevents acetylcholine release by cleaving SNARE proteins, causing flaccid paralysis.
- **Organophosphates:** inhibit acetylcholinesterase, causing excess acetylcholine, fasciculations, bronchoconstriction, secretions, bradycardia, and eventual paralysis.
- **Magnesium excess:** reduces calcium entry and acetylcholine release, weakening transmission.
- **Black widow spider toxin:** causes excessive transmitter release followed by depletion and neuromuscular dysfunction.

## 10. Myasthenia gravis

Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disorder of neuromuscular transmission. Most commonly, antibodies reduce or block postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors; some patients have antibodies against MuSK or related proteins.

The end-plate safety factor is reduced. Repeated activity progressively fails to produce a muscle action potential, causing **fatigable weakness**. Typical findings are ptosis, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, and proximal limb weakness. Sensation and the autonomic nervous system are generally preserved.

Diagnosis may include antibody tests, repetitive nerve stimulation or single-fibre electromyography, and clinical response to treatment. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors increase acetylcholine at the NMJ. Immunotherapy and treatment of thymic disease may be required. A crisis can impair respiratory muscles and is an emergency.

This differs from Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome, in which antibodies affect presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels. Strength may temporarily improve with repeated activity because calcium accumulates in the terminal.

## 11. Classification of muscle

### Skeletal muscle

Skeletal muscle is striated, usually voluntary, and supplied by somatic motor nerves. Fibres are long, cylindrical, multinucleated, and arranged in parallel. It produces posture, movement, heat, and joint stabilization.

### Cardiac muscle

Cardiac muscle is striated and involuntary. Cells are branched, usually single-nucleated, and connected by intercalated discs containing gap junctions and desmosomes. It has autorhythmicity and acts as a functional syncytium.

### Smooth muscle

Smooth muscle is non-striated and involuntary. It is found in blood vessels and hollow organs. It contracts slowly, economically, and often for long periods under autonomic, hormonal, local, or stretch control.

## 12. Structure of skeletal muscle

The organization is:

`whole muscle -> fascicle -> muscle fibre (cell) -> myofibril -> sarcomere -> thick and thin filaments`

- **Epimysium** surrounds the whole muscle.
- **Perimysium** surrounds each fascicle.
- **Endomysium** surrounds each muscle fibre.
- A muscle fibre is a long multinucleated cell with sarcolemma, sarcoplasm, mitochondria, myofibrils, sarcoplasmic reticulum, and transverse tubules.
- **T-tubules** carry the surface action potential into the fibre.
- **Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)** stores and releases calcium.
- **Triad:** one T-tubule between two terminal cisternae of the SR. In skeletal muscle it lies near the A-I junction.

## 13. Sarcomere

The sarcomere is the basic contractile unit between two Z discs.

- **Z disc:** boundary of a sarcomere; anchors thin filaments.
- **I band:** thin filaments without thick filaments; becomes shorter during contraction.
- **A band:** full length of thick filaments; remains constant during contraction.
- **H zone:** central part of A band containing thick filaments without thin overlap; becomes smaller or disappears during contraction.
- **M line:** holds thick filaments at the centre.
- **Titin:** elastic protein connecting thick filaments to Z discs and helping restore resting length.

The sliding-filament theory states that filaments do not significantly shorten. Thin filaments slide toward the M line, reducing I band and H zone while the A band remains approximately constant.

## 14. Contractile and regulatory proteins

### Thick filament

Myosin has a long tail and two globular heads. Each head contains an actin-binding site and ATPase activity. Myosin heads form cross-bridges with actin and generate force by a power stroke.

### Thin filament

- **Actin:** a double helix of globular actin molecules with myosin-binding sites.
- **Tropomyosin:** lies along actin and blocks myosin-binding sites at rest.
- **Troponin:** consists of TnT (binds tropomyosin), TnI (inhibits actin-myosin interaction), and TnC (binds calcium).

Calcium binding to TnC moves the troponin-tropomyosin complex and exposes actin sites. ATP binding detaches myosin from actin; ATP hydrolysis cocks the head; release of phosphate and ADP produces the power stroke.

## 15. Excitation-contraction coupling

Excitation-contraction coupling links a muscle action potential to force production.

1. A motor-neuron impulse releases acetylcholine at the NMJ.
2. The muscle end-plate potential triggers a muscle action potential.
3. The action potential spreads across the sarcolemma and down T-tubules.
4. T-tubule voltage sensors, mainly dihydropyridine receptors, change conformation.
5. They activate ryanodine receptors in the adjacent SR.
6. SR calcium is released into the sarcoplasm.
7. Calcium binds troponin C, moving tropomyosin.
8. Actin-myosin cycling produces tension and shortening.
9. SERCA pumps return calcium to the SR using ATP.
10. Calcium leaves troponin, tropomyosin again blocks actin sites, and relaxation occurs.

In skeletal muscle, T-tubule voltage sensors are mechanically coupled to SR calcium-release channels. External calcium is less essential for initiating one normal skeletal muscle contraction than it is for cardiac and smooth muscle contraction.

## 16. Mechanism of muscle contraction: cross-bridge cycle

1. At rest, ATP-bound myosin is detached from actin, and tropomyosin blocks actin.
2. Calcium exposes the binding site.
3. The energized myosin head, with ADP and phosphate attached, binds actin.
4. Phosphate release produces the power stroke; the thin filament moves toward the centre.
5. ADP leaves, leaving myosin tightly attached.
6. A new ATP binds myosin and causes detachment.
7. ATP is hydrolysed, re-cocking the myosin head.
8. The cycle repeats while calcium and ATP are present.

Force depends on the number of simultaneously attached cross-bridges, initial muscle length, calcium concentration, stimulation frequency, and muscle fibre type.

## 17. Types of muscle contraction

- **Isometric contraction:** tension develops but muscle length does not change; for example, holding a weight still.
- **Isotonic contraction:** muscle length changes while load is moved.
	- **Concentric:** muscle shortens while producing force.
	- **Eccentric:** muscle lengthens while resisting a load; it can produce high force and may cause delayed soreness.
- **Auxotonic contraction:** both tension and length change, as in many natural movements.

### Twitch and summation

A single action potential produces a muscle twitch with latent, contraction, and relaxation phases. A second stimulus arriving before full relaxation causes temporal summation. Repeated high-frequency stimulation can produce incomplete or complete tetanus. The nervous system grades whole-muscle force by changing motor-unit recruitment and firing frequency.

## 18. Motor unit

A motor unit consists of one alpha motor neuron and all skeletal muscle fibres it supplies. All fibres in one motor unit contract together.

- Small motor units have few fibres and provide fine control, as in eye and hand muscles.
- Large motor units have many fibres and provide powerful, less precise movement, as in large limb muscles.

Motor units are recruited according to the size principle: small, fatigue-resistant units are activated first, followed by larger, more powerful units as force demand increases. Rate coding then increases force by increasing firing frequency.

### Skeletal muscle fibre types

- **Type I, slow oxidative:** slow contraction, high myoglobin and mitochondria, rich blood supply, very fatigue resistant; posture and endurance.
- **Type IIa, fast oxidative-glycolytic:** fast, moderately fatigue resistant, suited to repeated powerful activity.
- **Type IIx/IIb, fast glycolytic:** very rapid and powerful but fatigue quickly; high anaerobic capacity.

## 19. Muscle fatigue

Fatigue is a reversible decline in the ability to produce force despite continued stimulation. It may involve:

- reduced central motor drive;
- failure at the NMJ during extreme activity;
- reduced SR calcium release;
- accumulation of inorganic phosphate, ADP, hydrogen ions, and extracellular potassium;
- depletion of glycogen or phosphocreatine;
- impaired cross-bridge function;
- reduced oxygen delivery or blood flow.

Lactate is associated with intense anaerobic work but is not the only cause of fatigue. Recovery requires restoration of oxygen stores, phosphocreatine, glycogen, ion gradients, and normal calcium handling.

## 20. Rigor mortis

Rigor mortis is postmortem stiffening caused by ATP depletion. Without ATP, myosin cannot detach from actin. Calcium leaks from damaged SR and activates cross-bridges, but ATP-dependent detachment and calcium reuptake cannot occur. Rigor begins after a delay, becomes pronounced, and later disappears as proteolysis breaks down muscle proteins.

This demonstrates two ATP requirements: ATP is needed both to detach myosin from actin and to pump calcium back into the SR.

## 21. Smooth muscle

Smooth muscle cells are spindle-shaped, contain one central nucleus, and lack sarcomeres and troponin. Thin filaments attach to dense bodies, which transmit force through the cell. Intermediate filaments help distribute tension.

### Excitation and contraction

Calcium enters from extracellular fluid and is also released from the SR. Calcium binds **calmodulin**, not troponin. The calcium-calmodulin complex activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK). MLCK phosphorylates the myosin light chain, allowing myosin to interact with actin. Relaxation occurs when myosin light-chain phosphatase removes the phosphate.

Smooth muscle can be activated by:

- autonomic neurotransmitters;
- circulating hormones;
- local metabolites and endothelial factors;
- stretch;
- spontaneous pacemaker activity;
- drugs and chemical mediators.

### Types and properties

- **Single-unit (visceral) smooth muscle:** cells are linked by gap junctions and contract together; found in intestine, uterus, ureter, and bladder. It often shows spontaneous activity and stretch-induced contraction.
- **Multiunit smooth muscle:** fibres act more independently and receive precise autonomic control; found in iris, ciliary muscle, and some large airways.

Smooth muscle has a slower onset and longer duration of contraction, uses little ATP, and can maintain tone by the latch state. It can shorten over a wide range of initial lengths and may show stress-relaxation, allowing hollow organs to accommodate volume without a large sustained pressure rise.

### Smooth-muscle relaxation

Relaxation may result from reduced intracellular calcium, reduced MLCK activity, activation of potassium channels, nitric oxide, cyclic GMP, cyclic AMP, or sympathetic beta-2 receptor stimulation, depending on the tissue.

## 22. Correlation of nerve and muscle physiology

### The complete excitation-contraction chain

`stimulus -> receptor or motor command -> membrane depolarization -> action potential -> calcium entry/release -> contractile protein activation -> force -> calcium removal -> relaxation`

The same chain explains normal movement and many diseases:

1. **Ion gradients create excitability.** Na+, K+, Cl-, and calcium determine RMP, threshold, and action-potential generation.
2. **Myelin determines speed.** Large myelinated fibres rapidly carry motor commands and proprioception; small or unmyelinated fibres carry slower pain and autonomic signals.
3. **The NMJ is the bridge.** The motor neuron uses calcium-dependent acetylcholine release to turn a nerve impulse into a muscle action potential.
4. **Calcium is the common messenger.** It triggers neurotransmitter release, activates skeletal-muscle contraction, regulates smooth-muscle myosin, and also participates in blood coagulation.
5. **ATP is essential at every stage.** It maintains ion gradients, powers axonal transport, detaches myosin, and pumps calcium back into storage.
6. **Blood supports excitable tissue.** Haemoglobin supplies oxygen for ATP production; plasma supplies electrolytes and nutrients; blood flow removes heat and metabolic waste.
7. **Fatigue can occur at different levels.** Failure may be central, in the nerve, NMJ, muscle membrane, SR calcium release, contractile proteins, or energy supply. Correct localization depends on the pattern of weakness and appropriate testing.
8. **The autonomic system links nerves to smooth muscle.** It regulates vessel diameter, gut movement, airway tone, bladder function, and pupil size, usually without conscious control.
9. **Injury has predictable consequences.** Wallerian degeneration interrupts motor and sensory conduction; denervated muscle atrophies and may show fasciculations before reinnervation or permanent loss.
10. **Clinical examples reveal the chain.** Myasthenia gravis affects postsynaptic transmission, botulism affects acetylcholine release, hypokalaemia affects membrane excitability, calcium disorders affect threshold and contraction, and ATP depletion produces rigor mortis.

## High-yield comparison

| Feature | Skeletal muscle | Cardiac muscle | Smooth muscle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Striations | Present | Present | Absent |
| Main control | Voluntary, somatic | Involuntary, intrinsic plus autonomic | Involuntary, autonomic/hormonal/local |
| Regulatory protein | Troponin-tropomyosin | Troponin-tropomyosin | Calmodulin-MLCK |
| Calcium source | Mainly SR for a normal twitch | Extracellular fluid and SR | Extracellular fluid and SR |
| Contraction | Rapid, can tetanize | Rhythmic, cannot normally tetanize | Slow, sustained, economical |
| Cell arrangement | Long multinucleated fibres | Branched cells with intercalated discs | Spindle-shaped cells |

The unifying principle is that electrical activity and mechanical activity are coupled by calcium, while ATP and blood supply determine whether the system can continue to work and recover.
