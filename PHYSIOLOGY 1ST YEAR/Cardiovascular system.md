# Cardiovascular System Physiology

## 1. Introduction to the cardiovascular system

The cardiovascular system consists of the **heart, blood, and blood vessels**. It maintains tissue perfusion by moving blood through the pulmonary and systemic circulations.

- **Pulmonary circulation:** right ventricle -> pulmonary arteries -> lung capillaries -> pulmonary veins -> left atrium. It oxygenates blood and removes carbon dioxide.
- **Systemic circulation:** left ventricle -> aorta -> arteries -> arterioles -> tissue capillaries -> venules -> veins -> right atrium. It supplies tissues and returns deoxygenated blood.
- **Coronary circulation:** supplies the myocardium itself.

The cardiovascular system transports oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, hormones, heat, immune cells, and metabolic waste. It helps regulate pH, temperature, fluid balance, and blood pressure. Its fundamental task is to match tissue blood flow to tissue metabolism while maintaining adequate arterial pressure.

### General principles of circulation

Blood flow through a vascular bed depends on the pressure gradient and resistance:

$$Q=\frac{\Delta P}{R}$$

Resistance is strongly affected by vessel radius. For ideal laminar flow, Poiseuille's relationship is:

$$R\propto\frac{1}{r^4}$$

Therefore, a small change in arteriolar radius can produce a large change in blood flow. Arterioles are the main resistance vessels and determine distribution of cardiac output and arterial pressure.

The circulation is arranged in series: the same average flow passes through pulmonary and systemic circuits. Within systemic organs, vascular beds are arranged mainly in parallel, so each organ receives blood at approximately the arterial pressure but can regulate its own resistance.

### Vessel functions

- **Arteries:** conduct blood away from the heart and dampen pulsatile pressure.
- **Arterioles:** regulate resistance, organ perfusion, and systemic blood pressure.
- **Capillaries:** exchange gases, water, nutrients, and wastes.
- **Veins:** capacitance vessels; store most of the blood volume and return blood to the heart.
- **Lymphatics:** return excess interstitial fluid and proteins to the circulation.

## 2. Properties of cardiac muscle

Cardiac muscle is striated, involuntary, and specialized for rhythmic pumping.

- **Excitability:** responds to a stimulus by producing an action potential.
- **Conductivity:** spreads excitation from cell to cell.
- **Contractility:** develops tension and shortens when activated.
- **Automaticity:** some cells generate impulses spontaneously.
- **Rhythmicity:** automatic impulses occur at regular intervals.
- **Refractoriness:** prevents immediate re-excitation and tetanic contraction.

Cardiac cells are joined by intercalated discs. Desmosomes provide mechanical attachment and gap junctions permit rapid electrical spread, so atrial and ventricular myocardium behave as functional syncytia. The fibrous cardiac skeleton electrically separates atria from ventricles; the atrioventricular bundle is the normal electrical bridge.

### Cardiac action potential

Ventricular contractile-cell action potentials have a long plateau:

1. **Phase 4:** resting potential, approximately -90 mV.
2. **Phase 0:** rapid sodium entry causes depolarization.
3. **Phase 1:** brief early repolarization.
4. **Phase 2:** calcium entry through L-type channels balances potassium exit; this plateau supports contraction.
5. **Phase 3:** calcium channels close and potassium efflux repolarizes the cell.

The long action potential produces a long absolute refractory period. This prevents summation and tetanus, allowing the heart to relax and fill between beats.

Pacemaker cells in the sinoatrial node have no stable resting potential. Their phase 4 spontaneous depolarization is produced by the funny current, calcium currents, and declining potassium conductance. The sinoatrial node normally sets the heart rate.

## 3. Cardiac cycle

The cardiac cycle is the sequence of events during one heartbeat. At a heart rate of 75/min, one cycle lasts about 0.8 seconds.

### Phases

1. **Atrial systole (about 0.1 s):** atria contract and add the final portion of ventricular filling.
2. **Isovolumetric ventricular contraction (about 0.05 s):** ventricles begin to contract; AV valves close, but semilunar valves remain closed. Pressure rises while volume remains constant.
3. **Ventricular ejection (about 0.25 s):** ventricular pressure exceeds aortic and pulmonary artery pressure; semilunar valves open and blood is ejected.
4. **Isovolumetric relaxation (about 0.08 s):** ventricles relax; semilunar valves close, but AV valves remain closed. Pressure falls at constant volume.
5. **Ventricular filling (about 0.4 s):** AV valves open. Most filling is passive, followed by atrial systole.

### Volumes and pressure

- **End-diastolic volume (EDV):** ventricular volume after filling, approximately 120 mL.
- **End-systolic volume (ESV):** volume remaining after ejection, approximately 50 mL.
- **Stroke volume (SV):** volume ejected per beat. $SV=EDV-ESV$, approximately 70 mL.
- **Ejection fraction:** fraction of EDV ejected. $EF=\frac{SV}{EDV}\times100$, normally approximately 55–70%.

The pressure-volume loop includes ventricular filling, isovolumetric contraction, ejection, and isovolumetric relaxation. Its area represents ventricular external mechanical work.

### Valve function

The tricuspid and mitral valves prevent backflow from ventricles to atria. The pulmonary and aortic valves prevent backflow from great arteries into ventricles. Valves open and close because of pressure differences, not because of active muscular movement.

## 4. Heart sounds

- **First heart sound (S1):** closure of mitral and tricuspid valves at the beginning of ventricular systole. It is lower-pitched and longer.
- **Second heart sound (S2):** closure of aortic and pulmonary valves at the beginning of ventricular diastole. It is shorter and higher-pitched. Physiological splitting during inspiration is common because right ventricular ejection lasts longer.
- **Third heart sound (S3):** low-frequency sound during rapid ventricular filling. It may be normal in children and young adults but can indicate volume overload or heart failure in older adults.
- **Fourth heart sound (S4):** sound from atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle. It is usually pathological and occurs before S1.

Murmurs are sounds produced by turbulent flow through abnormal valves, septal defects, or high-flow states.

## 5. Pulse

The arterial pulse is the pressure wave generated by left ventricular ejection and transmitted along arteries. It is not the same as the actual forward movement of one column of blood.

### Pulse examination

Rate, rhythm, volume, character, and equality are assessed. The radial pulse is commonly examined; carotid, brachial, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, and dorsalis pedis pulses assess other regions.

- **Tachycardia:** adult resting rate above approximately 100/min.
- **Bradycardia:** adult resting rate below approximately 60/min, although trained athletes may be normal.
- **Pulse pressure:** systolic minus diastolic pressure.
- **Dicrotic notch:** brief fall and rebound related to aortic valve closure.

Pulse pressure increases with increased stroke volume or reduced arterial compliance. It may be wide in aortic regurgitation and narrow in low stroke-volume states.

## 6. Cardiac output, factors, and measurement

Cardiac output (CO) is the volume pumped by one ventricle per minute:

$$CO=HR\times SV$$

At rest, a typical adult CO is about 5 L/min. The cardiac index adjusts CO for body surface area and is usually about 2.5–4.0 L/min/m².

### Factors controlling stroke volume

1. **Preload:** ventricular fibre stretch at the end of diastole, related to venous return and EDV. By the Frank-Starling law, increased filling increases force and stroke volume within physiological limits.
2. **Contractility:** force at a given preload. Sympathetic stimulation, circulating catecholamines, and increased intracellular calcium increase contractility. Hypoxia, acidosis, and some drugs reduce it.
3. **Afterload:** pressure the ventricle must overcome to eject blood. Increased arterial pressure or outflow resistance increases ESV and can reduce SV.

### Factors affecting heart rate

Heart rate is increased by sympathetic activity, catecholamines, exercise, fever, and some hormones. It is decreased by vagal activity, rest, sleep, and trained cardiovascular conditioning. Increased heart rate can initially raise CO, but very rapid rates shorten diastole and may reduce ventricular filling and coronary perfusion.

### Measurement of cardiac output

- **Fick principle:** $CO=\frac{O_2\ uptake}{arterial\ O_2 content-venous\ O_2 content}$.
- **Thermodilution:** a known indicator is injected into the right atrium and temperature change is detected in the pulmonary artery.
- **Echocardiography/Doppler:** measures ventricular outflow tract area and blood velocity.
- **Impedance and pulse-contour methods:** estimate beat-to-beat output non-invasively or minimally invasively.
- **Ventricular volumes:** MRI, radionuclide methods, or echocardiography estimate EDV and ESV, then calculate SV.

## 7. Heart rate regulation

The medullary cardiovascular centres receive input from baroreceptors, chemoreceptors, higher brain centres, and cardiopulmonary receptors.

- **Sympathetic stimulation:** increases SA-node firing, AV conduction, contractility, and venous tone through beta-1 receptors.
- **Parasympathetic vagal stimulation:** slows SA-node firing and AV conduction through muscarinic receptors; its effect on ventricular contractility is smaller.
- **Exercise, emotion, pain, and fever:** usually increase heart rate.
- **Respiratory sinus arrhythmia:** heart rate rises during inspiration and falls during expiration, partly due to changing vagal activity.

## 8. Blood pressure

Arterial blood pressure is the lateral force of blood against arterial walls. A typical adult resting pressure is close to 120/80 mmHg, but normal values vary.

- **Systolic pressure:** maximum pressure during ventricular ejection.
- **Diastolic pressure:** minimum pressure during ventricular diastole.
- **Pulse pressure:** $PP=SBP-DBP$.
- **Mean arterial pressure:** approximately $MAP=DBP+\frac{1}{3}(PP)$ at normal heart rates.

Mean arterial pressure is related to cardiac output and total peripheral resistance:

$$MAP\approx CO\times TPR$$

It is also influenced by blood volume, arterial compliance, blood viscosity, and the distribution of vascular resistance.

### Factors affecting blood pressure

- Cardiac output and its components, heart rate and stroke volume
- Arteriolar resistance and vessel radius
- Blood volume and venous return
- Arterial compliance
- Blood viscosity and haematocrit
- Age, posture, exercise, temperature, stress, sleep, and hormones
- Renal sodium and water handling

## 9. Measurement of blood pressure

### Direct measurement

An intra-arterial catheter measures pressure continuously and is used in critical care and research.

### Indirect measurement

The cuff is inflated above systolic pressure to occlude the artery, then slowly deflated.

- **Korotkoff phase I:** first tapping sound, approximating systolic pressure.
- **Korotkoff phase V:** disappearance of sound, approximating diastolic pressure in adults.

Accurate measurement requires an appropriately sized cuff, supported arm at heart level, quiet rest, and no talking. An undersized cuff gives falsely high values. Automated oscillometric devices detect pressure oscillations and estimate systolic and diastolic values.

## 10. Regulation of blood pressure

### Short-term regulation: seconds to minutes

#### Baroreceptor reflex

Stretch receptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch detect arterial pressure. Afferents travel through the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves to the nucleus tractus solitarius in the medulla.

- Increased pressure -> increased baroreceptor firing -> reduced sympathetic and increased parasympathetic activity -> reduced heart rate, contractility, arteriolar resistance, and venous return.
- Reduced pressure -> reduced firing -> increased sympathetic and reduced parasympathetic activity -> tachycardia, increased contractility, vasoconstriction, and venoconstriction.

The baroreceptor reflex is rapid and important during standing, haemorrhage, and sudden pressure changes. Baroreceptors reset during sustained hypertension, so they are less important for long-term pressure control.

#### Chemoreceptor reflex

Carotid and aortic bodies respond to low oxygen, high carbon dioxide, and low pH. Their reflex increases ventilation and, during severe hypotension, sympathetic vasoconstriction.

#### CNS ischemic response

Severe reduction in medullary blood flow causes accumulation of carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions, powerfully increasing sympathetic vasoconstriction. It is an emergency response rather than a normal regulator.

#### Adrenal response

Sympathetic stimulation releases adrenaline and noradrenaline. These increase cardiac activity and alter vascular tone according to receptor distribution.

### Intermediate regulation: minutes to hours

- **Capillary fluid shift:** changes in capillary hydrostatic pressure alter movement of fluid between plasma and interstitium, helping restore blood volume.
- **Stress relaxation:** vascular smooth muscle gradually adjusts its tension when vessels are stretched.
- **Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system begins:** renin release increases when renal perfusion or macula densa sodium delivery falls.
- **Vasopressin:** released in response to increased osmolality or low blood volume; causes water retention and, at higher levels, vasoconstriction.
- **Adrenal catecholamines:** support cardiac and vascular responses.

### Long-term regulation: hours to years

The kidneys provide the dominant long-term control by regulating sodium and water excretion. Increased arterial pressure promotes pressure diuresis and natriuresis, reducing extracellular fluid volume, venous return, and cardiac output.

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is activated by low renal perfusion, low distal sodium, or sympathetic beta-1 stimulation:

`renin -> angiotensin I -> ACE -> angiotensin II -> vasoconstriction + aldosterone + thirst + ADH`

Angiotensin II raises pressure directly and stimulates aldosterone, which increases renal sodium and water reabsorption. Atrial natriuretic peptide and brain natriuretic peptide oppose volume expansion by promoting natriuresis and reducing renin and aldosterone. Long-term control is also influenced by thirst, ADH, renal disease, adrenal hormones, and vascular remodelling.

## 11. ECG: uses, waveform, and significance

An electrocardiogram records voltage differences produced by cardiac electrical activity from the body surface. It helps assess rate, rhythm, conduction, chamber enlargement, ischemia, infarction, electrolyte effects, and some drug effects.

### Normal ECG waveform

- **P wave:** atrial depolarization.
- **PR interval:** beginning of atrial depolarization to beginning of ventricular depolarization; reflects AV conduction. Normal is about 0.12–0.20 s.
- **QRS complex:** ventricular depolarization; normally less than about 0.12 s. Atrial repolarization is hidden within it.
- **ST segment:** period when ventricles are uniformly depolarized; should be near the isoelectric line.
- **T wave:** ventricular repolarization.
- **QT interval:** ventricular depolarization and repolarization; varies with heart rate.

The ECG does not directly record contraction. Electrical events precede mechanical events, so an ECG can be abnormal even before pump function visibly changes.

### Heart block

Heart block is delayed or failed conduction through the cardiac conduction system.

- **First-degree AV block:** prolonged PR interval, but every atrial impulse conducts.
- **Second-degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach):** progressive PR prolongation followed by a dropped QRS.
- **Second-degree Mobitz II:** intermittent dropped QRS complexes without progressive PR prolongation; may progress to complete block.
- **Third-degree AV block:** no atrial impulses conduct to the ventricles; atria and ventricles beat independently. A slow escape rhythm may maintain life temporarily.

Bundle-branch blocks widen the QRS because ventricular depolarization uses an abnormal route. Causes include ischemia, fibrosis, drugs, electrolyte disturbances, and conduction-system disease.

## 12. Coronary circulation

The right and left coronary arteries arise from the aortic sinuses. The left coronary artery divides mainly into the anterior interventricular and circumflex arteries. Venous blood returns through cardiac veins to the coronary sinus and right atrium.

The myocardium extracts a high proportion of oxygen at rest, so increased cardiac oxygen demand is met mainly by increasing coronary blood flow. Left coronary flow is greatest during diastole because systolic contraction compresses intramyocardial vessels. Tachycardia is therefore dangerous: it increases oxygen demand and shortens the diastolic period available for coronary perfusion.

Local metabolic vasodilation is the most important regulator. Adenosine, low oxygen, increased carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions, potassium, and nitric oxide dilate coronary vessels. Sympathetic stimulation increases demand and can also produce direct beta-2 vasodilation, while alpha-mediated constriction may occur in some vessels.

Coronary ischemia occurs when oxygen supply cannot meet demand. It may cause angina, myocardial infarction, impaired contractility, electrical instability, and heart failure.

## 13. Cutaneous circulation and triple response

Skin blood flow regulates body temperature and contributes to blood pressure control. It is low in cold conditions and increases greatly in heat. Arteriovenous anastomoses in fingers, toes, ears, and nose permit rapid heat exchange. Sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibres dominate in cold; active vasodilation and sweating support heat loss in heat.

### Triple response of Lewis

Firm stroking or injury to skin produces:

1. **Red reaction:** a local red line from capillary dilation.
2. **Flare:** a wider red area caused by arteriolar dilation through an axon reflex.
3. **Wheal:** a raised pale swelling due to increased capillary permeability and local oedema.

Histamine and other inflammatory mediators contribute. The triple response demonstrates that nerves, arterioles, capillaries, and tissue mediators interact in local circulation.

## 14. Shock

Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery, causing cellular dysfunction and, if untreated, organ failure.

- **Hypovolaemic shock:** loss of blood or fluid reduces venous return and preload.
- **Cardiogenic shock:** pump failure reduces cardiac output despite adequate volume.
- **Distributive shock:** excessive vasodilation and maldistribution; includes septic, anaphylactic, and neurogenic shock.
- **Obstructive shock:** impaired circulation due to pulmonary embolism, cardiac tamponade, or tension pneumothorax.

Early compensation includes tachycardia, increased contractility, arteriolar vasoconstriction, venoconstriction, RAAS activation, ADH release, and increased ventilation. Skin becomes cool and pale in many forms because of vasoconstriction, although distributive shock may initially produce warm skin.

Progressive shock causes anaerobic metabolism, lactate accumulation, acidosis, myocardial depression, endothelial injury, capillary leak, renal failure, and worsening hypotension. Treatment requires restoring oxygen delivery and addressing the cause: airway and breathing support, fluid or blood replacement when appropriate, vasopressors, antibiotics for sepsis, or relief of obstruction.

## 15. Effects of exercise on the cardiovascular system

### Immediate responses

- Heart rate increases through withdrawal of vagal tone and increased sympathetic activity.
- Stroke volume rises through increased venous return, contractility, and the Frank-Starling mechanism.
- Cardiac output can increase four- to sixfold during strenuous exercise.
- Systolic pressure rises; diastolic pressure changes little or may fall slightly during dynamic exercise because of skeletal-muscle vasodilation.
- Blood flow increases to active skeletal muscle and myocardium.
- Blood flow to the kidneys and gastrointestinal tract decreases temporarily.
- Skin flow increases later as heat accumulates, but may be restricted during severe dehydration or shock.
- Venous return is supported by the muscle pump, respiratory pump, venoconstriction, and the heart's suction effects.

### Training adaptations

Regular aerobic training increases plasma volume, venous return, stroke volume, maximal cardiac output, capillary density, mitochondrial capacity, and oxygen extraction. Resting and submaximal heart rates decrease for a given workload. These changes improve cardiovascular reserve.

## 16. Effects of exercise on the respiratory system

- Ventilation increases through greater tidal volume and respiratory rate.
- Alveolar ventilation rises to increase oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide removal.
- Pulmonary blood flow and recruitment of pulmonary capillaries increase.
- Diffusion capacity improves because more capillaries participate and transit remains adequate.
- Ventilation-perfusion matching generally improves.
- Oxygen extraction by muscles increases, widening the arterial-venous oxygen difference.
- During intense exercise, lactate and hydrogen ions stimulate ventilation; carbon dioxide produced from buffering further increases ventilatory drive.

The respiratory system usually has reserve in healthy people, so maximal exercise is often limited by cardiovascular delivery, muscle metabolism, or fatigue rather than by lung diffusion alone. Lung disease, airway obstruction, pulmonary vascular disease, or respiratory muscle weakness can make ventilation the limiting factor.

## 17. Cardiovascular-respiratory correlation

The cardiovascular and respiratory systems form one oxygen-delivery system:

`ventilation -> alveolar oxygen -> pulmonary diffusion -> haemoglobin loading -> cardiac output -> tissue perfusion -> cellular oxygen use -> carbon dioxide return -> ventilation`

1. **The lungs load oxygen and remove CO2; the heart distributes the blood.** A normal lung cannot maintain tissue oxygen delivery if cardiac output is inadequate, and a strong heart cannot compensate for severe alveolar or diffusion failure.
2. **Cardiac output determines oxygen delivery:**

$$DO_2=CO\times C_{aO_2}$$

	Oxygen delivery therefore depends on flow, haemoglobin concentration, and oxygen saturation.
3. **Exercise raises demand:** muscle metabolism increases CO2 and hydrogen ions. Chemoreceptors and central command increase ventilation, while sympathetic activation increases heart rate, contractility, venous return, and muscle perfusion.
4. **Blood pressure preserves perfusion:** MAP must be high enough to drive blood through organ resistance beds, but local metabolic control redistributes flow toward active tissues.
5. **Coronary flow supports the pump:** increased heart work requires increased coronary oxygen supply. Tachycardia can compromise this by shortening diastole.
6. **The pulse reflects ventricular ejection:** changes in stroke volume, arterial compliance, and resistance alter pulse pressure and pulse character.
7. **The ECG predicts mechanical timing:** P, QRS, and T waves represent electrical events that coordinate atrial contraction, ventricular contraction, and relaxation.
8. **Shock breaks the chain:** reduced volume, pump failure, obstruction, or vasodilation reduces effective tissue oxygen delivery, causing anaerobic metabolism and organ dysfunction.
9. **Skin circulation balances competing needs:** vasoconstriction preserves core pressure during shock, while vasodilation during heat stress protects temperature at the cost of peripheral pooling.
10. **Blood disorders affect circulation:** anaemia reduces oxygen content, polycythaemia increases viscosity, and changes in volume alter preload and blood pressure.

## High-yield summary

The cardiovascular system is a pressure-flow pump regulated by electrical activity, cardiac muscle properties, vascular resistance, blood volume, and neural, hormonal, and renal feedback. Cardiac output and arterial pressure deliver oxygenated blood, while the respiratory system supplies oxygen and removes carbon dioxide. The ECG, heart sounds, pulse, blood pressure, coronary flow, skin response, shock patterns, and exercise responses are different observable results of the same integrated circulation mechanism.
