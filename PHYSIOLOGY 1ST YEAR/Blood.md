# Blood, Haematology and Introduction to Nerve-Muscle Physiology

## 1. Introduction: composition and functions of blood

Blood is a specialized fluid connective tissue. It circulates through the heart and blood vessels and maintains the internal environment of cells. Whole blood consists of **plasma** and **formed elements**.

| Component | Approximate proportion | Main role |
|---|---:|---|
| Plasma | 55% | Fluid medium for transport, buffering, and regulation |
| Red blood cells (RBCs) | 45% in an average adult male; slightly lower in females | Oxygen and carbon dioxide transport |
| White blood cells (WBCs) and platelets | <1% | Defence and haemostasis |

### Major functions of blood

1. **Transport:** oxygen from lungs to tissues, carbon dioxide from tissues to lungs, nutrients from the gastrointestinal tract, hormones, metabolic waste to excretory organs, and heat.
2. **Regulation:** pH, body temperature, water distribution, osmotic pressure, and electrolyte concentration.
3. **Protection:** leukocytes and antibodies defend against infection; platelets and clotting factors limit blood loss.
4. **Communication:** hormones, cytokines, and other chemical signals travel in plasma.
5. **Homeostasis:** blood links the respiratory, renal, endocrine, immune, and cardiovascular systems.

### Plasma

Plasma is the pale-yellow fluid portion of unclotted blood. It is about 90–92% water and 8–10% dissolved substances. Plasma without fibrinogen and most clotting factors is called **serum**.

#### Plasma proteins

| Protein | Approximate concentration | Functions |
|---|---:|---|
| Albumin | 3.5–5.0 g/dL | Produces most colloid osmotic pressure; transports bilirubin, fatty acids, hormones, calcium, and drugs; buffers pH |
| Globulins | 2.0–3.5 g/dL | Alpha and beta globulins transport lipids, metals, and hormones; gamma globulins are antibodies |
| Fibrinogen | 200–400 mg/dL | Soluble precursor of fibrin in clot formation |
| Regulatory proteins | Variable | Complement, enzymes, acute-phase proteins, cytokines, and coagulation factors |

The liver synthesizes albumin, fibrinogen, and most alpha and beta globulins. Plasma cells produce immunoglobulins. Low albumin causes reduced plasma oncotic pressure, tissue oedema, and altered transport of protein-bound substances.

## 2. Red blood cells

### Structure and functions

An erythrocyte is a flexible, biconcave disc about 7–8 micrometres in diameter. Mature human RBCs have no nucleus, mitochondria, or ribosomes. This leaves space for haemoglobin and prevents the cell from consuming the oxygen it carries. The biconcave shape provides a large surface area and short diffusion distance.

RBC functions are to:

- carry oxygen by haemoglobin;
- carry part of carbon dioxide as carbaminohaemoglobin;
- facilitate carbon dioxide transport as bicarbonate through carbonic anhydrase;
- buffer hydrogen ions and help maintain blood pH;
- influence blood viscosity and microcirculatory flow.

### Erythropoiesis

**Erythropoiesis** is the production of RBCs. In adults it occurs mainly in red bone marrow of the vertebrae, ribs, sternum, skull, pelvis, and proximal ends of long bones. Before birth, blood formation shifts from yolk sac to liver and spleen and finally to bone marrow.

The sequence is:

`haematopoietic stem cell -> common myeloid progenitor -> megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitor -> proerythroblast -> basophilic erythroblast -> polychromatophilic erythroblast -> orthochromatic erythroblast -> reticulocyte -> mature erythrocyte`

### Stages of differentiation

1. **Proerythroblast:** large cell with a large nucleus and deeply basophilic cytoplasm.
2. **Basophilic erythroblast:** active ribosomal haemoglobin synthesis; cytoplasm remains blue.
3. **Polychromatophilic erythroblast:** increasing haemoglobin makes the cytoplasm grey-blue.
4. **Orthochromatic erythroblast (normoblast):** cytoplasm becomes pink; the condensed nucleus is expelled.
5. **Reticulocyte:** anucleate cell containing residual ribosomal RNA. It enters blood and becomes a mature RBC in about 1–2 days.
6. **Mature erythrocyte:** survives about 120 days before being removed mainly by macrophages in spleen, liver, and bone marrow.

### Regulation and factors affecting erythropoiesis

Low tissue oxygen, detected especially by the kidney, stimulates renal peritubular cells to release **erythropoietin (EPO)**. EPO acts on erythroid progenitors, increasing survival, proliferation, and differentiation. More RBCs increase oxygen delivery and reduce the EPO stimulus: this is a negative-feedback loop.

Important requirements include:

- iron for haem synthesis;
- vitamin B12 and folate for DNA synthesis and nuclear maturation;
- amino acids and vitamin B6 for globin and haem synthesis;
- healthy bone marrow, kidney function, endocrine support, and adequate protein;
- normal thyroid hormones, androgens, and growth hormone, which generally support erythropoiesis.

Erythropoiesis increases with chronic hypoxia, high altitude, anaemia, exercise, and some lung or heart diseases. It decreases with renal failure, marrow failure, chronic inflammation, severe malnutrition, iron/B12/folate deficiency, and endocrine disease. EPO is also used therapeutically in selected cases of renal anaemia.

### RBC count and physiological variation

Typical adult values (laboratories vary):

- adult male: about **4.5–6.0 million cells/microlitre**;
- adult female: about **4.0–5.5 million cells/microlitre**;
- newborn: higher than adult values, often 5–7 million/microlitre.

Counts are usually higher in males because of androgen effects and lower in females because of menstrual blood loss and lower average androgen levels. They rise at high altitude, after dehydration, and transiently with exercise or stress. They fall in pregnancy because plasma volume expands more than RBC mass (physiological anaemia of pregnancy), and in overhydration because of haemodilution.

## 3. Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin (Hb) is an iron-containing conjugated protein in RBCs. Each molecule has four globin chains, each associated with a haem group containing ferrous iron ($Fe^{2+}$). Each Hb molecule can reversibly bind up to four oxygen molecules.

### Functions

1. Transports most oxygen from lungs to tissues.
2. Carries part of carbon dioxide from tissues to lungs.
3. Buffers hydrogen ions and contributes to acid-base balance.
4. Helps regulate oxygen unloading through cooperative binding and the Bohr effect.

### Concentration

Approximate adult ranges are **13–17 g/dL in males** and **12–15 g/dL in females**. Values differ with age, altitude, pregnancy, hydration, and laboratory method. Hb concentration is a better measure of oxygen-carrying capacity than RBC count alone because cells may be unusually small, large, or poorly haemoglobinized.

### Types of haemoglobin

- **HbA ($\alpha_2\beta_2$):** principal adult haemoglobin, about 95–98%.
- **HbA2 ($\alpha_2\delta_2$):** about 1.5–3.5% in adults.
- **HbF ($\alpha_2\gamma_2$):** predominant in fetal life; has greater oxygen affinity and facilitates placental oxygen transfer. It normally becomes a small fraction after infancy.
- **Embryonic haemoglobins:** temporary forms during early embryonic development.
- Abnormal variants include HbS, HbC, and others caused by globin gene changes.

### Methods of Hb estimation

The principle is conversion of haemoglobin to a stable coloured compound and measurement by comparison or photometry.

- **Cyanmethemoglobin (Drabkin) method:** reliable reference method; Hb is converted to cyanmethemoglobin and read spectrophotometrically. Cyanide makes it unsuitable where safer methods are required.
- **Sodium lauryl sulphate method:** converts Hb to a stable coloured complex without cyanide; common in automated analysers.
- **Azidemethemoglobin method:** used in several portable haemoglobinometers.
- **Automated analysers:** lyse RBCs and measure Hb photometrically, while also providing RBC indices.
- **Copper sulphate screening:** estimates whether blood has a minimum density, mainly for donor screening; it is not a precise Hb measurement.

### Fate of haemoglobin

Old RBCs are phagocytosed by macrophages. Globin is broken into amino acids and reused. Iron is recovered from haem and transported by transferrin for storage as ferritin/haemosiderin or reuse in marrow. The porphyrin ring becomes biliverdin and then **unconjugated bilirubin**, which travels bound to albumin to the liver. Hepatocytes conjugate it with glucuronic acid; conjugated bilirubin enters bile and intestine, where bacterial products become urobilinogen. Most becomes stercobilin in faeces and a small fraction becomes urinary urobilin.

## 4. Jaundice and anaemia

### Jaundice

Jaundice is yellow discoloration caused by increased bilirubin in blood and tissues.

1. **Prehepatic or haemolytic jaundice:** excessive RBC destruction produces excess unconjugated bilirubin. Urine bilirubin is absent, but urine urobilinogen may increase.
2. **Hepatic jaundice:** damaged hepatocytes cannot adequately take up, conjugate, or excrete bilirubin. Both fractions may rise.
3. **Posthepatic or obstructive jaundice:** blocked bile flow causes conjugated bilirubin to reflux into blood. Urine is dark, stools may be pale, and fat absorption may be impaired.
4. **Neonatal jaundice:** immature conjugation mechanisms and increased RBC turnover may raise unconjugated bilirubin. Severe unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia can cause kernicterus.

### Anaemia

Anaemia is reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, usually due to reduced Hb, with or without a reduced RBC count. Symptoms result from tissue hypoxia and compensatory cardiovascular responses: fatigue, pallor, breathlessness, tachycardia, dizziness, and reduced exercise tolerance.

#### Classification by mechanism

- **Blood loss:** acute haemorrhage or chronic occult loss.
- **Reduced production:** iron deficiency, B12/folate deficiency, marrow failure, chronic kidney disease, chronic inflammation, or endocrine disease.
- **Increased destruction (haemolytic):** intrinsic RBC defects such as membrane, enzyme, or Hb disorders; or extrinsic immune, infectious, mechanical, or toxic causes.

#### Classification by cell size and colour

- **Microcytic hypochromic:** iron deficiency, thalassaemia, sideroblastic anaemia, some chronic disease.
- **Normocytic normochromic:** acute blood loss, haemolysis, renal disease, marrow failure, or chronic disease.
- **Macrocytic:** B12/folate deficiency, liver disease, alcohol use, hypothyroidism, drugs, or marrow disorders.

Reticulocyte count helps separate production failure (low response) from blood loss/haemolysis (appropriately high response).

## 5. ESR, PCV, osmotic fragility, and blood indices

### Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)

ESR is the distance RBCs fall in a vertical tube in one hour, expressed in mm/hour. Increased fibrinogen and other acute-phase proteins reduce the normal repulsive force between RBCs, causing rouleaux formation and faster sedimentation.

ESR rises in inflammation, infection, autoimmune disease, malignancy, pregnancy, anaemia, and older age. It is a nonspecific marker and can be normal in some acute illnesses. It decreases with polycythaemia, abnormal RBC shapes, and severe hypofibrinogenaemia. The Westergren method is commonly used.

### Packed cell volume (PCV/haematocrit)

PCV is the percentage of whole blood volume occupied by packed RBCs after centrifugation. Approximate adult values are **40–54% in males** and **36–47% in females**.

It increases in dehydration and polycythaemia and decreases in anaemia, overhydration, and pregnancy haemodilution. The microhaematocrit method uses a capillary tube; automated analysers calculate haematocrit from RBC count and mean cell volume.

### Osmotic fragility

Osmotic fragility tests RBC resistance to lysis in hypotonic saline. Water enters RBCs; spherical cells have less membrane reserve and lyse earlier. Increased fragility occurs classically in hereditary spherocytosis. Decreased fragility occurs in thalassaemia and some iron-deficiency states. The test is interpreted with smear, Hb, reticulocytes, and other investigations.

### RBC indices

- **MCV:** average RBC volume. $MCV(fL)=\frac{PCV(\%)\times 10}{RBC(millions/\mu L)}$. Normal adult value: about 80–100 fL.
- **MCH:** average Hb per RBC. $MCH(pg)=\frac{Hb(g/dL)\times 10}{RBC(millions/\mu L)}$. About 27–33 pg.
- **MCHC:** Hb concentration in packed RBCs. $MCHC(g/dL)=\frac{Hb(g/dL)\times 100}{PCV(\%)}$. About 32–36 g/dL.
- **RDW:** variation in RBC size (anisocytosis); often increased in iron deficiency.

These indices connect laboratory findings to mechanism: low MCV suggests defective haemoglobin synthesis, while high MCV suggests impaired DNA synthesis or altered membrane lipid composition.

## 6. White blood cells and immunity

### Types, morphology, and functions

Normal adult total WBC count is approximately **4,000–11,000 cells/microlitre**.

| Cell | Adult differential count | Main morphology and function |
|---|---:|---|
| Neutrophils | 40–75% | Multilobed nucleus; rapid phagocytosis of bacteria and debris; first responders in acute inflammation |
| Lymphocytes | 20–40% | Large round nucleus; adaptive immunity, immune memory, antibodies, and cytotoxicity |
| Monocytes | 2–10% | Large cells with kidney-shaped nucleus; enter tissues and become macrophages or dendritic cells |
| Eosinophils | 1–6% | Bilobed nucleus and red-orange granules; parasites and allergic inflammation |
| Basophils | 0–1% | Dark granules containing histamine and heparin; immediate hypersensitivity and inflammatory signalling |

### Production and movement

WBCs arise from haematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow. Granulocytes mature in marrow and form a circulating pool plus a marginated pool along vessel walls. During inflammation, selectins slow leukocytes, integrins produce firm adhesion, and chemokines guide diapedesis and chemotaxis into tissue.

Neutrophils phagocytose, form a phagosome, fuse it with lysosomes, and kill organisms using enzymes and reactive oxygen species. Monocytes/macrophages phagocytose and present antigen. Lymphocytes coordinate specific immunity.

### Variation in WBC count

- **Leukocytosis:** infection, inflammation, stress, exercise, corticosteroids, tissue injury, and some leukaemias.
- **Leukopenia:** marrow suppression, severe infection, drugs, autoimmune disease, or viral illness.
- **Neutrophilia:** bacterial infection, acute inflammation, stress, and steroids.
- **Neutropenia:** reduced production, drug toxicity, overwhelming infection, or immune destruction.
- **Eosinophilia:** allergy, asthma, and parasitic infection.
- **Lymphocytosis:** many viral infections and some lymphoid disorders.

Age matters: children normally have a higher lymphocyte proportion than adults, and the differential changes during infancy.

### Immunity in brief

**Innate immunity** is rapid and nonspecific: skin, mucosa, complement, phagocytes, natural killer cells, interferons, fever, and inflammation. **Adaptive immunity** is specific and develops memory.

- **Humoral immunity:** B lymphocytes become plasma cells and produce antibodies. Antibodies neutralize toxins/viruses, opsonize organisms, activate complement, and support agglutination.
- **Cell-mediated immunity:** T-helper cells coordinate responses; cytotoxic T cells kill infected or abnormal cells; regulatory T cells limit excessive responses.
- **Primary response** is slower and smaller; **secondary response** is faster and stronger because of memory cells.

## 7. Platelets and bleeding disorders

### Origin and morphology

Platelets are anucleate cytoplasmic fragments formed from megakaryocytes in red bone marrow. Thrombopoietin, made mainly by the liver and kidney, regulates megakaryocyte production. Platelets are small, discoid cells about 2–4 micrometres in diameter and survive approximately 7–10 days.

They contain alpha granules (fibrinogen, von Willebrand factor, platelet-derived growth factor) and dense granules (ADP, ATP, calcium, and serotonin).

Normal platelet count is about **150,000–400,000/microlitre**.

### Functions and platelet plug

1. Vessel injury exposes collagen and tissue factor.
2. von Willebrand factor helps platelets adhere through platelet glycoprotein Ib.
3. Adhesion activates platelets: they change shape and release ADP, thromboxane A2, and serotonin.
4. ADP and thromboxane recruit and activate additional platelets.
5. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa binds fibrinogen, linking platelets into a temporary **primary platelet plug**.
6. Coagulation generates fibrin, which reinforces the plug into a stable clot.

**Thrombocytopenia** causes petechiae, mucosal bleeding, and prolonged bleeding time. **Thrombocytosis** may increase thrombosis risk or occur reactively. Platelet function defects can cause bleeding even when the count is normal, for example with aspirin use or inherited receptor disorders.

## 8. Haemostasis and coagulation

### Definition and normal sequence

Haemostasis is the controlled process that stops bleeding while keeping blood fluid elsewhere.

1. **Vasoconstriction:** immediate neural, myogenic, and local mediator responses reduce blood flow.
2. **Primary haemostasis:** platelet adhesion, activation, and aggregation form a temporary plug.
3. **Secondary haemostasis:** coagulation creates thrombin and fibrin, stabilizing the plug.
4. **Clot retraction and repair:** platelets contract through actin-myosin and release growth factors.
5. **Fibrinolysis:** plasmin digests fibrin after vessel repair.

### Clotting factors

| Factor | Name or description |
|---|---|
| I | Fibrinogen |
| II | Prothrombin |
| III | Tissue factor |
| IV | Calcium ions |
| V | Proaccelerin (labile factor) |
| VII | Proconvertin (stable factor) |
| VIII | Anti-haemophilic factor A |
| IX | Christmas factor; anti-haemophilic factor B |
| X | Stuart-Prower factor |
| XI | Plasma thromboplastin antecedent |
| XII | Hageman factor |
| XIII | Fibrin-stabilizing factor |

Factors II, VII, IX, and X, and proteins C and S, require vitamin K for synthesis. The liver synthesizes most clotting factors.

### Mechanism of clotting

- **Extrinsic pathway:** tissue factor from damaged tissue combines with factor VII and calcium to activate factor X. It is assessed mainly by **prothrombin time (PT/INR)**.
- **Intrinsic pathway:** factors XII, XI, IX, and VIII activate factor X on a phospholipid surface. It is assessed mainly by **activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)**.
- **Common pathway:** Xa with Va, calcium, and phospholipid converts prothrombin (II) to thrombin. Thrombin converts fibrinogen (I) to fibrin and activates XIII, which cross-links fibrin.

The pathways are laboratory models; in vivo coagulation is initiated largely by tissue factor and amplified on activated platelet surfaces.

### Anticoagulants and natural control

- **Antithrombin** inactivates thrombin and factors IXa, Xa, XIa, and XIIa; heparin greatly accelerates this action.
- **Protein C with protein S** inactivates Va and VIIIa.
- **Tissue factor pathway inhibitor** limits tissue-factor signalling.
- Healthy endothelium releases nitric oxide and prostacyclin and supports fibrinolysis.
- **Plasmin**, generated from plasminogen by tissue plasminogen activator, breaks down fibrin. D-dimer is a product of cross-linked fibrin breakdown.

Clinical anticoagulants include heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin, warfarin, and direct oral anticoagulants. They reduce thrombosis but increase bleeding risk.

### Coagulation disorders

- **Haemophilia A:** factor VIII deficiency; prolonged aPTT, deep tissue and joint bleeding.
- **Haemophilia B:** factor IX deficiency; similar pattern.
- **von Willebrand disease:** defective platelet adhesion and reduced factor VIII stability; mucosal bleeding and often prolonged bleeding time.
- **Vitamin K deficiency or warfarin effect:** reduced II, VII, IX, and X; PT rises early.
- **Liver disease:** reduced production of multiple clotting factors.
- **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC):** widespread activation consumes platelets and factors, causing both thrombosis and bleeding.
- **Thrombocytopenia/platelet disease:** primarily causes mucocutaneous bleeding, unlike the deep bleeding typical of coagulation-factor deficiency.

## 9. Blood groups and transfusion

### ABO system

ABO antigens are carbohydrate structures on RBC membranes. Plasma naturally contains antibodies against A or B antigens that a person lacks.

| Group | RBC antigen | Plasma antibody | Can receive RBCs from |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | A | Anti-B | A, O |
| B | B | Anti-A | B, O |
| AB | A and B | Neither anti-A nor anti-B | A, B, AB, O |
| O | Neither A nor B | Anti-A and anti-B | O |

For red-cell transfusion, O negative is often called the emergency universal donor and AB positive the broadest recipient type, but properly typed and cross-matched blood is always preferred. Plasma compatibility follows the opposite logic because plasma contains antibodies.

### Rh system and incompatibility

The most important Rh antigen is D. Rh-positive people possess D; Rh-negative people do not. Anti-D is not naturally present. An Rh-negative person may become sensitized after exposure to Rh-positive RBCs through transfusion or pregnancy.

In a later pregnancy, maternal IgG anti-D can cross the placenta and destroy Rh-positive fetal RBCs, causing haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn. Anti-D immunoglobulin given to an eligible Rh-negative mother prevents sensitization by clearing fetal Rh-positive cells.

### Blood typing and cross-matching

- **Forward typing:** patient RBCs are mixed with anti-A, anti-B, and anti-D reagents; agglutination identifies antigens.
- **Reverse typing:** patient plasma is mixed with known A and B cells; agglutination identifies antibodies.
- **Cross-match:** donor RBCs are mixed with recipient plasma to detect clinically important incompatibility before transfusion.
- Identity checks, antibody screening, compatibility testing, and observation during transfusion are essential safety steps.

### Hazards of mismatched transfusion

ABO-incompatible blood can cause rapid complement-mediated intravascular haemolysis: fever, chills, pain, hypotension, haemoglobinuria, acute kidney injury, DIC, shock, and death. Other hazards include delayed haemolytic reactions, febrile or allergic reactions, TRALI, transfusion-associated circulatory overload, infection, iron overload, and alloimmunization. A suspected reaction requires stopping the transfusion, maintaining an appropriate IV line, checking identification, and urgent clinical assessment.

## 10. Reticuloendothelial system, spleen, and lymph

The **reticuloendothelial system**, now commonly called the mononuclear phagocyte system, includes monocytes and tissue macrophages in liver (Kupffer cells), spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow, lungs, and other tissues. It removes aged cells, particles, microbes, and immune complexes and presents antigens.

The **spleen** filters blood rather than lymph. Its red pulp removes old or abnormal RBCs and recycles iron. Its white pulp contains lymphoid tissue for immune responses to blood-borne antigens. The spleen also stores some platelets and has important fetal haematopoietic functions. Splenectomy increases susceptibility to encapsulated bacteria and can cause persistent abnormal RBC forms.

**Lymph** is interstitial fluid returned to blood through lymphatic vessels. Lymphatics return excess fluid and plasma proteins, transport absorbed dietary fat through lacteals, and carry antigens and lymphocytes to lymph nodes. Lymph nodes filter lymph and provide sites for antigen presentation, lymphocyte activation, and antibody production.

## 11. How the blood topics are correlated

The topics are parts of one physiological system rather than isolated facts:

1. **Kidney, marrow, RBC, and lungs:** hypoxia stimulates EPO; marrow makes RBCs; Hb carries oxygen; lung oxygenation and tissue oxygen use complete the feedback loop.
2. **Hb, bilirubin, liver, and jaundice:** RBC breakdown supplies bilirubin; the liver conjugates and excretes it; failure at different points produces different types of jaundice.
3. **Plasma proteins and oedema:** albumin retains water inside capillaries; reduced albumin lowers oncotic pressure and produces oedema.
4. **Inflammation and ESR:** cytokines stimulate acute-phase protein production; fibrinogen promotes rouleaux and increases ESR.
5. **Platelets and coagulation:** platelets create the primary plug; clotting factors generate fibrin to stabilize it; anticoagulants and fibrinolysis prevent excessive clotting.
6. **Immunity and blood groups:** RBC antigens can be recognized as foreign; antibodies cause agglutination and haemolysis during incompatible transfusion.
7. **Spleen and blood cell quality:** splenic macrophages remove old RBCs, damaged platelets, and blood-borne microbes.

# 12. Nerve-Muscle Physiology

## Neuron structure

A neuron is an excitable cell specialized to receive, integrate, and transmit information.

- **Cell body (soma):** contains nucleus, metabolic machinery, Nissl substance, and organelles.
- **Dendrites:** receive signals; usually conduct graded potentials toward the soma.
- **Axon hillock and initial segment:** integrate inputs and usually initiate the action potential when threshold is reached.
- **Axon:** conducts action potentials away from the soma.
- **Myelin sheath:** insulating lipid layer produced by oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the PNS.
- **Nodes of Ranvier:** gaps in myelin with many voltage-gated sodium channels; action potentials jump between nodes.
- **Axon terminals:** release neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft.

Neurons maintain a resting membrane potential, commonly around -70 mV, because of selective permeability, the Na+/K+ pump, and unequal ion distribution. Stimulation opens ion channels. If threshold is reached, a rapid depolarization followed by repolarization produces an all-or-none action potential.

## Types of neurons

By function:

- **Sensory/afferent neurons:** carry information from receptors to the CNS.
- **Motor/efferent neurons:** carry commands from CNS to muscles or glands.
- **Interneurons:** connect neurons within the CNS and perform integration.

By structure:

- **Multipolar:** one axon and many dendrites; most motor neurons and CNS neurons.
- **Bipolar:** one dendrite and one axon; retina and some sensory pathways.
- **Pseudounipolar:** one process divides into peripheral and central branches; dorsal-root ganglion sensory neurons.

## Neuroglia

Neuroglia support neurons but do not usually conduct conventional action potentials.

### CNS glia

- **Astrocytes:** maintain the extracellular environment, regulate potassium and neurotransmitters, support the blood-brain barrier, and participate in repair.
- **Oligodendrocytes:** myelinate several CNS axons.
- **Microglia:** resident immune/phagocytic cells of the CNS.
- **Ependymal cells:** line ventricles and help produce/circulate cerebrospinal fluid.

### PNS glia

- **Schwann cells:** myelinate one segment of one peripheral axon and support regeneration.
- **Satellite cells:** surround neuron cell bodies in ganglia and regulate their local chemical environment.

## Nerve-fibre classification

### Erlanger and Gasser classification

- **A-alpha:** largest and fastest; skeletal muscle motor fibres and proprioception.
- **A-beta:** touch, pressure, and vibration.
- **A-gamma:** motor supply to muscle spindles.
- **A-delta:** fast, sharp pain and cold.
- **B fibres:** small myelinated autonomic preganglionic fibres.
- **C fibres:** unmyelinated, slow; dull pain, warmth, itch, and postganglionic sympathetic fibres.

Conduction speed rises with fibre diameter and myelination. Myelin reduces membrane capacitance and increases membrane resistance, allowing saltatory conduction from node to node. Local anaesthetics preferentially block small, rapidly firing pain fibres, although block order depends on fibre type, dose, and location.

### Lloyd-Hunt sensory classification

Group I fibres are the largest proprioceptive fibres, followed by Group II for touch/pressure, Group III for fast pain and temperature, and Group IV for slow pain and other visceral sensations. Group I is subdivided into Ia and Ib for muscle spindle and Golgi tendon organ afferents.

## Basic nerve-muscle relationship

At the neuromuscular junction, a motor action potential opens presynaptic calcium channels. Calcium triggers acetylcholine release; acetylcholine binds nicotinic receptors on the motor end plate, producing an end-plate potential. If threshold is reached, a muscle action potential travels along the sarcolemma and T-tubules. It causes sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium release; calcium binds troponin C, moves tropomyosin, and allows actin-myosin cross-bridge cycling. ATP powers cycling and calcium reuptake, producing relaxation.

This explains the major correlation with blood physiology:

- Hb and circulation deliver oxygen and glucose needed for ATP production.
- Plasma electrolytes, especially calcium, potassium, and sodium, determine nerve and muscle excitability.
- Calcium is both a clotting factor and a signal in neurotransmitter release and contraction.
- Severe anaemia reduces oxygen delivery and causes fatigue, dyspnoea, and reduced muscle performance.
- Abnormal potassium or calcium can disturb membrane potentials, producing weakness, cramps, tetany, arrhythmias, or paralysis.
- Platelets and haemostasis prevent bleeding around nerves and muscles, while excessive clotting can reduce their blood supply.
- Inflammation and immune responses can damage peripheral nerves or neuromuscular transmission.

### High-yield summary

Blood maintains the environment in which nerves and muscles function. RBCs and Hb provide oxygen, plasma provides electrolytes and proteins, WBCs defend tissues, and platelets/coagulation prevent blood loss. Nerve activity depends on ion gradients and myelin; muscle contraction depends on neural acetylcholine, calcium, ATP, and intact circulation. A disturbance in one part therefore produces effects across the whole system.
