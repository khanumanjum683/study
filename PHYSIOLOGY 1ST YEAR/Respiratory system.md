# Respiratory System Physiology

Respiration is the process by which the body obtains oxygen and removes carbon dioxide. It includes **ventilation** (movement of air), **external respiration** (exchange between alveoli and blood), **gas transport** in blood, **internal respiration** (exchange between blood and tissues), and **cellular respiration** (use of oxygen to produce ATP).

The functional sequence is:

`atmosphere -> conducting airways -> alveoli -> respiratory membrane -> pulmonary capillary blood -> systemic blood flow -> tissues`

## 1. Physiological anatomy of the respiratory system

### Conducting portion

The conducting portion moves, warms, humidifies, and filters air but does not normally exchange gases.

1. Nose and nasal cavity
2. Pharynx: nasopharynx, oropharynx, and laryngopharynx
3. Larynx
4. Trachea
5. Main bronchi
6. Lobar and segmental bronchi
7. Bronchioles
8. Terminal bronchioles

The nose filters particles with hairs and mucus. Ciliated epithelium moves mucus toward the pharynx, where it is swallowed or coughed out. The larynx protects the lower airway and participates in phonation. Cartilage supports the trachea and bronchi; bronchioles lack cartilage and regulate airflow mainly through smooth muscle.

### Respiratory portion

The respiratory portion begins at respiratory bronchioles and includes alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs, and alveoli. Alveoli provide a very large surface area and are surrounded by dense pulmonary capillary networks. Type I pneumocytes form most of the thin gas-exchange surface. Type II pneumocytes secrete surfactant. Alveolar macrophages remove particles and microorganisms.

### Lungs and pleura

The right lung has three lobes and the left lung has two lobes plus the lingula. Each lung is covered by visceral pleura. The thoracic wall is lined by parietal pleura. Between them is a thin pleural cavity containing a small amount of lubricating fluid.

The lungs have elastic tissue and a tendency to recoil inward. The chest wall tends to recoil outward at normal resting volumes. The opposing forces create a subatmospheric **intrapleural pressure**, which keeps the lungs expanded.

### Blood supply

The pulmonary circulation carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to alveolar capillaries for gas exchange and returns oxygenated blood to the left atrium. The bronchial circulation supplies oxygenated blood to airway tissues. Some bronchial venous blood enters pulmonary veins, producing a normal physiological shunt.

## 2. Muscles of respiration

### Inspiration

- **Diaphragm:** the principal muscle. Its contraction flattens and descends, increasing the vertical diameter of the thorax.
- **External intercostals:** elevate the ribs and increase anteroposterior and transverse diameters.
- **Accessory muscles:** sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, and upper chest muscles assist during deep or laboured inspiration.

### Quiet expiration

Quiet expiration is mainly passive. Inspiratory muscles relax, and elastic recoil of lungs and chest wall reduces thoracic volume.

### Forced expiration

- **Internal intercostals:** depress the ribs.
- **Abdominal muscles:** increase intra-abdominal pressure and push the diaphragm upward.

The phrenic nerves (C3–C5) supply the diaphragm. Intercostal nerves supply the intercostal muscles. Damage to both phrenic nerves or severe diaphragm weakness can cause ventilatory failure.

## 3. Respiratory and non-respiratory functions of lungs

### Respiratory functions

- Ventilation of alveoli
- Diffusion of oxygen into blood and carbon dioxide out of blood
- Regulation of blood pH through carbon dioxide removal
- Conditioning of inspired air
- Contribution to voice through airflow across the larynx

### Non-respiratory functions

- **Blood reservoir:** pulmonary vessels can store a significant volume of blood.
- **Filter:** pulmonary capillaries trap small thrombi, air bubbles, and particulate matter.
- **Metabolic and endocrine activity:** pulmonary endothelium converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II using ACE, inactivates bradykinin and some hormones, and produces signalling molecules.
- **Defence:** mucus, cilia, cough, alveolar macrophages, antibodies, and immune cells protect the lower airway.
- **Heat and water exchange:** expired air removes heat and water.
- **Acid-base control:** changes in alveolar ventilation rapidly alter arterial carbon dioxide and pH.

## 4. Dead space

Dead space is air that does not participate effectively in gas exchange.

- **Anatomical dead space:** volume in the conducting airways, approximately 150 mL in a typical adult.
- **Alveolar dead space:** air reaching alveoli that are poorly perfused or not perfused.
- **Physiological dead space:** anatomical plus alveolar dead space. In healthy adults it is close to anatomical dead space.

Minute ventilation is:

$$\dot V_E = V_T \times f$$

Alveolar ventilation is:

$$\dot V_A = (V_T - V_D) \times f$$

where $V_T$ is tidal volume, $V_D$ is dead-space volume, and $f$ is respiratory frequency. Rapid shallow breathing may produce a reasonable minute ventilation but poor alveolar ventilation because a greater fraction of each breath occupies dead space. Alveolar ventilation is inversely related to arterial $P_{CO_2}$ when carbon dioxide production is constant.

## 5. Mechanics of breathing

Air moves when a pressure gradient exists between atmosphere and alveoli.

### Inspiration

1. Diaphragm and external intercostals contract.
2. Thoracic volume increases.
3. Intrapleural pressure becomes more negative.
4. Transpulmonary pressure increases and expands the lungs.
5. Alveolar pressure falls slightly below atmospheric pressure.
6. Air flows into the lungs until alveolar and atmospheric pressures equalize.

### Expiration

1. Inspiratory muscles relax.
2. Elastic recoil reduces lung volume.
3. Alveolar pressure rises slightly above atmospheric pressure.
4. Air flows out until pressures equalize.

### Intrapulmonary and intrapleural pressures

**Intrapulmonary (alveolar) pressure** is pressure within alveoli. It fluctuates around atmospheric pressure during airflow. **Intrapleural pressure** is pressure in the pleural space and is normally negative relative to atmosphere. **Transpulmonary pressure** is:

$$P_{TP}=P_{A}-P_{pl}$$

where $P_A$ is alveolar pressure and $P_{pl}$ is pleural pressure. It is the distending pressure that keeps alveoli open.

At the end of quiet expiration, alveolar pressure is approximately atmospheric, while intrapleural pressure remains negative. If air enters the pleural cavity, as in pneumothorax, pleural pressure approaches atmospheric, transpulmonary pressure falls, and the lung recoils inward.

### Airway resistance

Airway resistance depends strongly on airway radius. According to the relationship for laminar flow, resistance rises markedly as radius decreases. Bronchoconstriction, mucus, oedema, and foreign bodies increase resistance. Bronchodilation, sympathetic beta-2 stimulation, and reduced mucus decrease it. Small bronchioles are especially affected because they have little cartilage support.

## 6. Compliance and surfactant

**Compliance** is the change in lung volume per unit change in distending pressure:

$$C=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta P}$$

High compliance means the lungs inflate easily but may recoil poorly. Low compliance means greater work is required to expand the lungs.

Compliance is reduced by pulmonary fibrosis, oedema, pneumonia, and inadequate surfactant. It is increased in emphysema because elastic tissue is destroyed.

### Surfactant

Pulmonary surfactant is a phospholipid-protein mixture secreted by type II pneumocytes. Its major phospholipid is dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine. Surfactant reduces surface tension at the air-fluid interface inside alveoli.

Without surfactant, surface tension promotes alveolar collapse, especially in small alveoli. The relationship is described by Laplace's law:

$$P=\frac{2T}{r}$$

where $P$ is collapsing pressure, $T$ is surface tension, and $r$ is alveolar radius. Surfactant lowers $T$ more effectively in small alveoli, helping stabilize alveoli of different sizes. It also increases compliance, reduces the work of breathing, and limits fluid movement into alveoli.

### Hyaline membrane disease

Hyaline membrane disease, or neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, results mainly from inadequate surfactant in premature infants. Alveoli collapse, lung compliance falls, work of breathing rises, and gas exchange becomes impaired. Hypoxaemia and acidosis worsen pulmonary vasoconstriction and injury. Protein-rich fluid and damaged epithelial material form hyaline membranes along alveolar walls.

Prevention and treatment include antenatal corticosteroids when premature delivery is expected, exogenous surfactant, continuous positive airway pressure, and carefully controlled oxygen and ventilation.

## 7. Lung volumes and capacities

Values vary with age, sex, body size, posture, and fitness.

| Measurement | Meaning | Approximate adult value |
|---|---|---:|
| Tidal volume (TV) | Air inspired or expired in quiet breathing | 500 mL |
| Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) | Extra air inspired after a normal inspiration | 2,500–3,000 mL |
| Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) | Extra air expired after a normal expiration | 1,000–1,200 mL |
| Residual volume (RV) | Air remaining after maximal expiration | 1,200 mL |
| Inspiratory capacity (IC) | TV + IRV | 3,000–3,500 mL |
| Functional residual capacity (FRC) | ERV + RV; volume after quiet expiration | 2,200–2,400 mL |
| Vital capacity (VC) | IRV + TV + ERV | 4,500–5,000 mL |
| Total lung capacity (TLC) | VC + RV | 5,700–6,000 mL |

Spirometry measures most volumes but cannot directly measure RV, FRC, or TLC because these contain residual volume. These require gas dilution or body plethysmography.

In obstructive disease, airflow is limited and RV/FRC may increase due to air trapping. In restrictive disease, TLC, VC, and other volumes decrease because the lungs or chest wall cannot expand normally.

## 8. Respiratory membrane and gas exchange

The respiratory membrane separates alveolar gas from pulmonary capillary blood. From air to blood it includes:

1. Alveolar fluid and surfactant layer
2. Type I alveolar epithelial cell
3. Epithelial basement membrane
4. Thin interstitial space
5. Capillary basement membrane
6. Capillary endothelial cell

Its very large surface area, thin barrier, and close capillary contact permit rapid diffusion. Gas transfer follows Fick's law: it increases with surface area and partial-pressure gradient and decreases with membrane thickness and gas distance.

### Partial pressures

Dry atmospheric air contains approximately 21% oxygen and 0.04% carbon dioxide. In the humidified conducting airway, water vapour reduces the partial pressure available to other gases. Typical alveolar values are approximately $P_{O_2}=100$ mmHg and $P_{CO_2}=40$ mmHg. Mixed venous blood arriving at pulmonary capillaries has approximately $P_{O_2}=40$ mmHg and $P_{CO_2}=46$ mmHg.

Oxygen therefore diffuses from alveoli to blood, while carbon dioxide diffuses from blood to alveoli. Carbon dioxide is more soluble than oxygen and diffuses readily despite a smaller pressure gradient.

### Ventilation-perfusion relationship

The average ventilation-perfusion ratio ($V/Q$) is about 0.8. A low $V/Q$ region is well perfused but poorly ventilated and produces hypoxaemia. A high $V/Q$ region is ventilated but poorly perfused and behaves like increased dead space. Pulmonary vasoconstriction in response to low alveolar oxygen diverts blood away from poorly ventilated alveoli.

## 9. Transport of oxygen

About 98–99% of oxygen is transported bound reversibly to haemoglobin in RBCs; 1–2% is dissolved in plasma. Each haemoglobin molecule can bind four oxygen molecules.

The oxygen-haemoglobin dissociation curve is sigmoid because haemoglobin binding is cooperative. Important shifts include:

- **Right shift:** reduced affinity and greater tissue unloading with increased $P_{CO_2}$, increased hydrogen ions, increased temperature, and increased 2,3-BPG. This is the Bohr effect.
- **Left shift:** increased affinity with reduced temperature, reduced $P_{CO_2}$, reduced hydrogen ions, reduced 2,3-BPG, fetal haemoglobin, and carbon monoxide exposure.

Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin with very high affinity, reduces available oxygen-binding sites, and shifts the remaining sites leftward. A patient may have severe tissue hypoxia despite a deceptively normal $P_{O_2}$.

Oxygen content depends mainly on haemoglobin concentration and saturation:

$$C_{aO_2}\approx 1.34(Hb)(S_{aO_2})+0.003(P_{aO_2})$$

Thus, severe anaemia can cause low oxygen content even when arterial $P_{O_2}$ is normal.

## 10. Transport of carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide is transported in three forms:

- about 7–10% dissolved in plasma;
- about 20–30% as carbaminohaemoglobin, mainly bound to globin;
- about 60–70% as bicarbonate ($HCO_3^-$).

Inside RBCs, carbonic anhydrase catalyses:

$$CO_2+H_2O\rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3\rightleftharpoons H^++HCO_3^-$$

Bicarbonate leaves the RBC in exchange for chloride, called the chloride shift. In the lungs the reaction reverses, bicarbonate re-enters RBCs, and carbon dioxide is exhaled.

Deoxygenated haemoglobin binds hydrogen ions and carbon dioxide more readily than oxygenated haemoglobin. This is the **Haldane effect**: oxygenation in the lungs promotes carbon dioxide unloading.

## 11. Chemical regulation of respiration

The principal chemical stimulus for ventilation is arterial carbon dioxide through its effect on hydrogen ion concentration in brain extracellular fluid.

### Central chemoreceptors

Central chemoreceptors near the ventrolateral medulla respond mainly to the fall in cerebrospinal-fluid pH produced when CO2 crosses the blood-brain barrier and forms hydrogen ions. They provide a powerful response to hypercapnia.

### Peripheral chemoreceptors

Carotid bodies, supplied by glossopharyngeal nerves, and aortic bodies, supplied by vagal nerves, respond to:

- low arterial $P_{O_2}$, especially below about 60 mmHg;
- increased $P_{CO_2}$;
- increased arterial hydrogen ion concentration.

They respond rapidly and are especially important during hypoxaemia and metabolic acidosis. Increased ventilation lowers $P_{CO_2}$ and raises pH, forming a negative-feedback system.

## 12. Neural regulation of respiration

Respiratory rhythm is generated in networks in the medulla and modified by pontine centres.

- **Dorsal respiratory group:** mainly inspiratory activity and integration of sensory input.
- **Ventral respiratory group:** inspiratory and expiratory neurons; recruited during forced breathing.
- **Pre-Botzinger complex:** important rhythm-generating network.
- **Pontine respiratory groups:** shape the transition between inspiration and expiration.

Higher centres modify breathing during speech, singing, emotion, exercise, and voluntary control. The cerebral cortex can temporarily control breathing, but rising carbon dioxide eventually overrides voluntary breath-holding.

Pulmonary stretch receptors participate in the Hering-Breuer inflation reflex. Irritant receptors cause cough and bronchoconstriction. J receptors near pulmonary capillaries respond to congestion or interstitial fluid and may produce rapid shallow breathing.

## 13. Hypoxia

Hypoxia is inadequate oxygen availability at the tissue level. **Hypoxaemia** specifically means low oxygen in arterial blood.

### Types of hypoxia

1. **Hypoxic hypoxia:** low arterial $P_{O_2}$, as in high altitude, hypoventilation, or lung disease.
2. **Anaemic hypoxia:** reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, as in anaemia or carbon monoxide poisoning.
3. **Stagnant or circulatory hypoxia:** inadequate blood flow, as in shock or local vascular obstruction.
4. **Histotoxic hypoxia:** tissues cannot use oxygen, classically with cyanide poisoning.

Effects include tachycardia, increased ventilation, restlessness, impaired judgement, cyanosis in some cases, loss of consciousness, and cellular injury. Prolonged hypoxia causes pulmonary vasoconstriction, pulmonary hypertension, metabolic changes, and organ dysfunction.

## 14. Acclimatization to high altitude

At altitude, barometric pressure falls, so inspired and alveolar oxygen partial pressure falls. The body adapts over hours to weeks:

- peripheral chemoreceptors increase ventilation;
- respiratory alkalosis develops initially; the kidneys excrete bicarbonate, allowing ventilation to rise further;
- renal EPO increases, raising RBC count and haemoglobin;
- 2,3-BPG increases, improving tissue oxygen unloading;
- cardiac output initially rises, then may fall toward normal as adaptation develops;
- pulmonary and systemic vascular changes alter perfusion;
- tissues increase capillary density, mitochondria, and oxygen extraction over time.

Excessive pulmonary vasoconstriction may cause high-altitude pulmonary oedema. Severe cerebral vasodilation and swelling may cause high-altitude cerebral oedema.

## 15. Dysbarism

Dysbarism refers to disorders caused by changes in ambient pressure.

### Decompression sickness

During rapid ascent after deep diving, dissolved nitrogen forms bubbles in blood and tissues. It can cause joint pain, skin symptoms, neurological deficits, respiratory distress, or circulatory obstruction. Prevention involves controlled ascent and decompression stops. Treatment is recompression with high-concentration oxygen.

### Arterial gas embolism

Gas entering pulmonary veins or systemic arteries can obstruct cerebral or coronary circulation. It may occur with lung overexpansion during ascent or medical procedures.

### Barotrauma

Pressure differences can injure the ears, sinuses, lungs, or other air-containing spaces. Breath-holding during ascent is dangerous because expanding gas can rupture alveoli.

### Nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity

High nitrogen partial pressure at depth can impair judgement and coordination. Prolonged exposure to high oxygen partial pressure can damage lungs or cause CNS toxicity and seizures.

## 16. Artificial respiration

Artificial respiration is assisted ventilation when spontaneous breathing is absent or inadequate. The immediate priorities are safety, response, airway, breathing, and circulation.

Basic principles include opening the airway, assessing normal breathing, calling emergency services, beginning CPR when indicated, and using rescue breaths or a barrier device if trained and willing. A bag-valve-mask or mechanical ventilator can provide positive-pressure ventilation.

Positive pressure pushes air into the lungs, unlike normal inspiration, which creates negative intrathoracic pressure. Excessive pressure can cause barotrauma and reduce venous return and cardiac output. Oxygen concentration, tidal volume, respiratory rate, airway pressure, and end-expiratory pressure must be controlled clinically.

Artificial ventilation supports gas exchange but does not correct the underlying cause. Airway obstruction, opioid toxicity, neuromuscular failure, lung disease, and metabolic disorders require different treatments.

## 17. Important respiratory definitions

### Periodic breathing

Periodic breathing is a repeating pattern of alternating deeper breathing and reduced or absent breathing. Cheyne-Stokes respiration has crescendo-decrescendo ventilation followed by apnoea. It may occur in heart failure, neurological disease, sleep, or at altitude because of delayed feedback between blood gases and respiratory control.

### Dyspnoea

Dyspnoea is the subjective sensation of difficult, uncomfortable, or laboured breathing. It can result from airway obstruction, low lung compliance, respiratory muscle overload, hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, cardiac disease, anxiety, or abnormal respiratory drive.

### Apnoea

Apnoea is cessation of airflow for a clinically significant period. It may be central, due to absent respiratory drive, or obstructive, due to airway collapse despite respiratory effort. Mixed apnoea contains both mechanisms.

### Asphyxia

Asphyxia is severe impairment of oxygen delivery and carbon dioxide removal due to inadequate ventilation, airway obstruction, drowning, strangulation, toxic gases, or other causes. It can produce hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, acidosis, loss of consciousness, and death.

### Cyanosis

Cyanosis is bluish discoloration of skin or mucous membranes caused by increased deoxygenated haemoglobin or abnormal haemoglobin. Central cyanosis affects lips and tongue and usually reflects arterial desaturation. Peripheral cyanosis may result from slow flow and increased local oxygen extraction. Cyanosis may be difficult to detect in severe anaemia because there is less haemoglobin available to become visibly deoxygenated.

## 18. How the respiratory topics are correlated

1. **Mechanics determines ventilation:** respiratory muscles change thoracic volume; pressure changes then move air into and out of alveoli.
2. **Compliance and surfactant determine work:** low compliance or absent surfactant requires greater inspiratory effort and can cause respiratory muscle fatigue.
3. **Dead space determines effective ventilation:** only fresh air reaching perfused alveoli contributes substantially to gas exchange; rapid shallow breathing is therefore inefficient.
4. **The respiratory membrane determines diffusion:** its thinness, surface area, and pressure gradients determine oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide removal.
5. **Ventilation and perfusion must match:** a ventilated but unperfused alveolus acts like dead space, while a perfused but poorly ventilated region acts like a shunt.
6. **Blood links lungs to tissues:** haemoglobin carries oxygen, bicarbonate carries most carbon dioxide, and circulation delivers the gases to cells.
7. **Carbon dioxide regulates ventilation:** increased tissue metabolism raises CO2, central and peripheral chemoreceptors increase ventilation, and exhalation restores pH.
8. **Oxygen has a weaker immediate ventilatory effect:** peripheral chemoreceptors respond strongly when arterial $P_{O_2}$ becomes markedly low, especially below approximately 60 mmHg.
9. **Altitude exposes every step:** reduced barometric pressure lowers alveolar oxygen, causing hyperventilation, alkalosis, EPO release, increased 2,3-BPG, and possible dysbaric illness.
10. **Disease patterns localize failure:** airway obstruction raises resistance; fibrosis lowers compliance; emphysema increases compliance but reduces recoil; anaemia lowers oxygen content despite normal $P_{O_2}$; neuromuscular disease prevents adequate ventilation.
11. **Symptoms reflect the chain:** dyspnoea may arise from increased work, abnormal blood gases, or respiratory drive; cyanosis indicates deoxygenated haemoglobin; apnoea interrupts ventilation; asphyxia ultimately injures every oxygen-dependent organ.
12. **Artificial respiration replaces the pump:** positive-pressure ventilation can restore alveolar ventilation, but oxygen transport, circulation, airway patency, and cellular oxygen use must also be preserved.

## High-yield summary

The respiratory system maintains oxygen delivery and acid-base balance. Respiratory muscles create pressure gradients, surfactant stabilizes alveoli, and the respiratory membrane permits diffusion. Oxygen is carried mainly by haemoglobin, while carbon dioxide is carried mainly as bicarbonate. Chemoreceptors and brainstem centres continuously adjust ventilation according to CO2, pH, oxygen, movement, and higher-brain activity. Respiratory failure can therefore arise from a problem with the pump, airways, alveoli, membrane, blood, circulation, or cellular oxygen use.
