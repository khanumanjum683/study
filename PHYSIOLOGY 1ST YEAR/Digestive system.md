 # Digestive System Physiology

Digestion is the mechanical and chemical processing of food into absorbable molecules. The gastrointestinal (GI) tract also functions as an endocrine, immune, excretory, and fluid-regulating organ.

`ingestion -> propulsion -> mechanical digestion -> secretion -> chemical digestion -> absorption -> storage and defecation`

## 1. Physiological anatomy of the GIT

The alimentary canal extends from mouth to anus and includes the oral cavity, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, and anal canal. Accessory organs are the salivary glands, pancreas, liver, and gall bladder.

### General wall structure

Most of the tract has four layers:

1. **Mucosa:** epithelium, lamina propria, and muscularis mucosae. It performs secretion, absorption, and immune defence.
2. **Submucosa:** connective tissue containing blood vessels, lymphatics, glands in some regions, and the submucosal (Meissner) plexus.
3. **Muscularis externa:** usually inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle with the myenteric (Auerbach) plexus between them.
4. **Serosa or adventitia:** outer covering. Serosa is visceral peritoneum; adventitia anchors retroperitoneal regions.

The mucosa contains cells specialized for protection, secretion, absorption, and hormone release. Tight junctions regulate paracellular movement, while folds, villi, and microvilli increase absorptive surface area.

### Regional specializations

- **Mouth and oesophagus:** ingestion, lubrication, swallowing, and transport.
- **Stomach:** storage, mixing, acid and enzyme secretion, and controlled emptying.
- **Small intestine:** most digestion and nutrient absorption; duodenum, jejunum, and ileum.
- **Large intestine:** water and electrolyte absorption, bacterial fermentation, faecal formation, and storage.
- **Liver and gall bladder:** bile formation, storage, concentration, and metabolic processing.
- **Pancreas:** digestive enzyme and bicarbonate secretion.

Absorbed nutrients from the stomach, intestine, pancreas, and spleen travel through the hepatic portal vein to the liver. Most long-chain dietary lipids enter lymph as chylomicrons before reaching systemic blood.

## 2. Enteric nervous system and functions of the GIT

The **enteric nervous system (ENS)** is an intrinsic nervous system in the gut wall. It can coordinate many functions independently but is modified by autonomic nerves and hormones.

- **Myenteric (Auerbach) plexus:** between muscle layers; controls tone, peristalsis, segmentation, and sphincters.
- **Submucosal (Meissner) plexus:** in the submucosa; controls secretion, absorption, mucosal blood flow, and muscularis mucosae.

Enteric sensory neurons detect distension, chemical composition, osmolarity, and irritation. Excitatory motor neurons commonly release acetylcholine and substance P. Inhibitory motor neurons commonly release nitric oxide, vasoactive intestinal peptide, and ATP.

Parasympathetic stimulation through the vagus and pelvic nerves generally increases motility and secretion and relaxes sphincters. Sympathetic stimulation generally reduces motility and secretion, contracts sphincters, and constricts splanchnic vessels.

Important GI hormones include gastrin, secretin, cholecystokinin (CCK), glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP), motilin, and somatostatin.

General functions are ingestion and storage, propulsion and mixing, mechanical and chemical digestion, absorption of nutrients and water, endocrine regulation, immune defence, excretion of indigestible material and bile pigments, and maintenance of fluid and electrolyte balance.

## 3. Saliva

The major salivary glands are the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands. Acinar cells form primary secretion and duct cells modify it.

### Composition and functions

Saliva is about 99% water and contains sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphate, thiocyanate, mucins, salivary amylase, lingual lipase, lysozyme, lactoferrin, peroxidases, and secretory IgA.

It lubricates food, facilitates swallowing and speech, begins starch and some fat digestion, dissolves molecules for taste, buffers acids, protects teeth, and supports oral mucosal defence. At low flow it is hypotonic with relatively low sodium/chloride and higher potassium. At high flow there is less ductal modification, so sodium, chloride, and bicarbonate rise toward plasma values.

### Regulation and disorders

Food, chewing, taste, smell, nausea, and conditioned stimuli activate salivatory nuclei in the medulla and pons. Parasympathetic stimulation produces abundant watery saliva and increases glandular blood flow. Sympathetic stimulation produces a smaller, more viscous, protein-rich secretion.

- **Xerostomia:** dry mouth due to dehydration, drugs, autonomic dysfunction, radiation, or gland disease; causes dental caries and difficulty swallowing.
- **Sialorrhoea:** excessive salivation, seen with nausea, oral irritation, pregnancy, or neurological disease.
- **Sialadenitis or salivary stones:** painful swelling, often worse during meals.

## 4. Deglutition

Swallowing transfers a bolus from mouth to stomach while protecting the airway.

1. **Oral phase:** voluntary. The tongue presses the bolus against the palate and into the oropharynx.
2. **Pharyngeal phase:** involuntary. The soft palate closes the nasopharynx, the larynx elevates, vocal cords adduct, breathing is inhibited, and the upper oesophageal sphincter relaxes.
3. **Oesophageal phase:** primary peristalsis carries the bolus to the stomach; secondary peristalsis clears residue. The lower oesophageal sphincter relaxes ahead of the bolus.

The swallowing centre is in the medulla and lower pons. **Dysphagia** is difficult swallowing. **Achalasia** is failure of lower sphincter relaxation with weak oesophageal peristalsis. Gastro-oesophageal reflux results from reflux of gastric contents, while aspiration occurs when airway protection fails.

## 5. Stomach

The stomach consists of fundus, body, antrum, and pylorus. It stores food, mixes it into chyme, begins protein digestion, contributes to fat digestion, secretes intrinsic factor, kills many microbes, and meters chyme into the duodenum.

### Gastric juice

Gastric juice contains water, hydrochloric acid, pepsinogen, gastric lipase, intrinsic factor, mucus, bicarbonate, and electrolytes.

- **Hydrochloric acid:** secreted by parietal cells via H+/K+-ATPase; denatures protein, activates pepsin, releases minerals, and kills microbes.
- **Pepsinogen:** secreted by chief cells and converted to pepsin in acid; begins protein digestion.
- **Gastric lipase:** contributes to triglyceride digestion, especially in infants.
- **Intrinsic factor:** binds vitamin B12 for absorption in the terminal ileum.
- **Mucus and bicarbonate:** protect the mucosa from acid and pepsin.

### Regulation

The **cephalic phase** is triggered by sight, smell, taste, thought of food, and chewing through vagal pathways. The **gastric phase** is triggered by distension and peptides; vagovagal/local reflexes and gastrin stimulate secretion. Gastrin also stimulates enterochromaffin-like cells to release histamine, which acts on parietal H2 receptors. The **intestinal phase** briefly stimulates secretion but mainly inhibits the stomach when duodenal acid, fat, hyperosmolarity, or distension is present. Secretin, CCK, GIP, somatostatin, and enterogastric reflexes slow gastric activity.

The mucosal barrier depends on mucus, bicarbonate, tight junctions, blood flow, prostaglandins, and rapid epithelial repair. Breakdown may cause gastritis or peptic ulcer disease.

## 6. Gastric motility, MMC, and vomiting

The proximal stomach undergoes receptive relaxation to receive a meal. The distal stomach generates mixing waves; antral contractions grind solids against the closed pylorus, producing retropulsion. Liquids and small particles empty faster than solids. Fat, acid, hyperosmolar chyme, and duodenal distension slow emptying.

The **migrating motor complex (MMC)** is a fasting cycle that clears residual food, mucus, and bacteria from the stomach and small intestine. It includes quiescent, irregular, and short intense contraction phases. Motilin helps initiate the active phase. Eating interrupts the MMC and produces fed-pattern mixing.

Vomiting is coordinated by a medullary centre receiving GI vagal, vestibular, cortical, and area-postrema chemoreceptor inputs. Nausea is followed by salivation, airway closure, soft-palate elevation, lower oesophageal relaxation, inspiration, and forceful contraction of diaphragm and abdominal muscles. Complications include dehydration, electrolyte loss, metabolic alkalosis, aspiration, and mucosal tears.

## 7. Pancreas and pancreatic juice

Pancreatic acinar cells secrete digestive enzymes; duct cells secrete bicarbonate-rich fluid. Pancreatic juice contains water, bicarbonate, pancreatic amylase, lipase, colipase, cholesterol esterase, phospholipase, nucleases, and protease zymogens.

Trypsinogen is activated by enteropeptidase on the duodenal brush border. Trypsin activates chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, and procarboxypeptidases. Pancreatic trypsin inhibitor limits activation inside the gland.

- **Secretin:** released by duodenal acid; stimulates bicarbonate-rich ductal secretion.
- **CCK:** released by fatty acids and amino acids; stimulates enzyme-rich acinar secretion and gall-bladder contraction.
- **Vagal acetylcholine:** supports enzyme and fluid secretion.

Bicarbonate neutralizes gastric acid and creates a pH suitable for pancreatic enzymes. Pancreatic insufficiency causes maldigestion, especially of fats.

## 8. Liver, gall bladder, and bile

### Liver functions

The liver produces bile, processes portal nutrients, regulates blood glucose, synthesizes plasma proteins and clotting factors, makes cholesterol and lipoproteins, detoxifies drugs and toxins, converts ammonia to urea, stores glycogen/iron/copper/vitamins, removes aged cells through Kupffer cells, conjugates bilirubin, and participates in immune and endocrine regulation.

### Gall bladder and bile

The gall bladder stores and concentrates bile. CCK causes its contraction and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi after a fatty meal; the vagus contributes. Bile contains water, bile salts, bile pigments, cholesterol, phospholipids, bicarbonate, and electrolytes.

Bile salts emulsify fat and form micelles that carry fatty acids, monoglycerides, cholesterol, and fat-soluble vitamins to the brush border. Bile also excretes bilirubin, excess cholesterol, and some drugs.

Hepatocytes secrete bile into canaliculi, which drain through hepatic ducts and the common bile duct. Most bile salts are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and return through the portal vein to the liver: this is **enterohepatic circulation**. Secretin increases bicarbonate-rich bile, while CCK controls gall-bladder delivery.

## 9. Small intestine and succus entericus

The small intestine is the principal site of digestion and absorption. Circular folds, villi, and microvilli increase its surface area; villi contain blood capillaries and a central lacteal.

Succus entericus comes from crypt cells, Brunner glands, and surface epithelial cells. It contains water, electrolytes, mucus, bicarbonate, antimicrobial substances, and brush-border enzymes. Enteropeptidase, disaccharidases, aminopeptidases, and dipeptidases complete digestion at the epithelial surface. Brunner glands protect the duodenum with alkaline mucus; crypt cells secrete chloride-rich fluid and surface cells absorb water and electrolytes.

### Movements

- **Segmentation:** rhythmic circular contractions mix chyme with secretions and expose it to mucosa.
- **Peristalsis:** contraction behind and relaxation ahead of contents, producing aboral propulsion.
- **MMC:** fasting clearance pattern.
- **Villus movements:** muscularis mucosae contractions improve absorption and lymph flow.

The gastroileal reflex increases ileal activity after a meal. The ileocaecal sphincter limits colonic reflux and regulates entry into the caecum.

## 10. Large intestine and defecation

The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes, stores and concentrates faeces, supports bacterial fermentation, absorbs short-chain fatty acids and some vitamin K/B vitamins, and secretes protective mucus. Its microbiota produce metabolites, gases, and immune signals.

Haustral contractions mix contents. Peristalsis moves them along the colon. Mass movements propel faeces over long distances, often after meals through gastrocolic and duodenocolic reflexes.

Rectal distension initiates the defecation reflex. The intrinsic enteric reflex produces weak peristalsis and internal anal sphincter relaxation. Parasympathetic pelvic nerves intensify contraction and relaxation. Voluntary relaxation of the external anal sphincter, supplied by the pudendal nerve, plus abdominal and pelvic-muscle contraction completes defecation. The cortex can delay or facilitate the act.

## 11. Digestion and absorption of carbohydrates

Salivary amylase begins starch digestion but is inactivated by gastric acid. Pancreatic amylase forms maltose, maltotriose, and alpha-limit dextrins. Brush-border enzymes finish digestion:

- maltase -> glucose;
- sucrase -> glucose and fructose;
- lactase -> glucose and galactose;
- isomaltase -> glucose from branch points.

Glucose and galactose enter enterocytes through sodium-glucose cotransporter 1 (SGLT1), fructose through GLUT5, and all exit basolaterally through GLUT2 into portal blood. Unabsorbed carbohydrate is fermented in the colon to short-chain fatty acids and gas. Lactase deficiency causes osmotic diarrhoea, bloating, and gas.

## 12. Digestion and absorption of proteins

Gastric acid denatures protein and pepsin begins hydrolysis. Pancreatic trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, and carboxypeptidases continue digestion. Brush-border and intracellular peptidases produce amino acids, dipeptides, and tripeptides.

Amino acids enter through sodium-dependent transporters. Dipeptides and tripeptides enter through hydrogen-dependent PepT1 and are usually hydrolysed in enterocytes. Amino acids enter portal blood, and the liver uses them for protein synthesis, energy metabolism, gluconeogenesis, or urea production.

## 13. Digestion and absorption of fats

Lingual and gastric lipases begin fat digestion. In the small intestine, bile salts emulsify fat. Pancreatic lipase, assisted by colipase, hydrolyses triglycerides into monoglycerides and fatty acids. Cholesterol esterase and phospholipase digest other lipids.

Micelles carry lipid products through the unstirred water layer to enterocytes. Inside, lipids are re-esterified and combined with cholesterol, phospholipids, and apolipoprotein B-48 to form chylomicrons. Chylomicrons enter lacteals, pass through lymph, and reach blood through the thoracic duct. Lipoprotein lipase releases fatty acids to muscle and adipose tissue. Short- and medium-chain fatty acids can enter portal blood directly.

Fat malabsorption causes steatorrhoea and deficiencies of vitamins A, D, E, and K. Causes include pancreatic insufficiency, bile obstruction, ileal disease, and loss of intestinal surface.

## 14. Integrated regulation and correlation

The cephalic phase prepares saliva, gastric acid, pepsinogen, and pancreatic secretion before food arrives. The gastric phase responds to distension and peptides with motility and gastrin. The intestinal phase slows gastric emptying and stimulates secretin and CCK when acid, fat, and amino acids enter the duodenum.

1. **The ENS links structure to function:** stretch and chemical sensors coordinate secretion, segmentation, peristalsis, and sphincters.
2. **Secretions act sequentially:** saliva starts carbohydrate digestion; acid and pepsin process protein; pancreatic bicarbonate neutralizes acid; pancreatic enzymes digest all major nutrients; bile enables lipid digestion; brush-border enzymes finish digestion.
3. **Motility controls exposure time:** receptive relaxation stores food, antral waves grind it, the pylorus meters chyme, segmentation mixes it, and peristalsis moves it toward absorptive sites.
4. **Hormones coordinate organs:** gastrin supports acid, secretin supplies bicarbonate, and CCK coordinates pancreatic enzymes, bile delivery, and delayed gastric emptying.
5. **The liver is the metabolic gateway:** sugars and amino acids enter portal blood, while chylomicrons use lymph because they are too large for ordinary capillaries.
6. **Bile and pancreas cooperate:** bile salts emulsify and transport fat; pancreatic lipase hydrolyses it. Failure of either causes fat malabsorption.
7. **GI disease affects fluid and pH:** vomiting loses hydrogen and chloride and may cause metabolic alkalosis; diarrhoea loses bicarbonate and may cause metabolic acidosis.
8. **The colon completes digestion:** it recovers water and electrolytes, uses bacteria to ferment residual carbohydrate, forms faeces, and coordinates storage with defecation.
9. **Protective reflexes link systems:** swallowing protects the airway, vomiting removes harmful contents, and sphincters prevent reflux and aspiration.
10. **Disease localizes to a step:** achalasia affects propulsion, ulcer disease affects mucosal defence, pancreatic insufficiency affects enzymes, bile obstruction affects fat absorption, lactase deficiency affects carbohydrate digestion, and ileal disease affects bile-salt and B12 handling.

## High-yield summary

The GI tract is an integrated neuromuscular, secretory, endocrine, immune, and absorptive system. The ENS provides local control, autonomic nerves modify it, and hormones coordinate stomach, pancreas, liver, gall bladder, and intestine. Digestion converts food into absorbable units, the small intestine absorbs them through blood or lymph, and the large intestine conserves water and stores waste. Timing ensures that propulsion, secretion, enzyme action, neutralization, and absorption occur in the correct region.
