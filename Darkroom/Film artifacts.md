# Film Artifacts

## 1. Definition
Film artifacts are unwanted marks, patterns, or image defects that appear on a photographic or radiographic film and reduce the quality of the final image. They are not part of the intended image, but they can confuse interpretation, reduce contrast, or mimic pathology or other important features.

In simple words, an artifact is any unwanted change in density, contrast, sharpness, or appearance caused by something other than the true subject being recorded. In radiography, artifacts are especially important because they may be mistaken for real anatomical findings or technical faults in the image.

### Why artifacts matter
- They reduce image clarity.
- They may hide important details.
- They can lead to incorrect diagnosis or poor visual interpretation.
- They show that something went wrong during exposure, processing, handling, or storage.

---

## 2. Causes of Film Artifacts
Film artifacts occur because the film is affected by one or more steps in the imaging chain. The main causes can be grouped as follows:

### A. Exposure-related causes
These happen before or during image formation.
- Incorrect exposure factors such as too much or too little radiation.
- Patient motion during exposure, causing blur.
- Poor film-screen contact, which may create unclear or mottled images.
- Improper positioning or movement of the cassette or object.
- Scatter radiation or poor collimation, which may reduce contrast.

### B. Processing-related causes
These happen during development, fixation, washing, or drying.
- Incorrect developer temperature.
- Overdevelopment or underdevelopment.
- Dirty or exhausted chemicals.
- Poor agitation or uneven chemical flow.
- Inadequate fixing, rinsing, or drying.
- Contamination of chemicals by splashes or particles.

### C. Handling-related causes
These occur when the film is touched, bent, scratched, or exposed incorrectly.
- Fingerprints or greasy marks.
- Scratches from rough handling.
- Pressure marks caused by stacking or squeezing the film.
- Creases or bends while loading or unloading.
- Static electricity discharge, especially in dry conditions.

### D. Storage-related causes
These happen when the film is not stored properly.
- Excessive heat or humidity.
- Light exposure before or after processing.
- Old or expired film.
- Poor packaging or poor ventilation.
- Chemical fumes near storage area.

### E. Equipment and material causes
These arise from the imaging or processing system itself.
- Dirty intensifying screens.
- Damaged cassettes.
- Faulty rollers or transport mechanisms in automatic processors.
- Poor quality or contaminated film.
- Defective or poorly maintained processing equipment.

## 3. Types of Film Artifacts
Artifacts can be classified according to their appearance and origin.

### A. Exposure artifacts
These are caused by problems during image formation.
- Overexposure or underexposure
- Motion blur
- Double exposure
- Poor screen-film contact
- Grid cut-off
- Scatter-related fog

These artifacts usually affect the overall image density and sharpness.

### B. Processing artifacts
These are caused by faults during the development process.
- Developer streaks
- Fixer streaks
- Water spots
- Chemical fog
- Reticulation (wrinkled or cracked appearance due to sudden temperature changes)
- Brown staining due to incomplete fixation
- Uneven development or patchy density

These artifacts often appear as streaks, blotches, or irregular density changes.

### C. Handling artifacts
These result from physical contact or mechanical damage.
- Fingerprints
- Scratches
- Creases
- Pressure marks
- Static marks
- Curling or warping

These usually appear as visible lines, marks, or surface damage on the film.

### D. Storage artifacts
These are caused by environmental or aging effects.
- Light fog
- Age fog
- Moisture marks
- Yellowing or discoloration
- Fungal or chemical damage from poor storage

These often reduce contrast and create a dull or foggy appearance.

### E. Equipment-related artifacts
These are due to the imaging or processing machine.
- Roller marks
- Cassette marks
- Screen marks
- Guide shoe marks
- Roller pressure artifacts

These are common in automatic processing systems and can appear as repeated lines or patterns.

## 4. How These Topics Are Related
The topics of definition, causes, and types are closely connected because artifacts are not isolated events. They are the result of a chain of processes that begins with image formation and ends with storage and interpretation.

### Relationship between definition and causes
A film artifact is defined by the fact that it is an unwanted image defect. Its cause may be exposure, processing, handling, or storage. Therefore, the definition of an artifact depends on understanding what created it.

### Relationship between causes and types
The same cause can produce different types of artifacts. For example:
- Improper processing may produce streaks, fog, or staining.
- Poor handling may produce scratches, fingerprints, or pressure marks.
- Exposure errors may produce blur, underexposure, or poor contrast.

So the type of artifact seen on the film is often a visible clue to its underlying cause.

### Relationship between types and image quality
Different artifact types affect image quality in different ways:
- Exposure artifacts reduce accuracy of density and contrast.
- Processing artifacts create uneven appearance and may obscure fine detail.
- Handling artifacts cause visible physical defects that distract from the real image.
- Storage artifacts reduce overall clarity and may make the film look foggy or dull.

### Overall correlation
Film artifacts are related because the entire imaging process is interconnected:
1. Exposure creates the initial image.
2. Processing develops and fixes the image.
3. Handling determines whether the film is damaged physically.
4. Storage preserves or destroys image quality.

If one stage is poor, the artifact may appear later or become more severe. For example, a film that was poorly stored may become foggy, and if it is then processed at the wrong temperature, the fogging may become worse. Likewise, a film with poor screen contact may be more vulnerable to visible defects during handling or processing.

---

## 5. In Short
Film artifacts are unwanted defects on film that reduce clarity and may lead to misinterpretation. They are caused by problems during exposure, processing, handling, storage, or equipment use. Their types vary from blur and fog to streaks, scratches, stains, and pressure marks. All these topics are closely related because every artifact is a result of a breakdown in one or more steps of the imaging process.

Understanding film artifacts is important because it helps in preventing them, recognizing them correctly, and maintaining good image quality.
