# The recording system: film material

## 1. Introduction
The film is the recording medium in radiography. It captures the invisible X-ray image and converts it into a visible image after processing. In radiography, the film does not record the image directly in the same way a digital detector does; instead, it responds to radiation and light, forming a latent image that becomes visible after chemical development.

The performance of the film depends on its construction, the type of radiation it receives, and whether it is used with intensifying screens or without them. Therefore, understanding film material means understanding not only the layers of the film, but also how those layers influence image quality, speed, contrast, and sharpness.

---

## 2. Film construction
A radiographic film is made of several layers, each with a distinct purpose.

### 2.1 Film base
The film base is the thick supporting layer of the film.

- It provides mechanical strength and flexibility.
- It must be transparent so that light can pass through during viewing.
- It should be dimensionally stable so that it does not shrink or stretch during handling and processing.
- In older systems, cellulose triacetate was used; modern films commonly use polyester because it is more durable and stable.

The base itself does not record the image. Its importance lies in supporting the active layers and preserving image quality.

### 2.2 Subbing layer
The subbing layer is a thin adhesive layer between the base and the emulsion.

- It helps the emulsion adhere firmly to the base.
- It prevents the emulsion from peeling off during processing or handling.
- It ensures the film remains intact under repeated processing and mechanical stress.

Although it is not the image-forming layer, it contributes to the physical stability of the film.

### 2.3 Emulsion
The emulsion is the most important layer because it is where the image is formed.

- It contains silver halide crystals, usually silver bromide, often mixed with silver iodide.
- These crystals are sensitive to radiation and visible light.
- When X-rays or light strike the crystals, some silver ions are reduced and form a latent image center.
- During development, these centers are amplified so that the affected crystals become black metallic silver.

The size, shape, and concentration of silver halide crystals determine the film’s speed, contrast, and resolution.

### 2.4 Supercoat
The supercoat is a protective outer layer.

- It protects the emulsion from scratches, fingerprints, and mechanical damage.
- It helps prevent damage during handling and processing.
- It is transparent and does not interfere with image formation.

### 2.5 Backing layer
The backing layer is present on the opposite side of the film in some films.

- It reduces light reflection and halation.
- It helps absorb stray light and minimize image blur.
- In some films, it also reduces the crossover effect.

---

## 3. How the film records an image
Radiographic film works by forming a latent image.

1. X-rays or light from intensifying screens interact with the silver halide crystals in the emulsion.
2. The interaction creates a small amount of chemical change in the crystals.
3. This change forms a latent image center, which is invisible at first.
4. During development, the exposed crystals are reduced to black metallic silver.
5. Unexposed crystals are removed during fixing, leaving a visible image.

This means the film is a chemical recording system. The final image depends on how many crystals were exposed, how strongly they were exposed, and how the film was processed.

---

## 4. Crossover effect and irradiation
### 4.1 Crossover effect
The crossover effect occurs when light from one emulsion layer crosses over and exposes the opposite emulsion layer in a double-emulsion film.

This is important because most screen films are duplitized, meaning they have two emulsion layers, one on each side of the base. When an intensifying screen exposes one side, light may scatter and pass through the base into the other emulsion layer. As a result, the opposite side may also be exposed, causing image blur or loss of sharpness.

### 4.2 Why it happens
Crossover happens because light produced by the screen is not perfectly directional. Some light spreads and travels through the film structure. In a duplitized film, this can expose the emulsion on the other side of the base.

### 4.3 Effects of crossover
- Reduced image sharpness
- Increased blur around edges
- Loss of detail
- Poor definition in the final image

### 4.4 Irradiation
Irradiation is the spread of light or radiation within the film and screen system. It can increase the area of exposure beyond the original point of interaction.

In practical terms, irradiation can cause the image to appear slightly larger, less sharp, or less distinct at boundaries. It is especially relevant in screen-film systems where light from intensifying screens spreads across the film.

### 4.5 How crossover and irradiation are related
Crossover and irradiation are related because both involve the spread of exposure beyond the intended point. The main difference is that crossover specifically refers to light crossing from one emulsion layer to the other, while irradiation refers to a broader spread of exposure within the recording system.

Both reduce spatial resolution and therefore affect image quality.

## 5. Types of films
Radiographic films can be classified in several ways.

### 5.1 Screen films
Screen films are designed to be used with intensifying screens.

- They are more sensitive to light than to direct X-rays.
- The screen absorbs X-rays and emits light, and the film responds to that light.
- Screen films are widely used in general radiography because they require lower radiation dose.

Advantages:
- Lower patient dose
- Faster exposure
- Suitable for routine imaging

Disadvantages:
- Slightly less detail than direct exposure films
- Image quality depends on screen performance

### 5.2 Non-screen films
Non-screen films are used without intensifying screens.

- They are mainly sensitive to direct X-rays.
- They require higher radiation exposure because they do not benefit from screen amplification.
- They are used when very high detail is required.

Advantages:
- Very high resolution
- Excellent detail

Disadvantages:
- Higher dose to the patient
- Slower exposure

### 5.3 Single-emulsion films
Single-emulsion films have one emulsion layer only.

- They are used when maximum sharpness and reduced crossover are needed.
- They are common in mammography and some special imaging applications.
- They are often used with one intensifying screen or with direct exposure systems.

Advantages:
- Better detail
- Less crossover effect
- Better control of image geometry

Disadvantages:
- Lower speed compared with duplitized films in some applications

### 5.4 Duplitized films
Duplitized films have two emulsion layers, one on each side of the base.

- They are faster because each X-ray or light interaction can be recorded by both emulsions.
- They are commonly used for routine radiography because they increase film speed and reduce exposure time.
- They are highly efficient in screen-film systems.

Advantages:
- Greater sensitivity
- Lower exposure required
- Useful for high-throughput imaging

Disadvantages:
- More prone to crossover effect
- Can have slightly reduced sharpness if not well designed

### 5.5 CRT films
CRT films are films used in connection with cathode ray tube (CRT) display systems, especially in older imaging and medical viewing systems.

- They are designed to be sensitive to the light emitted by a CRT screen.
- They are mainly associated with photographic recording of displayed images rather than direct X-ray exposure.
- Their main role is to capture an image displayed on a monitor or screen for documentation or archival purposes.

Although CRT films are not a major part of modern digital radiography, they are historically important because they connect the image display and film recording systems.

### 5.6 Film storage
Proper film storage is essential because film is chemically sensitive and can deteriorate if not handled correctly.

- Films should be stored in a cool, dry, and clean environment.
- Excessive heat, humidity, and light exposure can cause fogging and reduce image quality.
- Films should be kept away from radiation, chemical fumes, and strong sunlight.
- They should be stored upright and protected from pressure, dust, and physical damage.
- Proper storage helps maintain film speed, contrast, and clarity.

Poor storage can lead to:
- Increased background fog
- Reduced contrast
- Loss of detail
- Poor diagnostic quality

Film storage is closely related to film performance because even a well-made film can produce a poor image if it has been damaged by improper storage.

## 6. Relationship between the subtopics
All these topics are closely connected.

### 6.1 Film construction and image quality
The structure of the film directly influences:
- Speed: how quickly the film responds to radiation or light
- Contrast: how differences in exposure appear as different densities
- Resolution: how clearly fine details are recorded
- Sensitivity: how easily the film forms a visible image

For example, a film with larger silver halide crystals may be faster but have lower detail. A film with finer crystals may record more detail but require more exposure.

### 6.2 Film type and patient dose
Screen films reduce patient dose because the intensifying screen converts X-rays into light, and the film is very sensitive to that light. Non-screen films require more radiation because the film must respond directly to X-rays.

Thus, film type is strongly related to dose and efficiency.

### 6.3 Single-emulsion and duplitized film systems
Single-emulsion films are usually chosen when detail is the primary concern. Duplitized films are chosen when speed and efficiency are more important.

This is why the crossover effect becomes especially relevant in duplitized films. Because double-emulsion films have two layers, the chance of light spreading from one layer to the other is greater, unless the design reduces this problem.

### 6.4 Crossover, irradiation, and sharpness
Crossover and irradiation both reduce the ability of the film to reproduce fine structures sharply. They are therefore linked to the choice of film type and construction. A film designed to reduce crossover, or a system using appropriate screens and layers, will produce a sharper image.

---

## 7. Summary
The recording system in radiography depends heavily on the film material. The film is built from layers such as the base, subbing layer, emulsion, supercoat, and backing layer, each of which performs an important function. The most important part is the emulsion, where silver halide crystals form the latent image.

The major concepts of crossover effect and irradiation are closely related to how light spreads within the film-screen system. These effects influence sharpness and detail. Different film types—screen films, non-screen films, single-emulsion films, and duplitized films—are selected depending on the balance required between dose, speed, contrast, and resolution.

In short, film material is not just a passive support; it is the active recording system that determines how well an X-ray image is captured and how clearly the anatomical details are seen.
