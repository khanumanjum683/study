# Film Artifacts

## 1. Definition of film artifacts

Film artifacts are unwanted marks, patterns, or changes in density that appear on a radiographic or photographic film but do not represent the true anatomy, object, or image being recorded. They are abnormalities introduced during any part of the image-making process, including exposure, processing, handling, storage, or transport.

In simple terms, an artifact is any image defect that does not belong to the real subject. It may make the film look unclear, noisy, stained, streaked, or distorted. In radiography, artifacts are especially important because they can sometimes mimic disease, fracture, foreign bodies, or other abnormalities, leading to wrong interpretation.

### Why artifacts matter

Artifacts reduce image quality because they:

- obscure fine details,
- reduce contrast,
- create false shadows or bright areas,
- confuse diagnosis or interpretation,
- lower the usefulness of the film for teaching, reporting, and storage.

So, understanding artifacts is not only about recognizing defects; it is also about understanding the whole imaging chain and preventing avoidable errors.

## 2. Causes of film artifacts

Film artifacts can result from many causes, but they usually arise from one or more of the following categories:

### A. Exposure-related causes

These occur before processing and are related to how the film was exposed to radiation or light.

- Incorrect exposure factors such as too much or too little radiation
- Patient movement during exposure
- Improper positioning or misalignment
- Double exposure or repeated exposure
- Faulty cassette or screen contact
- Foreign objects or unwanted scatter radiation

These problems may produce blurred images, poor density, or false shadows that resemble real pathology.

### B. Processing-related causes

These occur during development, fixing, washing, or drying and are among the most common sources of artifacts.

- Incorrect developer temperature
- Excessive or insufficient development time
- Poor agitation during processing
- Contaminated or exhausted chemicals
- Wrong pH or chemical concentration
- Inadequate fixing or washing
- Dirty tanks, trays, or hangers

Processing defects often appear as streaks, fog, stains, mottling, or uneven density.

### C. Handling and mechanical causes

These occur when the film is touched, bent, scratched, or stored badly.

- Finger marks or greasy fingerprints
- Scratches from rough handling
- Pressure marks from bending or stacking
- Static electricity
- Curling or warping during drying
- Poor darkroom handling

These artifacts are often easy to recognize because they appear as visible physical marks rather than chemical image changes.

### D. Storage and environmental causes

Poor storage conditions can cause film deterioration and artifact-like appearance.

- Excessive heat
- High humidity
- Light exposure
- Old or expired film
- Improper packing or stacking

Storage problems may cause fogging, discoloration, brittleness, or loss of image clarity.

## 3. Types of film artifacts

Artifacts can be classified according to their origin and appearance. The main types are described below.

### A. Exposure artifacts

These are caused by problems during exposure.

#### 1. Motion blur

This occurs when the patient, film, or x-ray tube moves during exposure. The image becomes blurred and fine details may be lost.

- Causes: patient movement, shaking, unsteady positioning
- Effect: unclear structures, loss of sharpness

#### 2. Double exposure

This happens when the film is exposed twice, often due to operator error.

- Causes: repeated exposure without resetting the system
- Effect: superimposed images, confusing density patterns

#### 3. Poor positioning or alignment artifacts

These arise when the object is not properly placed relative to the beam or film.

- Causes: incorrect centering, wrong projection, improper cassette placement
- Effect: distortion, cutoff, or missing anatomy

#### 4. Scatter or backscatter artifacts

These are caused by unwanted radiation that reaches the film from unintended directions.

- Causes: poor beam collimation, inappropriate shielding
- Effect: fog, reduced contrast, extra density

### B. Processing artifacts

These are caused by errors during development, fixing, washing, or drying.

#### 1. Developer streaks and marks

These appear as lines or irregular streaks caused by uneven developer action.

- Causes: poor agitation, uneven chemical flow, dirty processing equipment
- Effect: streaked or patchy density

#### 2. Fixer streaks or incomplete fixation

These occur when the fixer does not remove all undeveloped silver halide crystals properly.

- Causes: exhausted fixer, insufficient fixing time, poor solution quality
- Effect: milky appearance, poor image permanence, residual unreduced silver

#### 3. Chemical fog

Chemical fog is an overall unwanted grayness or haze over the film.

- Causes: overdevelopment, old developer, high temperature, contamination
- Effect: reduced contrast and detail visibility

#### 4. Staining and discoloration

The film may appear yellow, brown, or blotchy.

- Causes: exhausted fixer, poor washing, residual chemicals, contamination
- Effect: reduced clarity and archival quality

#### 5. Reticulation

Reticulation is a wrinkled or net-like pattern on the film surface.

- Causes: sudden temperature changes between solutions
- Effect: emulsion damage and visible patterning

#### 6. Emulsion swelling or blistering

This happens when the gelatin layer is damaged by chemical or temperature imbalance.

- Causes: poor processing conditions, high temperature, poor solution balance
- Effect: rough texture, peeling, or irregular image appearance

#### 7. Uneven development and mottle

This appears as patchy or grainy density differences.

- Causes: poor agitation, uneven developer levels, contaminated solution
- Effect: nonuniform film density

### C. Handling artifacts

These arise from physical contact or mechanical damage.

#### 1. Finger marks

Fingerprints or smudges appear when the film is touched with oily or dirty hands.

- Causes: poor darkroom handling
- Effect: dark or light marks on the film

#### 2. Scratches

These are linear marks caused by rough contact with surfaces or sharp objects.

- Causes: careless handling, dirty hanger surfaces, poor storage
- Effect: visible lines that may resemble structures

#### 3. Pressure marks

These are caused by pressure or bending of the film.

- Causes: stacking, bending, excessive force
- Effect: local dark or light areas

#### 4. Static marks

Static artifacts appear as tree-like or branching black marks.

- Causes: friction, dry conditions, rapid handling
- Effect: lightning-like black lines or patterns

### D. Storage artifacts

These arise after processing if the film is stored badly.

#### 1. Fogging

The film becomes generally gray or dull over time.

- Causes: light exposure, old film, heat, humidity
- Effect: loss of contrast and visibility

#### 2. Yellowing or browning

The film becomes discolored because of chemical change or poor fixation.

- Causes: poor washing, chemical residues, aging
- Effect: reduced clarity and image quality

#### 3. Curling and brittleness

The film may become hard, brittle, or warped.

- Causes: improper drying, humidity changes, age
- Effect: cracking and handling difficulty

## 4. How these topics are related

The definition, causes, and types of film artifacts are closely connected. They are not separate ideas; they form one continuous chain of understanding.

### A. Definition and cause are linked

An artifact is defined by its unwanted nature, but its existence is always due to a cause. For example:

- if the developer is too warm, the artifact may be chemical fog;
- if the film is bent, the artifact may be a pressure mark;
- if the patient moves, the artifact may be motion blur.

So, the definition tells us what an artifact is, while the cause explains why it happened.

### B. Cause and type are closely connected

A single cause can produce more than one type of artifact. For example:

- poor processing conditions can cause streaking, fog, staining, and mottling;
- bad handling can cause scratches, fingerprints, and pressure marks;
- poor storage can cause fogging, yellowing, and brittleness.

In this way, the same faulty condition may appear as different artifact types depending on the stage of the process and the nature of the defect.

### C. The whole image chain is interdependent

Film artifacts are strongly related to the complete workflow:

1. Exposure determines the initial image formation.
2. Processing determines how clearly that image is developed and preserved.
3. Handling and storage determine whether the image remains intact over time.

If one step is weak, the final film may show defects even if the other steps were correct. For example:

- poor exposure may create weak contrast;
- weak development may make the image look thin and foggy;
- incomplete fixation may leave residual chemicals that cause staining;
- poor washing may worsen discoloration and shorten film life.

This means that artifacts are not isolated problems. They reflect the quality of the entire imaging and processing system.

---

## 5. Summary

Film artifacts are unwanted defects that appear on film and do not represent the true object or anatomy. They are caused by exposure errors, processing faults, handling problems, or poor storage conditions. Their types include exposure artifacts, processing artifacts, handling artifacts, and storage artifacts.

The three topics are deeply related:

- Definition tells us what an artifact is.
- Causes explain why it forms.
- Types describe how it appears.

In practice, recognizing artifacts helps us prevent them, improve image quality, and avoid misinterpretation. A good understanding of artifacts is therefore essential in both darkroom work and radiographic imaging.
