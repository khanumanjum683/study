# Factors Affecting Image Quality

## 1. Radiographic image

A radiographic image is an image formed when X-rays pass through the patient and reach an image receptor such as film, a computed radiography cassette, or a digital detector. The purpose of the image is not only to make the body visible, but to make the important structures visible in a clear, accurate, and diagnostically useful way.

A good radiographic image should show:

- the relevant anatomy clearly,
- the abnormality or pathology if present,
- the true shape and size of structures as much as possible,
- and enough detail for proper interpretation.

Therefore, radiographic image quality is the degree to which an image successfully represents the object being examined.

### Main factors that determine radiographic image quality

The quality of a radiographic image depends on several interrelated factors:

- Density
- Contrast
- Sharpness
- Noise
- Resolution
- Magnification and distortion

These factors do not act independently. A change in one factor may improve or worsen another. For example, increasing exposure may reduce noise but increase patient dose, while changing the positioning may improve sharpness but also affect distortion.

## 2. Factors affecting image quality

### A. Density

Density is the overall blackness or brightness of the radiograph. In film radiography, it refers to how dark the image appears. In digital radiography, it is often described as exposure level or brightness.

If too few X-rays reach the receptor, the image is underexposed and appears too light. If too many X-rays reach the receptor, the image is overexposed and appears too dark.

#### Factors affecting density

- mAs: the main controlling factor for density. Increasing mAs increases the number of X-ray photons and usually increases density.
- kVp: affects penetration. Higher kVp often increases density because more X-rays penetrate the body.
- Patient thickness: thicker body parts absorb more radiation, so the image becomes lighter.
- Tissue type: bone absorbs more radiation than soft tissue, so it appears lighter.

#### Importance of density

Density is important because it influences visibility. If the image is too light, fine details may be hidden. If it is too dark, important structures may become difficult to distinguish.

---

### B. Contrast

Contrast is the difference in density or brightness between adjacent structures. It allows us to distinguish one tissue from another.

A high-contrast image shows strong black-and-white differences. A low-contrast image shows more gray shades and is useful for seeing subtle differences between similar tissues.

#### Factors affecting contrast

- kVp: lower kVp increases contrast, while higher kVp decreases contrast.
- Tissue composition: bone, air, fat, and soft tissue absorb X-rays differently.
- Scatter radiation: excess scatter reduces contrast and causes fogging.
- Image processing: important especially in digital imaging.

#### Importance of contrast

Without enough contrast, the image may look flat and important structures may blend together. Contrast is essential for identifying pathology and differentiating organs or tissues.

---

### C. Sharpness

Sharpness refers to how clearly the edges of structures are defined. A sharp image has crisp borders; a blurred image has fuzzy edges.

Blurring reduces the ability to see small structures and makes interpretation less accurate.

#### Causes of poor sharpness

- Patient motion
- Large focal spot size
- Improper positioning of object and receptor
- Movement of the equipment
- Poor detector performance

#### Importance of sharpness

Sharpness is vital for seeing fine details such as fractures, lung markings, or small lesions.

---

### D. Noise

Noise is unwanted random variation in the image that makes it look grainy or unclear. It reduces visibility and may obscure fine detail.

#### Types of noise

- Quantum mottle: caused by too few X-ray photons reaching the receptor
- Scatter radiation: adds unwanted exposure and reduces clarity
- Electronic noise: caused by the detector or processing system
- Motion-related noise: due to patient movement

#### Importance of noise

Even if contrast is good, too much noise can hide subtle changes. A radiograph with high noise may be less diagnostic even if it appears technically exposed.

---

### E. Resolution

Resolution is the ability to distinguish two closely spaced objects as separate details. It is one of the most important factors in seeing small structures.

There are two main types:

- Spatial resolution: ability to see very small structures
- Contrast resolution: ability to distinguish structures with slight differences in contrast

#### Importance of resolution

Good resolution allows the radiologist to detect small fractures, tiny calcifications, fine lung patterns, and subtle pathology.

---

### F. Magnification and distortion

Magnification means that the structure appears larger than its true size. Distortion means that the shape or position of the structure is altered on the image.

#### Magnification

Magnification increases when:

- the object is farther from the image receptor,
- or the X-ray source is closer to the object.

#### Distortion

Distortion occurs when the object is not parallel to the image receptor or when the central ray is not properly aligned. This may produce:

- foreshortening: the object appears shorter than it really is,
- elongation: the object appears longer than it really is.

#### Importance of magnification and distortion

Even if the image is clear and well exposed, inaccurate magnification and distortion can lead to wrong interpretation of size and shape.

## 3. Geometry of the radiographic image

The geometry of a radiographic image refers to the spatial relationship among three main elements:

1. the X-ray tube (source),
2. the patient or object being examined,
3. the image receptor.

The X-rays travel in a diverging beam from the focal spot. Because of this, the geometry of the beam strongly influences the appearance of the final image.

### Key geometric principles

#### A. Source-image distance (SID)

SID is the distance between the X-ray source and the image receptor.

- A longer SID reduces magnification.
- A longer SID also improves sharpness because the beam becomes more nearly parallel.

#### B. Object-image distance (OID)

OID is the distance between the object and the image receptor.

- A shorter OID reduces magnification.
- A shorter OID improves sharpness and reduces geometric blur.

#### C. Focal spot size

The focal spot is the area of the X-ray tube where the X-rays are produced.

- A smaller focal spot produces sharper images.
- A larger focal spot causes more geometric unsharpness, often called penumbra.

#### D. Central ray alignment

The central ray should pass through the object and strike the image receptor perpendicularly.

If the central ray is not properly aligned, the image may become distorted.

#### E. Object and receptor alignment

If the object is not parallel to the image receptor, shape distortion can occur.

This is why patient positioning is so important in radiography.

---

## 4. Geometric unsharpness

Geometric unsharpness is the blurring caused by the geometry of the X-ray beam. It occurs because the X-ray source is not a point source; it has a finite size.

The amount of geometric unsharpness increases when:

- the focal spot is large,
- the OID is large,
- or the SID is short.

A simple relationship is:

$$U = \frac{F \times OID}{SID}$$

where:

- $U$ = geometric unsharpness,
- $F$ = focal spot size,
- $OID$ = object-image distance,
- $SID$ = source-image distance.

This formula shows that geometric unsharpness becomes greater when the focal spot is larger, the object is farther from the receptor, or the source is too close to the object.

---

## 5. How radiographic image quality and geometry are related

Radiographic image quality and geometric factors are strongly connected. The geometry of the image determines how accurately the image represents the object.

### Relationship between geometry and image quality

- Geometry affects sharpness: proper SID, short OID, and a small focal spot produce sharper images.
- Geometry affects magnification: increasing SID and decreasing OID reduce magnification.
- Geometry affects distortion: correct alignment of the object, central ray, and receptor reduces distortion.
- Geometry affects resolution: a well-controlled geometric setup helps the system display fine detail more clearly.

### Why they are co-related

A radiograph is not only affected by exposure factors such as mAs and kVp. It is also affected by the physical arrangement of the source, object, and receptor. If the geometry is poor, even a perfectly exposed image may still be blurred, magnified, or distorted.

In other words:

- exposure factors control density, contrast, and noise,
- geometric factors control sharpness, magnification, and distortion,
- and together they determine the overall diagnostic quality of the radiographic image.

---

## 6. Summary

Radiographic image quality depends on a balance of several factors. The image must be properly exposed, clear, detailed, and accurate.

The main factors are:

- Density: how dark or light the image is
- Contrast: how well different tissues can be distinguished
- Sharpness: how clearly edges are defined
- Noise: unwanted image graininess or irregularity
- Resolution: ability to see fine detail
- Magnification and distortion: accuracy of size and shape

The geometry of the radiographic image is especially important because it directly influences sharpness, magnification, and distortion. Good geometry and proper exposure together produce a high-quality radiograph.

In simple terms, a good radiographic image is one that is well exposed, properly positioned, geometrically correct, and diagnostically useful.
