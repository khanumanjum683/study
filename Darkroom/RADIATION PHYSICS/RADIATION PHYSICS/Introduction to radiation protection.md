# Introduction to Radiation Protection

## 1. Need for protection
Radiation protection is necessary because ionizing radiation can damage living tissues. When radiation passes through the body, it can interact with atoms and molecules, causing ionization. This may lead to:

- Immediate tissue damage, such as skin burns, hair loss, or radiation sickness at high doses.
- Delayed effects, such as cancer or genetic damage, which may appear years later.
- Harm to developing tissues, especially in children and unborn fetuses.

The main reason for protection is to prevent unnecessary exposure and to keep radiation doses as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA). Protection is especially important in medical imaging, radiotherapy, industrial radiography, and nuclear work.

## 2. Aim of radiation protection
The aim of radiation protection is to:

1. Prevent deterministic effects by avoiding high doses.
2. Limit the probability of stochastic effects such as cancer and hereditary disease.
3. Justify the use of radiation so that benefits outweigh risks.
4. Optimize protection so that exposure is kept as low as reasonably achievable.
5. Keep dose limits within internationally accepted recommendations.

In simple terms, radiation protection aims to protect people, workers, patients, and the public from harmful effects while allowing useful applications of radiation to continue.

## 3. Basic radiation units and quantities
Several quantities are used to describe radiation and its biological effects. These quantities are related but not identical.

### 3.1 Radiation field and exposure
The radiation field refers to the presence and distribution of radiation in space. It is important for measuring how much radiation is present around a source.

### 3.2 Exposure
Exposure is a measure of the ability of X-rays or gamma rays to ionize air. It is defined as the amount of electrical charge produced by ionization per unit mass of air.

- Unit: coulomb per kilogram (C/kg)
- Older unit: roentgen (R)

Exposure is mainly used for X-ray and gamma-ray measurements in air. It tells us how intense the radiation field is, but it does not directly tell us how much energy is absorbed by tissue.

## 4. Absorbed dose
Absorbed dose describes the amount of energy deposited by radiation in a material, including human tissue.

- Definition: energy absorbed per unit mass
- Unit: gray (Gy)
- 1 Gy = 1 joule per kilogram (J/kg)

This is more directly related to biological damage because tissue injury depends on energy deposition. For example, if two tissues receive the same absorbed dose, the biological effect may still differ depending on the type of radiation and the tissue involved.

## 5. Dose equivalent (absorbed dose equivalent)
Different types of radiation cause different biological effects even when their absorbed dose is the same. For example, alpha particles are much more damaging than X-rays or gamma rays for the same energy deposited.

To account for this, the dose equivalent is used:

- Dose equivalent = absorbed dose × quality factor
- Unit: sievert (Sv)

This quantity better reflects the biological effect of radiation exposure.

## 6. Quality factor
The quality factor (Q) is a factor that reflects how harmful a particular type of radiation is compared with X-rays or gamma rays.

- High-LET radiation such as alpha particles has a higher quality factor.
- Low-LET radiation such as X-rays and gamma rays has a lower quality factor.

Typical examples:

- X-rays, gamma rays, beta particles: lower biological effectiveness
- Neutrons: moderate to high effectiveness depending on energy
- Alpha particles: very high biological effectiveness

The quality factor is important because the same absorbed dose can produce different biological damage depending on the radiation type.

> In modern radiation protection, the term radiation weighting factor is often used instead of quality factor, but the concept is similar.

## 7. Tissue weighting factor
Not all tissues in the body are equally sensitive to radiation. Some organs are more radiosensitive than others.

The tissue weighting factor (wT) is used to represent the relative sensitivity of a tissue or organ to radiation-induced cancer or hereditary effects.

Examples of tissues with higher weighting factors include:

- Bone marrow
- Colon
- Lung
- Stomach
- Breast
- Gonads

Tissues such as skin and bone have lower weighting factors.

## 8. Effective dose
Effective dose combines the dose equivalent received by different tissues with the sensitivity of those tissues.

- Formula: effective dose = sum of (tissue dose × tissue weighting factor)
- Unit: sievert (Sv)

This is a very important quantity because it allows radiation exposure to be expressed as a single number that reflects overall risk to the whole body.

## 9. How these quantities are related
These quantities are connected in a stepwise way:

1. Exposure measures the ionization produced in air by X-rays or gamma rays.
2. Absorbed dose measures the energy actually deposited in matter, including tissue.
3. Dose equivalent adjusts the absorbed dose for the type of radiation using the quality factor.
4. Tissue weighting factor adjusts the dose according to how sensitive the organ or tissue is.
5. Effective dose combines all of these to estimate overall biological risk.

### In simple terms:
- Exposure tells us how much radiation is present.
- Absorbed dose tells us how much energy is deposited.
- Dose equivalent tells us how biologically harmful that dose is for a given radiation type.
- Tissue weighting tells us which organs are more vulnerable.
- Effective dose gives a practical estimate of overall risk.

## 10. Why the relationship matters in radiation protection
The relationship between these quantities is essential because protection is not based only on the amount of radiation present. A radiation field may produce a certain exposure, but the biological effect depends on:

- The energy absorbed by tissue
- The radiation type
- The tissue or organ exposed
- The total risk to the whole body

This is why radiation protection uses a hierarchy of quantities rather than a single value alone. The goal is to estimate and reduce the real biological risk, not just measure the radiation field.

## 11. Conclusion
Radiation protection depends on understanding how radiation is measured and how it affects living tissue. Exposure, absorbed dose, dose equivalent, quality factor, and tissue weighting factor are all linked parts of the same system. Together, they help professionals assess risk, set safety limits, and ensure radiation is used safely and responsibly.
