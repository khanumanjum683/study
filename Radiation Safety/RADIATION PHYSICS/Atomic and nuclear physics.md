# Atomic and Nuclear Physics

## 1. Review of ideas on atomic and nuclear physics

Atomic and nuclear physics is the branch of physics that describes the structure of matter at the smallest scale and the behavior of nuclei. It provides the scientific basis for understanding radiation, radioactivity, and the interaction of atomic particles with matter.

### 1.1 Structure of the atom

An atom consists of:

- a central nucleus containing protons and neutrons
- electrons revolving around the nucleus in shells or orbitals

The atomic nucleus is extremely small but contains almost all the mass of the atom.

#### Important particles:

- proton: positively charged, mass approximately 1 atomic mass unit
- neutron: neutral, mass slightly greater than the proton
- electron: negatively charged, very small mass compared with nucleons

The number of protons in an atom determines its atomic number, Z, and identifies the element.

The total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons) is the mass number, A.

Thus, nuclide notation is:

A
X
Z

where:

- X = chemical symbol
- Z = atomic number = number of protons
- A = mass number = number of protons + neutrons

Example:

- 146C: carbon with 6 protons and 8 neutrons
- 23892U: uranium with 92 protons and 146 neutrons

### 1.2 Nuclear forces

The nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force, which acts between nucleons and is much stronger than the electrostatic repulsion between protons at short distances.

This force is responsible for:

- nuclear stability
- binding of nucleons in the nucleus
- nuclear reactions and radioactive decay

### 1.3 Atomic structure and energy levels

Electrons are arranged in discrete energy levels. When electrons move from one shell to another, radiation may be emitted or absorbed.

These transitions produce:

- characteristic X-rays
- atomic spectra
- electron binding energies

This is related to nuclear and atomic physics because nuclear transitions and atomic transitions both involve quantized energy levels.

## 2. Nuclides and their classification

A nuclide is a species of atom characterized by its number of protons and neutrons.

A nuclide is completely described by:

- atomic number Z
- mass number A
- energy state of nucleus

### 2.1 Isotopes

Isotopes are nuclides with the same atomic number but different mass numbers.

Example:

- 12C, 13C, 14C

They have the same chemical properties but different nuclear properties.

### 2.2 Isobars

Isobars are nuclides with the same mass number A but different atomic numbers Z.

Example:

- 14C and 14N

### 2.3 Isotones

Isotones are nuclides with the same number of neutrons but different proton numbers.

Example:

- 14C and 15N (both have 8 neutrons)

### 2.4 Isomers

Nuclear isomers are nuclides with the same Z and A but different nuclear energy states.

Example:

- metastable nuclear states or isomeric states

These states differ in internal energy and often decay by gamma emission or internal conversion.

### 2.5 Stable and unstable nuclides

Nuclides may be:

- stable: remain unchanged indefinitely
- unstable (radioactive): spontaneously decay to become more stable

The stability of a nucleus depends on:

- neutron-to-proton ratio
- nuclear binding energy
- shell effects
- pairing effects

Unstable nuclei are radioactive and undergo decay processes such as alpha decay, beta decay, and gamma emission.

---

## 3. Radioactivity

Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of an unstable nucleus with emission of particles and/or electromagnetic radiation.

Radioactive decay occurs because the nucleus is in an excited or unstable state and seeks a lower-energy configuration.

### 3.1 Types of radioactive decay

The major decay modes are:

- alpha decay
- beta decay
- gamma emission
- internal conversion
- electron capture (in some cases)

### 3.2 Decay law

Radioactive decay follows an exponential law:

N = N0 e^{-λt}

where:

- N = number of radioactive nuclei remaining at time t
- N0 = initial number
- λ = decay constant
- t = time

This law shows that radioactive nuclei decrease in number with time according to a fixed probability per unit time.

---

## 4. Half-life

Half-life is the time required for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay.

It is denoted by T1/2.

The relationship between half-life and decay constant is:

T1/2 = 0.693 / λ

### Importance of half-life

- tells how fast a radionuclide decays
- helps determine radiation exposure and storage times
- used in medicine, radiography, environmental monitoring, and nuclear waste management

### Examples

- short half-life: decays quickly, emits intense radiation for a short time
- long half-life: persists for a long time, lower activity per unit time

### Half-life and activity

Activity A is the number of decays per second and is written as:

A = λN

Thus, as the number of radioactive atoms decreases, the activity also decreases.

This is directly related to the decay law and the concept of radioactivity.

## 5. Alpha decay

Alpha decay is the emission of an alpha particle from the nucleus.

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus:

2He4

It consists of:

- 2 protons
- 2 neutrons

### General equation

ZXA → Z-2YA-4 + 2He4 + Q

where Q is the released energy.

### Characteristics of alpha decay

- occurs mainly in heavy nuclei such as uranium and radium
- because the nucleus is too large and unstable
- alpha particles are relatively heavy and carry +2 charge
- alpha radiation has low penetration but high ionization power

### Why alpha decay happens

A nucleus with too many protons and neutrons may reduce its mass and charge by emitting an alpha particle, moving toward a more stable configuration.

### Typical example

23892U → 23490Th + 42He + energy

### Biological significance

Alpha particles are highly ionizing but have low penetration. They are dangerous if inhaled or ingested, because they can irradiate body tissues internally.

---

## 6. Beta decay

Beta decay is the process by which a nucleus changes its proton-to-neutron ratio by emitting a beta particle.

There are two main types:

- beta-minus decay (β−)
- beta-plus decay (β+)

### 6.1 Beta-minus decay (β−)

A neutron in the nucleus converts into a proton, electron, and antineutrino.

n → p + e− + ν̄

#### General equation

ZXA → Z+1YA + e− + antineutrino + energy

#### Example

14C → 14N + β− + antineutrino + energy

#### Characteristics

- neutron converts to proton
- atomic number increases by 1
- mass number remains the same
- beta particles are electrons
- beta radiation has moderate penetration

### 6.2 Beta-plus decay (β+)

A proton converts into a neutron, positron, and neutrino.

p → n + e+ + ν

#### General equation

ZXA → Z−1YA + e+ + neutrino + energy

#### Example

11C → 11B + β+ + neutrino + energy

#### Characteristics

- proton converts to neutron
- atomic number decreases by 1
- mass number remains the same
- positron is emitted and usually annihilates with an electron producing gamma photons

### Why beta decay happens

It allows the nucleus to move toward a more stable neutron-to-proton ratio.

### Biological significance

Beta particles are more penetrating than alpha particles but less ionizing. They can cause external and internal radiation exposure depending on source and energy.

---

## 7. Gamma emission

Gamma emission is the release of a high-energy photon from the nucleus after a nuclear transition.

It usually occurs when a nucleus is in an excited state after alpha or beta decay.

### General concept

The nucleus may have excess energy after decay. It loses this excess energy by emitting a gamma photon.

### Equation

ZXA* → ZXA + γ

where the asterisk indicates a nucleus in an excited state.

### Characteristics

- no change in Z or A
- gamma rays are electromagnetic waves
- very penetrating
- often emitted after alpha or beta decay
- high energy compared with visible light

### Example

99mTc → 99Tc + γ

Here the metastable state decays by gamma emission.

### Biological significance

Gamma rays are highly penetrating and are important in imaging, radiotherapy, and radiation protection. Because they can pass through tissues, they are a major external hazard.

---

## 8. Internal conversion

Internal conversion is a process in which the nucleus transfers its excitation energy directly to one of the orbital electrons, usually a K-shell electron, instead of emitting a gamma ray.

### Process

- nucleus is in an excited state
- instead of emitting a gamma photon, the energy is transferred to an inner-shell electron
- the electron is ejected from the atom
- the atom becomes ionized

### Result

- no gamma photon is emitted
- an electron is ejected from the atomic shell
- the atom becomes ionized

### Comparison with gamma emission

In gamma emission, the excess nuclear energy is released as a photon.

In internal conversion, the energy is transferred directly to an orbital electron.

### Importance

This process competes with gamma emission. It is especially important for low-energy nuclear transitions and for nuclei with high internal conversion coefficients.

---

## 9. Nuclear isomerism

Nuclear isomerism refers to the existence of nuclides with the same atomic number Z and same mass number A but different nuclear energy states.

These are called nuclear isomers.

### Example

99mTc and 99Tc are isomeric forms of technetium-99.

The metastable form 99mTc has a higher energy state and decays to the lower-energy ground state with gamma emission or internal conversion.

### Characteristic features

- same Z and A
- different nuclear energy levels
- one state may be metastable and survive longer than usual
- transition between states may release gamma radiation

### Relationship to gamma emission

Nuclear isomerism is closely related to gamma decay because the decay of a metastable nuclear state often involves gamma emission.

This explains why many diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals are produced as metastable isomers.

---

## 10. Radioactive decay and nuclear stability

Nuclear decay occurs when a nucleus is unstable. Stability depends largely on the balance between protons and neutrons and the total energy state of the nucleus.

Unstable nuclei undergo spontaneous transformations to reach more stable arrangements.

### Common reasons for instability

- too many neutrons
- too many protons
- too much energy in the nucleus
- unfavorable neutron-to-proton ratio

### Decay modes are selected according to the type of instability

- too many neutrons → beta-minus decay
- too many protons → beta-plus decay or electron capture
- too heavy nucleus → alpha decay
- excess excitation energy → gamma emission or internal conversion

This shows how the different decay processes are related and how they work together to restore nuclear stability.

---

## 11. Radioactivity and its relationship with half-life

Radioactivity is a property of unstable nuclei, while half-life is a quantitative measure of how quickly they decay.

A radionuclide may have:

- short half-life → high activity initially, rapid decay
- long half-life → lower activity over time, persists longer

### Relation between activity and half-life

Activity decreases as the number of radioactive atoms decreases. Since the number remaining falls exponentially, the activity also falls exponentially.

This means the half-life provides a simple way to predict how long a radionuclide remains active.

### Example

If a radionuclide has a half-life of 10 hours, after 10 hours half of it remains, after 20 hours one quarter remains, and so on.

This rule is essential in radiation protection, nuclear medicine, and environmental radioactivity assessment.

---

## 12. How these topics are interrelated

All of these topics are connected by the central concept of nuclear instability and the search for lower-energy states.

### The overall flow is:

1. Atomic structure defines the arrangement of electrons and nucleus.
2. Nuclear structure determines whether a nuclide is stable or unstable.
3. Unstable nuclei are radioactive.
4. They decay by alpha emission, beta decay, gamma emission, or internal conversion.
5. The decay process changes the nucleus to a more stable form.
6. If the nucleus remains in an excited state, it may emit gamma rays or undergo internal conversion.
7. Nuclear isomerism explains different energy states of the same nuclide.
8. Half-life measures the rate at which the radionuclide decays.
9. The entire process is governed by nuclear physics and is fundamental to radiological protection.

### Example linking several concepts

Consider a radioactive isotope of technetium:

- it is a nuclide with a specific Z and A
- it is unstable and decays
- it may exist in a metastable isomeric state
- this state emits gamma radiation or undergoes internal conversion
- the decay rate is described by its half-life

This single example connects nuclides, nuclear isomerism, gamma emission, internal conversion, and radioactivity.

---

## 13. Final summary

Atomic and nuclear physics provides the foundation for understanding radiation. The atom consists of a nucleus and electrons, and the nucleus contains protons and neutrons bound together by the strong nuclear force. Nuclides are classified by atomic number and mass number, and unstable nuclides are radioactive.

Radioactive decay occurs when an unstable nucleus transforms into a more stable state. The major decay modes are alpha decay, beta decay, and gamma emission. Internal conversion is another way in which an excited nucleus can lose energy by ejecting an orbital electron directly. Nuclear isomerism describes different energy states of the same nucleus, and metastable states are often important in medicine and radiography.

Half-life is the time required for half the atoms in a sample to decay and is the key measure of radioactive decay rate. These concepts are all interconnected because each one describes a different aspect of the same process: the instability of the atomic nucleus and its movement toward a lower-energy, more stable state.

This is the basis of all radiation science, including radiological protection, nuclear medicine, and radiation measurement.
