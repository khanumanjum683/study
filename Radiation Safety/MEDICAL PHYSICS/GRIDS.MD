 # Grids

## 1. Definition and purpose

An X-ray grid is a device placed between the patient and the image receptor to absorb scattered radiation before it reaches the detector. It is made of radiopaque strips separated by radiolucent interspaces.

The primary purpose of a grid is to improve radiographic contrast by transmitting more primary radiation than scattered radiation. It is most useful when the patient is thick, the field is large, or the kVp is relatively high and scatter production is substantial.

The grid does not prevent scatter from being produced in the patient. It removes a portion of the scatter after it has been produced and before it reaches the receptor.

This distinction is important:

- collimation reduces the volume of tissue exposed and therefore reduces scatter production
- a grid absorbs scatter that has already been produced
- the air-gap technique reduces the amount of scatter reaching the receptor by increasing the distance between patient and detector

These approaches are related but not identical.

---

## 2. Construction of a grid

A conventional grid contains:

- lead strips, which absorb X-rays
- interspaces made of aluminium, fibre, or another low-attenuation material
- a protective cover
- a supporting frame

The lead strips are arranged so that primary rays travelling from the focal spot can pass through the interspaces, while obliquely travelling scattered rays are preferentially absorbed.

### 2.1 Grid lines

Grid lines are the visible or physical lead-strip pattern. If the lines are too widely spaced or are not properly removed by the imaging system, they may appear in the image.

### 2.2 Focused grid

A focused grid has angled lead strips that follow the divergence of the primary X-ray beam from a specified focal distance. It must be used at the correct source-to-image distance and with the correct tube orientation.

### 2.3 Parallel grid

A parallel grid has lead strips that are parallel to one another. It is less sensitive to focal-distance selection in some ways, but it can produce edge cut-off because the primary beam diverges while the strips do not.

### 2.4 Crossed grid

A crossed grid consists of two linear grids placed at right angles. It provides strong scatter clean-up but is very sensitive to positioning errors and cannot usually be angled across the tube axis.

### 2.5 Linear grid

A linear grid has lead strips running in one direction. It is commonly used with moving grid mechanisms that blur the grid lines during exposure.

### 2.6 Stationary grid

A stationary grid remains fixed during exposure. Its lines may be visible on the image unless the strip frequency is high enough or digital processing is used appropriately.

### 2.7 Moving grid or Potter-Bucky grid

A moving grid moves during exposure so that the lead strips are blurred and do not appear prominently in the radiograph. The grid may move continuously or oscillate during the exposure.

## 3. Important grid terms

### 3.1 Grid ratio

Grid ratio is the height of the lead strips divided by the distance between them:

Grid ratio = h / D

where:

- h = height of the lead strips
- D = width of the interspace

A high grid ratio absorbs scatter more effectively, but it is more sensitive to positioning, alignment, and focal-distance errors.

Common diagnostic grid ratios include approximately 5:1, 6:1, 8:1, 10:1, and 12:1. The appropriate value depends on patient thickness, kVp, field size, and examination type.

### 3.2 Grid frequency

Grid frequency is the number of lead strips per unit length, commonly expressed as lines per centimetre or lines per inch.

A high-frequency grid makes grid lines less visible but may contain thinner lead strips and have different scatter-absorption characteristics.

### 3.3 Focal distance or focal range

A focused grid is designed for a particular focal distance and range. Using it outside this range increases the chance of cut-off.

### 3.4 Interspace material

Aluminium interspaces provide mechanical strength but absorb more primary radiation than fibre interspaces. Fibre interspaces can improve primary transmission and reduce grid weight.

---

## 4. Types of grids

Grids can be classified by strip geometry, movement, and construction.

### 4.1 Parallel versus focused grids

Parallel grids have straight parallel strips. Focused grids have angled strips designed to align with beam divergence.

Focused grids generally provide better primary transmission over a useful field when used correctly, but they require correct focal distance and centring.

### 4.2 Linear versus crossed grids

Linear grids have one strip direction and are commonly used with moving mechanisms. Crossed grids have two superimposed strip directions and remove more scatter but are more difficult to position.

### 4.3 Stationary versus moving grids

Stationary grids remain in place and may produce visible grid lines. Moving grids blur the lines during exposure and are commonly used in Bucky systems.

### 4.4 Focused and unfocused grids

An unfocused grid may be parallel or crossed and does not require the same focused geometry. A focused grid must be used according to the focal distance, central ray, and tube orientation marked on the grid.

### 4.5 Reciprocating grid

A reciprocating grid moves back and forth during exposure. The movement reduces the visibility of grid lines while allowing the grid to clean up scatter.

### 4.6 Cellular or alternative scatter-reduction devices

Some imaging systems use specialized cellular structures or software-assisted scatter correction. These may perform a similar function but are not identical to a conventional lead-strip grid.

---

## 5. Evaluation of grid performance

Grid performance is evaluated by how effectively the grid improves contrast while transmitting an acceptable amount of primary radiation.

### 5.1 Primary transmission

Primary transmission is the fraction of useful primary radiation that passes through the grid. A good grid should transmit as much primary radiation as possible.

### 5.2 Scatter transmission

Scatter transmission is the fraction of scattered radiation that passes through the grid. A good grid should transmit as little scatter as possible.

### 5.3 Total transmission

Total transmission is the fraction of all radiation, primary plus scattered, that passes through the grid.

### 5.4 Selectivity

Grid selectivity is the ratio of primary transmission to scatter transmission:

Selectivity = Tp / Ts

where:

- Tp = primary transmission
- Ts = scatter transmission

A high selectivity means the grid is good at distinguishing primary radiation from scatter.

### 5.5 Contrast improvement factor

The contrast improvement factor compares radiographic contrast with a grid to contrast without a grid:

K = contrast with grid / contrast without grid

A value greater than 1 indicates improved contrast.

### 5.6 Bucky factor or grid factor

The Bucky factor is the ratio of incident radiation without a grid to the radiation required with a grid to produce the same receptor exposure. It is commonly expressed as:

B = incident radiation with grid / incident radiation without grid

A grid absorbs some primary radiation as well as scatter, so mAs usually must be increased when a grid is used. This increases patient dose if not managed carefully.

### 5.7 Contrast-to-noise and clinical performance

In digital imaging, grid performance should not be judged only by brightness because digital systems can rescale image appearance. Evaluation should include:

- contrast improvement
- noise and signal-to-noise ratio
- patient dose
- visibility of grid lines
- uniformity of the image
- frequency of cut-off or repeat exposures

### 5.8 Factors affecting grid performance

- grid ratio
- grid frequency
- strip material and thickness
- interspace material
- kVp
- field size
- patient thickness
- grid alignment
- source-to-image distance

Higher grid ratios generally provide greater scatter rejection but require more accurate positioning and greater exposure compensation.

## 6. Grid cut-off

Grid cut-off is the unwanted loss of primary radiation caused by incorrect alignment between the X-ray beam and the grid. It produces an image that is too light or noisy in part or all of the field and may lead to a repeat exposure.

### 6.1 Types of grid cut-off

#### Off-focus cut-off

This occurs when a focused grid is used at the wrong source-to-image distance. The beam divergence does not match the angled lead strips.

#### Off-centre cut-off

This occurs when the central ray is not centred to the grid or the tube is not aligned with the grid's central axis.

#### Off-level cut-off

This occurs when the grid is tilted relative to the central ray. It may happen if the tube, grid, or image receptor is angled incorrectly.

#### Upside-down focused-grid cut-off

A focused grid used upside down causes severe cut-off, often with more loss of primary radiation toward the edges. The focal distance markings and tube-side indicator must be checked.

#### Parallel-grid edge cut-off

With a parallel grid, beam divergence causes more primary radiation to be absorbed toward the edges, especially with a short source-to-image distance or a large field.

### 6.2 Causes of grid cut-off

- incorrect source-to-image distance
- tube not centred to the grid
- grid tilted or angled incorrectly
- focused grid upside down
- wrong grid focal range
- grid not level with the image receptor
- grid damage or manufacturing defect
- incorrect detector or Bucky alignment

### 6.3 Prevention

- use the marked focal distance
- centre the tube to the grid
- keep the central ray perpendicular when required
- keep the grid level
- place the correct side toward the tube
- use correct source-to-image distance
- inspect and test the Bucky system
- check for uniformity and cut-off during quality control

Grid cut-off is important because it reduces image quality, may obscure anatomy, and can lead to repeat exposures and additional patient dose.

## 7. Air-gap technique

The air-gap technique reduces scatter reaching the image receptor by increasing the distance between the patient and the receptor, creating an air gap.

It is an alternative to using a grid in selected situations.

### 7.1 Principle

Scattered photons leave the patient in many directions. When the image receptor is moved farther away, much of the obliquely travelling scatter misses the receptor. Primary photons travelling in the forward direction continue toward the receptor and form the image.

### 7.2 Technique

The patient is separated from the detector by a deliberate gap, often several centimetres. The source-to-image distance may be increased to maintain magnification and geometric sharpness.

### 7.3 Advantages

- reduces scatter reaching the receptor
- can improve contrast
- avoids grid-line artifacts
- avoids mechanical grid cut-off
- may be useful when a grid cannot be placed close to the patient

### 7.4 Disadvantages

- increases image magnification
- may increase geometric unsharpness if geometry is not adjusted
- requires careful positioning
- may require a longer source-to-image distance
- can require increased exposure depending on the technique
- may not be suitable for all body parts or equipment

### 7.5 Air-gap magnification relationship

The magnification factor is:

M = SID / SOD

where:

- M = magnification factor
- SID = source-to-image distance
- SOD = source-to-object distance

If the object-to-image distance is increased, SOD decreases for a fixed SID and magnification increases. To control magnification, the SID may also be increased.

---

## 8. Grid versus air-gap technique

Both techniques reduce the amount of scatter reaching the detector, but they do so differently.

### Grid

- absorbs scatter after it leaves the patient
- also absorbs some primary radiation
- requires mAs compensation
- does not normally introduce magnification
- may produce cut-off or grid-line artifacts

### Air gap

- allows scatter to miss the detector geometrically
- does not use lead strips
- introduces magnification and possible blur
- avoids grid cut-off
- requires careful distance and positioning control

The choice depends on patient thickness, anatomy, equipment, required contrast, acceptable magnification, dose, and clinical protocol.

---

## 9. Relationship with collimation and filtration

Scatter control is strongest when several methods are used appropriately.

### Collimation

Collimation reduces the volume of tissue irradiated and therefore reduces scatter production. It should be used whether or not a grid is present.

### Filtration

Filtration removes low-energy photons and increases beam quality. It does not remove scatter that has already been produced in the patient.

### Grid

A grid absorbs scattered radiation between the patient and detector. It is useful when scatter production remains high despite appropriate collimation.

### Air gap

An air gap reduces scatter reaching the detector by allowing oblique scattered photons to diverge away from the receptor.

Thus:

- filtration controls photon energy
- collimation controls irradiated area
- grids absorb scatter
- air gap uses geometry to reject scatter

---

## 10. Practical selection of a grid

A grid should be considered when:

- the body part is thick
- the field size is large
- kVp is relatively high
- scatter is reducing contrast
- the examination requires improved contrast detail

A grid may not be necessary for:

- small or thin body parts
- small fields
- low-scatter examinations
- situations where added dose cannot be justified

The grid ratio should be selected carefully. A high-ratio grid provides stronger scatter rejection but increases sensitivity to alignment errors and usually requires greater exposure compensation.

## 11. How all the topics are co-related

The complete imaging sequence is:

1. The X-ray beam enters the patient.
2. Photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering occur.
3. The photoelectric component contributes to useful differential attenuation.
4. Compton interactions create scattered photons.
5. Collimation reduces the amount of tissue producing scatter.
6. A grid absorbs much of the scatter before it reaches the detector, or an air gap allows scatter to miss the detector.
7. Primary radiation is used to form the image.
8. Grid selection, alignment, exposure compensation, and geometry determine contrast, noise, magnification, and patient dose.

Therefore, grids, air gaps, collimation, and filtration are complementary tools. A grid improves contrast by absorbing scatter but may increase dose and cause cut-off. An air gap reduces scatter without grid strips but may cause magnification. The best method is the one that provides the required diagnostic quality with the least unnecessary exposure.

---

## 12. Final summary

An X-ray grid is a scatter-reduction device made of radiopaque strips and radiolucent interspaces. Grid types include parallel, focused, crossed, linear, stationary, moving, and reciprocating grids. Grid performance is evaluated using primary transmission, scatter transmission, selectivity, contrast improvement factor, Bucky factor, image noise, and clinical dose.

Grid cut-off is the loss of primary radiation caused by incorrect focal distance, centring, levelling, tube orientation, or use of an upside-down focused grid. It causes non-uniform exposure and may result in repeat examinations.

The air-gap technique reduces scatter by separating the patient from the detector so that oblique scattered photons miss the receptor. It can improve contrast and avoid grid cut-off, but it increases magnification and requires careful geometry.

Grids and air gaps are related methods of scatter reduction. Collimation reduces scatter production, filtration shapes beam energy, grids absorb scatter, and air gaps reject scatter geometrically. Together, these techniques determine image contrast, noise, magnification, patient dose, and radiation protection.

## 13. Short exam-oriented conclusion

An X-ray grid is a device that absorbs scattered radiation before it reaches the image receptor. It may be parallel, focused, crossed, linear, stationary, or moving. Grid performance is assessed by primary and scatter transmission, selectivity, contrast improvement, and Bucky factor. Grid cut-off occurs when a focused grid is used at the wrong focal distance, is off-centred, tilted, or placed upside down. The air-gap technique reduces scatter by increasing the patient-to-detector distance so that scattered photons miss the receptor, but it may increase magnification. Grids, air gaps, collimation, and filtration are complementary methods that together improve contrast and control patient dose.
