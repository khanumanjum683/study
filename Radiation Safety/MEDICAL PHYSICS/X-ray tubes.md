 # X-Ray Tubes

## 1. Basic principle and construction

An X-ray tube converts electrical energy into X-rays. It contains a cathode and an anode inside a highly evacuated envelope. A high voltage accelerates electrons from the cathode to the anode target. Most electron energy becomes heat; only a small fraction becomes X-ray photons.

The cathode contains a heated tungsten filament and a focusing cup. The filament emits electrons by thermionic emission. The focusing cup directs them toward the focal spot. The anode contains a tungsten target, commonly supported by copper, molybdenum, or graphite. The tube housing provides electrical insulation, radiation shielding, mechanical support, and cooling oil.

The principal controls are:

- kVp: electron energy and X-ray beam quality
- mA: number of electrons and X-ray quantity
- exposure time: total number of electrons and total output
- focal spot: balance between image sharpness and heat capacity

These controls are linked: the cathode creates electrons, the generator accelerates them, the anode produces X-rays and heat, and filtration shapes the useful beam.

## 2. Thermionic emission

Thermionic emission is the release of electrons from a heated metal surface. A low-voltage, high-current filament circuit heats the tungsten filament. As temperature increases, more electrons escape and form a space-charge cloud around the filament.

Increasing filament temperature usually increases electron emission and tube current. Increasing kVp attracts more of the emitted electrons to the anode. At low kVp, the tube may be space-charge limited; at higher kVp, it may approach emission-limited or saturation operation.

Excessive filament heating shortens filament life and increases heat loading, so filament current is carefully controlled.

## 3. Gas-filled X-ray tube

An early gas-filled tube contained a cathode, anode, and low-pressure gas. Heating the cathode released electrons, and collisions with gas molecules caused ionization. The resulting electrons and positive ions allowed current to flow.

### Limitations

- gas pressure changes during use
- output and tube current are unstable
- residual gas absorbs electrons and radiation
- ionization produces unpredictable conduction
- exposure reproducibility is poor
- tube life and control are limited

Gas-filled tubes were therefore replaced by highly evacuated thermionic tubes. The evacuated tube allows electrons to travel with very few collisions and provides stable, controllable output.

## 4. Stationary-anode tube

A stationary-anode tube has a fixed tungsten target embedded in a copper anode. It is simple, compact, and used for dental, portable, and other relatively low-loading systems.

### Working

Electrons from the cathode are focused and accelerated to the fixed target. Bremsstrahlung is produced when electrons are decelerated near nuclei, and characteristic radiation is produced by inner-shell ionization. The target remains in one position, so heat is concentrated at one focal track.

### Advantages and limitations

Advantages include simple construction, low cost, and mechanical reliability. Limitations include low heat capacity, lower permissible mA, longer cooling intervals, and a greater risk of target damage if overloaded.

### Cooling methods

Heat is removed by conduction through the copper anode, convection and circulation of insulating oil, and radiation from the anode and housing. Special systems may use forced air or water cooling.

## 5. Rating and cooling charts

A tube rating chart gives the maximum safe combinations of kVp, mA, exposure time, focal spot, and sometimes anode speed. It prevents electrical overload and excessive instantaneous heat concentration. A large focal spot usually permits higher loading than a small focal spot.

A cooling chart shows the reduction of stored heat with time and helps determine when another exposure is safe. Cooling is faster when the tube is very hot and slower as it approaches ambient temperature.

Heat units are commonly estimated as:

HU = kVp x mA x seconds

For three-phase or high-frequency generators, a generator correction factor may be included. The exact factor and all chart limits are manufacturer-specific.

## 6. Rotating-anode tube

A rotating-anode tube spreads heat around a circular focal track. It is used in general radiography, fluoroscopy, CT, and other high-output systems.

### Construction and working

It contains a tungsten-rhenium focal track on a molybdenum or graphite disc, a rotor inside the evacuated envelope, and stator coils outside the envelope. When an exposure is selected, the stator produces a rotating magnetic field, causing the rotor and anode to spin. Electrons then strike a moving focal track, distributing heat over a much larger area.

### Advantages and limitations

Advantages are high heat capacity, higher permissible mA, repeated exposure capability, and improved heat distribution. Limitations include greater cost, mechanical noise, bearing wear, and the need to reach operating speed before exposure.

Diagnostic rotating anodes commonly operate at approximately 3,000 to 10,000 revolutions per minute, although the permitted speed is model-specific. A rating chart identifies safe kVp, mA, time, focal spot, and speed combinations.

### Anode angle

The target is inclined. A smaller anode angle can provide a smaller effective focal spot and better resolution, but it restricts field coverage and increases intensity variation. The angle also contributes to the anode heel effect.

## 7. Dual-focus tubes and choice of focus

A dual-focus tube has two filaments and provides a small and a large focal spot.

### Small focal spot

The small focus gives less geometric unsharpness and better fine detail. It is chosen for small or thin parts and when mA is low enough to remain within its rating. Its limitation is lower heat capacity.

### Large focal spot

The large focus permits greater mA and heat loading. It is chosen for thick body parts, large patients, and short high-output exposures. Its disadvantage is greater geometric unsharpness.

Thus focus selection is a compromise: choose the smallest focus that can safely handle the required kVp, mA, and time.

## 8. Anode heel effect

The anode heel effect is the variation of beam intensity across the field caused by absorption within the angled target. X-rays travelling toward the anode side pass through more target material, so intensity is lower on the anode side and higher on the cathode side.

The effect increases with a smaller anode angle, a larger field, and a shorter target-to-image distance. The thicker or more attenuating part of the body can be placed toward the cathode side, where intensity is greater, to improve exposure uniformity.

## 9. Grid-controlled X-ray tube

A grid-controlled tube has a control grid near the filament. When the grid is sufficiently negative, it repels electrons and stops tube current. When its voltage is changed, electrons flow and an exposure begins.

Grid control allows rapid switching, pulsed fluoroscopy, cine imaging, accurate exposure timing, and reduced motion blur. It controls electron flow and therefore tube current and X-ray intensity; kVp still controls maximum photon energy and beam quality.

## 10. Effect of anode voltage and filament temperature

Increasing anode voltage or kVp increases electron kinetic energy. It increases maximum and average photon energy, beam penetration, and usually X-ray output. It also changes image contrast and scatter conditions.

Increasing filament temperature increases thermionic emission, tube current, photon number, and beam intensity. It has little direct effect on maximum photon energy. Therefore, kVp mainly controls quality, whereas filament temperature mainly controls quantity. Both settings increase heat production and must remain within the rating chart.

## 11. Continuous and characteristic spectra

### Continuous spectrum

Bremsstrahlung occurs when a fast electron is deflected and decelerated by the electric field of a target nucleus. The lost kinetic energy becomes an X-ray photon. Since electrons lose different amounts of energy, a continuous range of photon energies is produced.

The maximum photon energy is approximately equal to the kVp in keV. For example, a 90 kVp tube can produce photons up to approximately 90 keV.

### Characteristic spectrum

An incident electron may eject an inner-shell electron from a target atom. An outer-shell electron falls into the vacancy and emits a photon whose energy equals the difference between the shell binding energies. These photons appear as discrete peaks characteristic of the target material.

kVp determines whether a characteristic transition can occur, the target determines the peak energies, and mA determines the number of photons produced.

## 12. Inherent and added filtration

Filtration removes low-energy photons that would be absorbed in the patient without contributing much to the image.

### Inherent filtration

Inherent filtration is supplied by the tube envelope, insulating oil, housing, and permanent tube window. Its amount depends on tube and housing construction.

### Added filtration

Added filtration is placed deliberately in the beam outside the tube housing. Aluminium is commonly used for diagnostic beams.

### Effect on the spectrum

Filtration:

- removes low-energy photons
- reduces total intensity
- increases mean and effective energy
- increases beam quality and penetration
- increases the half-value layer
- reduces unnecessary patient skin dose

Filtration does not increase maximum photon energy. It hardens the beam by preferentially removing its low-energy portion. The final spectrum is therefore shaped by kVp, target material, mA, and filtration.

## 13. How the topics are co-related

The complete operating sequence is:

1. The filament is heated and emits electrons by thermionic emission.
2. The focusing cup directs electrons toward the selected focal spot.
3. Anode voltage accelerates the electrons and controls their energy.
4. The stationary or rotating target converts electron energy into heat and X-rays.
5. Bremsstrahlung creates the continuous spectrum and shell transitions create characteristic peaks.
6. Rating charts limit safe electrical and thermal loading.
7. Cooling charts determine recovery time after exposure.
8. Focus selection balances spatial resolution against heat capacity.
9. The anode angle determines focal geometry and heel-effect intensity variation.
10. A grid-controlled tube can rapidly regulate electron flow.
11. Inherent and added filtration remove low-energy photons and reduce unnecessary dose.

Therefore, tube construction controls electron production and heat management, electrical settings control photon energy and quantity, and filtration controls the energy distribution of the final beam. Together they determine image sharpness, beam quality, X-ray intensity, tube safety, and patient dose.

## 14. Final summary

An X-ray tube uses a heated cathode to emit electrons and a positively charged anode target to produce X-rays. Gas-filled tubes are historically important but have poor stability and have been replaced by evacuated thermionic tubes. Stationary anodes are suitable for low loading, while rotating anodes distribute heat and permit higher output. Rating charts specify safe kVp, mA, time, focal spot, and speed conditions, while cooling charts show the removal of stored heat.

Dual-focus tubes allow a choice between resolution and heat capacity. The anode heel effect results from absorption within the angled target. Grid control rapidly regulates tube current. The continuous spectrum is produced by Bremsstrahlung and the characteristic spectrum by electron-shell transitions. Inherent and added filtration remove low-energy photons, increase effective energy and HVL, and reduce unnecessary patient dose.

All these topics are connected because they describe how the X-ray tube creates, controls, shapes, and safely delivers the useful X-ray beam.
