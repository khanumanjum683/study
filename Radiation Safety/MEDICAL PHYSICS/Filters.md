 # Filters

## 1. Definition of filtration

Filtration is the deliberate removal of selected low-energy X-ray photons from a beam before the beam reaches the patient. These photons are easily absorbed in superficial tissue and contribute little to image formation, so removing them improves the efficiency of the beam and reduces unnecessary patient dose.

Filtration is usually produced by placing an absorbing material, such as aluminium or copper, in the path of the X-ray beam. It changes the energy distribution of the beam rather than changing the maximum photon energy.

The main purposes of filtration are to:

- remove photons that would mostly increase skin dose
- increase the mean and effective photon energy
- increase beam penetration
- increase the half-value layer (HVL)
- improve radiation protection

Filtration is therefore closely related to beam quality, patient dose, exposure factors, and image formation.

---

## 2. X-ray spectrum before filtration

An X-ray tube produces a spectrum containing many photon energies. It includes:

- low-energy photons
- intermediate-energy photons
- high-energy photons
- characteristic radiation peaks

The maximum photon energy is controlled by kVp. However, many photons have energies well below the maximum. Low-energy photons are more likely to be absorbed in the patient's skin and do not penetrate far enough to contribute effectively to the image.

Filtration preferentially removes this low-energy portion of the spectrum.

## 3. Types of filtration

### 3.1 Inherent filtration

Inherent filtration is filtration already present in the X-ray tube assembly. It may come from:

- the glass or metal-ceramic tube envelope
- insulating oil
- the tube housing
- the permanent tube window
- other fixed material in the useful beam path

Inherent filtration is always present and cannot normally be removed by the operator. Its amount depends on the construction of the tube and housing.

### 3.2 Added filtration

Added filtration is material deliberately placed in the beam outside the tube housing. Aluminium is commonly used in diagnostic radiography. Copper may be used when stronger filtration is needed because it removes low-energy photons more efficiently per unit thickness.

Added filters may be fixed, removable, or automatically selected according to the examination and tube voltage.

### 3.3 Total filtration

Total filtration is the sum of inherent and added filtration:

Total filtration = inherent filtration + added filtration

The total filtration must meet the applicable equipment and safety requirements for the selected kVp range.

### 3.4 Aluminium filtration

Aluminium is widely used in general diagnostic radiography because it is practical, relatively inexpensive, and suitable for removing low-energy photons from diagnostic beams.

### 3.5 Copper filtration

Copper has a higher atomic number than aluminium and is more effective at removing low-energy photons for a given thickness. It may be combined with aluminium or used in specialized radiographic systems.

### 3.6 Compound filters

A compound filter uses more than one material, commonly copper followed by aluminium. The copper removes low-energy photons strongly, while the aluminium absorbs characteristic radiation or scattered low-energy photons produced in the filter.

The order and material combination are selected to produce the desired spectrum and comply with equipment requirements.

### 3.7 Compensating filters

Compensating filters have a non-uniform thickness. They reduce the beam more where the body part is thinner or less attenuating, producing a more uniform detector exposure.

Examples include:

- wedge filters
- trough filters
- contoured filters
- bow-tie filters

### 3.8 Wedge filter

A wedge is thick at one end and thin at the other. It is placed so that the thin part is over the thicker body region and the thick part is over the thinner region. This compensates for unequal body thickness.

### 3.9 Bow-tie filter

A bow-tie filter is commonly used in CT. It is thicker at the edges and thinner at the centre, matching the shape of the patient and reducing radiation to peripheral regions. It also helps produce a more uniform detector signal.

### 3.10 Mammography filters

Mammography uses specialized target-filter combinations, such as molybdenum, rhodium, or selected tungsten spectra. These produce a controlled low-energy beam suited to high-contrast soft-tissue imaging while limiting unnecessary energy.

### 3.11 Rare-earth and K-edge filters

Specialized filters may use materials with suitable absorption edges to shape the spectrum for particular imaging applications. Their use depends on the target material, detector, tube voltage, and clinical purpose.

## 4. Effect of filtration on the quality of the spectrum

Beam quality means the penetrating ability or hardness of the X-ray beam. Filtration changes quality by removing low-energy photons.

### 4.1 Beam hardening

After filtration, the average photon energy rises because the lower-energy photons have been removed. The beam is therefore called harder or more penetrating.

### 4.2 Effects on spectral shape

Filtration:

- removes the low-energy side of the spectrum
- increases mean energy
- increases effective energy
- increases HVL
- reduces the total area under the spectrum, representing fewer photons
- leaves the maximum photon energy essentially unchanged

Filtration does not create additional high-energy photons. It only selectively removes photons that are less useful for transmission through the patient.

### 4.3 Half-value layer

The half-value layer is the thickness of a specified absorber required to reduce beam intensity to one-half. A higher HVL generally indicates a harder, more penetrating beam.

Additional filtration increases HVL. HVL is therefore a practical measure of the effect of filtration and beam quality.

### 4.4 Homogeneous and heterogeneous beams

Diagnostic X-ray beams are heterogeneous, meaning they contain a range of photon energies. Filtration makes the beam more penetrating and reduces its low-energy component, but it does not usually make it completely monoenergetic.

## 5. Effect of filtration on intensity and exposure factors

Filtration reduces the number of photons in the beam, so it reduces beam intensity at the same kVp and mAs. However, the remaining photons have higher average energy and greater penetrating ability.

### 5.1 If mAs is unchanged

Adding filtration while keeping kVp and mAs unchanged usually causes:

- lower total photon quantity
- lower detector exposure
- higher beam penetration
- increased HVL
- reduced entrance skin dose in many situations

If filtration is excessive, the image receptor may receive insufficient exposure.

### 5.2 mAs compensation

To maintain the same detector exposure after adding filtration, mAs may need to be increased. This is called mAs compensation.

The exact compensation depends on:

- kVp
- filter material and thickness
- patient thickness
- detector sensitivity
- image receptor system
- automatic exposure control

The purpose of compensation is to preserve image receptor exposure without restoring the same unnecessary low-energy skin dose.

### 5.3 Effect on kVp

Filtration does not directly increase kVp or maximum photon energy. It changes the spectrum by removing low-energy photons. In some protocols, kVp and filtration are selected together to obtain the desired beam quality.

### 5.4 Effect on exposure time

If mA is fixed and additional mAs is required, exposure time may need to be increased. Alternatively, a higher mA can be selected if the tube rating permits it.

### 5.5 Effect on automatic exposure control

With automatic exposure control, the system may prolong the exposure or adjust output until the detector receives the required signal. The final mAs and patient dose depend on the selected filtration, patient thickness, kVp, and AEC response.

---

## 6. Effect of filtration on the patient

Filtration is primarily a patient-protection measure.

### 6.1 Reduction of skin dose

Low-energy photons are absorbed mainly in superficial tissue. Removing them reduces the number of photons depositing energy in the skin without contributing significantly to the image.

### 6.2 Reduction of unnecessary absorption

The filtered beam has a greater proportion of photons capable of passing through the patient and reaching the detector. This improves the useful fraction of the radiation output.

### 6.3 Possible change in image contrast

Increasing beam energy and hardness may reduce subject contrast in some examinations because attenuation differences between tissues become less pronounced. The change must be balanced against improved penetration and reduced superficial dose.

### 6.4 Effect on total dose

With suitable mAs compensation, filtration can reduce entrance skin dose while maintaining detector exposure. The exact dose change depends on patient thickness, kVp, beam geometry, filter material, and the imaging protocol.

Filtration is not a substitute for correct collimation, positioning, shielding policies, and optimized exposure selection.

---

## 7. Effect of filtration on image formation

Image formation depends on the photons that pass through the patient and reach the detector.

Filtration affects the image by:

- increasing beam penetration
- reducing the proportion of photons absorbed superficially
- changing subject contrast
- changing scatter conditions indirectly through changes in beam energy
- potentially improving exposure uniformity when a compensating filter is used

Ordinary beam filtration controls the energy distribution across the whole field. Compensating filtration additionally controls the spatial distribution of intensity across the field.

---

## 8. Practical selection of filters

The filter must be selected according to the examination.

### General radiography

Aluminium or aluminium-equivalent total filtration is commonly used to remove low-energy photons while preserving useful penetration.

### High-output or high-kVp imaging

Copper may be added when stronger spectral shaping is required. The resulting beam may require mAs adjustment.

### Mammography

Target-filter combinations are selected to provide suitable low-energy contrast for breast tissue.

### CT

Bow-tie filters shape the beam according to patient geometry, reduce peripheral dose, and help equalize detector exposure.

### Unequal body thickness

Wedge or other compensating filters can improve exposure uniformity across the image field.

---

## 9. Advantages and limitations of filtration

### Advantages

- reduces unnecessary low-energy radiation
- lowers entrance skin dose when appropriately used
- increases beam penetration
- increases HVL and beam quality
- improves the efficiency of the useful beam
- can equalize exposure across regions of unequal thickness

### Limitations

- reduces total beam intensity
- may require increased mAs
- may reduce subject contrast when beam energy is increased
- incorrect selection can cause inadequate receptor exposure
- does not remove the need for correct kVp, mAs, collimation, and positioning

---

## 10. How filtration is co-related with other exposure factors

Filtration is part of a connected exposure system:

1. The X-ray tube produces a heterogeneous spectrum.
2. Inherent and added filters remove low-energy photons.
3. The beam becomes harder, with higher mean energy and HVL.
4. Total photon intensity falls at unchanged mAs.
5. mAs or exposure time may need adjustment to maintain detector exposure.
6. The patient receives fewer low-energy photons and usually a lower entrance skin dose.
7. Beam penetration and transmitted radiation increase.
8. Image contrast and scatter conditions may change.
9. Compensating filters additionally equalize exposure across different tissue thicknesses.

Therefore, filtration cannot be considered separately from kVp, mAs, patient thickness, detector response, HVL, and dose. A useful protocol selects all of these together.

---

## 11. Final summary

Filtration is the removal of selected low-energy photons from an X-ray beam. Inherent filtration comes from the tube and housing, while added filtration is deliberately placed in the beam. Aluminium, copper, compound, wedge, bow-tie, mammography, and specialized edge filters are used for different purposes.

Filtration hardens the beam by increasing mean energy, effective energy, penetration, and HVL, while reducing total intensity. It does not increase maximum photon energy. When the same detector exposure is required, mAs or exposure time may need to be increased, but the removal of low-energy photons can reduce unnecessary entrance skin dose.

Thus, filtration links the X-ray spectrum with beam quality, exposure factors, image contrast, detector exposure, patient dose, and radiation protection. Correct filtration makes the beam more useful and efficient without unnecessarily increasing radiation risk.

## 12. Short exam-oriented conclusion

Filtration is the removal of low-energy photons from an X-ray beam using absorbing materials. Inherent filtration is provided by the tube and housing, whereas added filtration is placed deliberately in the beam. Aluminium, copper, compound, wedge, bow-tie, and specialized target-filter combinations are common types. Filtration removes low-energy photons, increases beam hardness, mean energy, penetration, and HVL, and reduces total intensity. If detector exposure must remain constant, mAs or exposure time may need compensation. By reducing photons that would be absorbed superficially, filtration reduces unnecessary patient skin dose. Its effects are therefore directly related to the X-ray spectrum, beam quality, exposure factors, image formation, and radiation protection.
