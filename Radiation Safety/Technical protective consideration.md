# Technical Protective Consideration During Radiography

This document explains technical protective considerations for radiography modalities and how they interrelate to reduce patient and staff dose.

## Topics
- Evaluation of hazards
- Effective communication

- Filtration

- Mammography

- CT scan

## Evaluation of hazards
Systematic hazard evaluation identifies radiation sources (primary beam, scatter, leakage), likely exposed populations (patients, staff, public), procedure frequency, and environmental constraints. Methods:
- Walkthrough surveys with a calibrated meter
- Workload estimation (exposures per day/week and typical mA·s)
- Task analysis (who is where during exposures)
- Incident and near‑miss review

Output: prioritized list of risks, required engineering controls (shielding, beam limitation), administrative controls (procedures, training), and PPE needs.

## Effective communication
Clear pre‑procedure instructions and intra‑procedure signals reduce repeats and inadvertent exposures. Elements:
- Pre‑exposure briefing for the patient
- Staff roles and audible/visual exposure indicators
- Clear signage and cordoned exclusion zones for mobile procedures

Good communication reduces unnecessary movements, the need for manual restraint, and the likelihood of repeat exposures.

## Immobilization
Use mechanical immobilization devices (positioning aids, straps, cushions) to prevent motion that would cause repeats. Prefer devices that do not require staff to hold patients during exposure. For paediatrics, use dedicated immobilization tools and distraction techniques to reduce distress and movement.

## Beam‑limiting devices
Collimators, diaphragms and shutters restrict the irradiated field to the anatomy of interest. Benefits:
- Reduces patient dose and skin exposure
- Reduces scatter production (improves image contrast and lowers staff/adjacent area dose)
- Lowers required secondary shielding by decreasing scatter fluence

Regularly verify light‑beam congruence and collimator alignment.

## Filtration
Inherent and added filtration remove low‑energy photons that increase patient skin dose without improving image quality. Typical filters:
- Aluminium (general radiography)
- Copper or additional aluminium for high kVp
- Molybdenum/Rhodium for mammography

Appropriate filtration reduces patient dose, alters beam quality (hardening), and can change optimal exposure factors.

## Exposure factors
Selecting kVp, mA and exposure time balances image quality and dose. Principles:
- Use the highest kVp consistent with diagnostic contrast to reduce mAs (lower patient dose), but recognise higher kVp increases scatter penetration.
- Use automatic exposure control (AEC) where appropriate and ensure correct detector positioning.
- Tailor factors to patient size and clinical question.

Exposure factors directly affect workload and shielding considerations; high throughput or high kVp techniques increase shielding requirements.

## Protection in fluoroscopy
Fluoroscopy has continuous or pulsed beams and potentially long beam‑on times. Key protections:
- Use pulsed fluoroscopy, last‑image hold and dose‑saving software
- Maximise distance for staff; use ceiling‑mounted lead screens and table skirts
- Limit fluoroscopy time and collimate tightly
- Monitor staff doses and use real‑time dose feedback when available

Fluoroscopy increases staff dose and scatter, so room shielding and local protection must be designed for higher workloads.

## Mammography
Mammography uses low‑energy spectra and tight beam limitation to the breast. Protective considerations:
- Specific filtration and target/filter combinations (Mo/Mo, Mo/Rh, W/Rh)
- Compression and immobilization to decrease motion and repeats
- Shielding focuses on control room placement and observation window glazing when needed

Because mammography uses low energies, shielding materials and window glazing specifications differ from general radiography.

## Mobile radiography
Portable X‑ray units operate in unshielded clinical areas. Controls:
- Portable collimation and reduced field size
- Use of portable lead shields and distancing of bystanders
- Clear communication and temporary exclusion of nearby occupants when practicable

Mobile radiography shifts protection from structural barriers to administrative controls and portable shielding.

## CT scan
CT has high instantaneous dose rates and different workload metrics. Protective considerations:
- Shielding calculations use scanner‑specific workload (mA·s per slice, studies per week)
- Control room and operator window must be designed to attenuate scatter/neutron (for high kVp) as applicable
- Protocol optimisation, iterative reconstruction and patient centring reduce dose

CT scanners create high room workloads; structural shielding and interlocks are essential.

## Angiography / Interventional suites
These suites combine fluoroscopy and interventional procedures with long beam‑on times and close staff proximity. Protections:
- Ceiling‑mounted lead shields, table skirts, and remote operation capability
- Mandatory PPE (lead aprons, thyroid shields, lead glasses) and routine dosimetry
- Procedural planning to minimise exposure (team positions, imaging angles)

Interventional rooms require robust shielding and operational controls because staff doses can accumulate rapidly.

## How these topics are correlated
- Hazard evaluation determines which combination of engineering, administrative, and PPE controls are required.
- Beam limiting, filtration and exposure factors directly reduce patient dose and scatter production — lowering risks to staff and adjacent areas.
- Immobilization and communication reduce repeats and fluoroscopy time, decreasing workload (W) used in shielding design.
- Modalities differ in typical workload and beam characteristics (CT and interventional high; mammography low energy), which drives shielding material choices and thickness.
- Mobile radiography relies more on procedural controls and portable shielding than fixed barriers.
- Protective devices (PPE) protect staff in the room; structural shielding protects adjacent areas and the public. Both are required for a complete protection strategy.
