# Radiographic Positioning: 3-Mark Exam Answers

*Study notes based on common conventional-radiography positioning. Exact centering and angles can vary by textbook, equipment, patient condition, and local protocol. In trauma, do not force a position; modify the view and follow the supervising radiographer/department protocol.*

## 1. Positioning landmarks of the skull

Skull landmarks and baseline lines provide reproducible head alignment and prevent rotation or tilt.

- **Nasion:** junction of the frontal and nasal bones; anterior reference point.
- **Glabella:** smooth prominence between the eyebrows; useful for midline alignment.
- **Acanthion:** tip of the anterior nasal spine, at the base of the nose.
- **External auditory meatus (EAM):** opening of the ear canal; common posterior reference point.
- **Outer canthus:** outer corner of the eye; used with the EAM to form a positioning line.
- **Infraorbital margin:** lower bony edge of the orbit; used for the infraorbitomeatal line.
- **Inion:** palpable external occipital protuberance at the back of the skull.
- **Vertex:** highest point of the skull.
- **Gonion:** angle of the mandible.

Important lines: **OML** (outer canthus to EAM), **IOML** (infraorbital margin to EAM), **AML** (acanthion to EAM), and **MML** (mentum/chin to EAM). Align the appropriate line and the midsagittal plane according to the projection being performed.

## 2. Mummy-wrap technique

The mummy wrap is a temporary immobilisation method for infants and young children who cannot reliably remain still for an exposure. A clean blanket or purpose-made swaddle is wrapped snugly around the body, with arms safely alongside the body and the examination area exposed. A second staff member may assist; use the least restrictive method that achieves stillness.

**Safety:** obtain the caregiver’s cooperation, maintain the child’s airway and breathing, avoid tight pressure on the chest or abdomen, monitor continuously, and remove the wrap promptly after imaging. Never use it to restrain a child without appropriate supervision and consent.

	  Head free and observed
		  O
	    ___/|\___
	   /  wrapped \
	  |   trunk    |  ← snug blanket; chest can expand

	   \__________/
	    legs supported

## 3. Thumb AP view

**Purpose:** assesses the phalanges, first metacarpal, and thumb joints for fracture, dislocation, or arthritis.

- Seat the patient beside the table; place the hand palm-down. Abduct and extend the thumb away from the fingers so it lies flat without rotation; support the hand as needed.
- Centre the CR to the first metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint; collimate to include the thumb and its joints. Use a lateral and/or oblique view as required for a complete series.
- The image should show the thumb in AP alignment, with the phalanges, first metacarpal, and interphalangeal, MCP, and first carpometacarpal joints included.

## 4. Operating principle of orthopantomography (OPG)

OPG is panoramic tomography of the jaws. The X-ray tubehead and detector rotate around the patient in opposite directions. A narrow beam scans the dental arches while a curved **focal trough** (image layer) is brought into focus; structures inside or near that layer appear relatively sharp, while structures outside it are blurred or distorted.

The patient is positioned with the teeth in the bite-block groove, midsagittal plane vertical, and head stabilised; the tongue is held against the palate during exposure. One scan displays both jaws and teeth, useful for impacted teeth, jaw lesions, fractures, and dental assessment. Accurate positioning is essential because rotation or incorrect chin height causes distortion and unequal magnification.

## 5. Pelvic inlet view

The AP axial pelvic inlet projection shows the pelvic brim and ring, including the sacral promontory and pubic symphysis. It is commonly requested in pelvic trauma to assess displacement and the integrity of the ring.

- Position supine, with the pelvis straight and legs internally rotated slightly if safe and tolerated. Do not manipulate a suspected unstable fracture.
- Direct the CR on the midline, approximately 5 cm (2 in) above the symphysis pubis, with a **caudad** angle commonly around 40° (protocols may vary).
- Collimate to the pelvis. The inlet should be open, with the sacral promontory and pelvic brim visible; assess symmetry and displacement.

## 6. Planes of the body

- **Median (midsagittal):** divides the body into equal right and left halves.
- **Sagittal:** parallel to the median plane; divides right and left portions, not necessarily equally.
- **Coronal (frontal):** divides the body into anterior and posterior portions.
- **Transverse (axial/horizontal):** divides the body into superior and inferior portions.
- **Oblique:** passes through the body at an angle to the standard planes.

```text
		    CORONAL
	    anterior | posterior
			 |
     left  --------+-------- right
		 MEDIAN/SAGITTAL

	 TRANSVERSE:  upper half
			  ---------
			  lower half
```

These planes describe anatomy and the orientation of radiographic, CT, and MRI images.

## 7. Forearm AP and lateral views

**AP:** seat the patient with the elbow extended if possible, forearm supinated (palm up), and the posterior forearm against the detector. Keep the humeral epicondyles parallel to the detector. Centre to the mid-forearm and include both wrist and elbow joints.

**Lateral:** flex the elbow about 90° if possible, place the forearm in a true lateral position with the thumb upward, and superimpose the epicondyles. Centre to the mid-forearm and include both joints. If movement or trauma prevents standard positioning, use a modified view rather than forcing the arm.

The two views show the radius and ulna in orthogonal planes and help define fractures, alignment, and joint involvement.

## 8. Abdominal quadrants and nine regions

The **four quadrants** are formed by the median plane and a horizontal transumbilical plane:

- **RUQ:** liver, gallbladder, duodenum, head of pancreas, right kidney, hepatic flexure.
- **LUQ:** stomach, spleen, body/tail of pancreas, left kidney, splenic flexure.
- **RLQ:** caecum, appendix, lower ascending colon, right ureter and adnexa.
- **LLQ:** sigmoid colon, lower descending colon, left ureter and adnexa.

The **nine regions** are formed by two midclavicular vertical lines and two horizontal planes (commonly subcostal and transtubercular):

```text
┌──────────────────┬──────────────────┬──────────────────┐
│ Right             │ Epigastric        │ Left              │
│ hypochondriac     │                   │ hypochondriac     │
├──────────────────┼──────────────────┼──────────────────┤
│ Right lumbar      │ Umbilical         │ Left lumbar       │
├──────────────────┼──────────────────┼──────────────────┤
│ Right iliac       │ Hypogastric       │ Left iliac        │
│ (inguinal)        │ (pubic)           │ (inguinal)        │
└──────────────────┴──────────────────┴──────────────────┘
```

These divisions help document pain, examination findings, and the likely location of disease. Organ positions overlap and vary; the divisions are clinical guides, not exact boundaries.

## 9. Foot AP weight-bearing view

This dorsoplantar projection is taken with the patient standing to demonstrate the foot under load, useful for alignment, arch, and deformity assessment.

- Patient stands on the detector with weight distributed evenly on the foot being examined; keep the foot flat and straight. For comparison, both feet may be imaged together according to protocol.
- Direct the CR through the base of the third metatarsal, commonly with about 10° posterior angulation toward the heel; follow local protocol and detector setup.
- Include the entire foot and ankle region as requested. Check that the metatarsals and tarsals are included and that weight-bearing alignment is demonstrated.

Do not ask a patient with acute injury or unsafe weight-bearing ability to stand; use a non-weight-bearing alternative if clinically appropriate.

## 10. Care and protection during paediatric radiography

- Explain the procedure in age-appropriate language; involve the caregiver, verify identity and request, and use consent/assent procedures required locally.
- Apply **ALARA**: justify the examination, use paediatric exposure settings, tight collimation, correct exposure factors, and the minimum number of images needed. Avoid repeats through careful preparation and immobilisation.
- Use a caregiver or trained assistant only when necessary, with appropriate protective measures and outside the primary beam; check pregnancy status where relevant. Use immobilisation devices safely, maintain communication and observation, and never compromise breathing or circulation.
- Follow current local shielding guidance; shielding must not obscure anatomy or interfere with automatic exposure control. Document any necessary assistance and provide privacy, warmth, and reassurance.

## 11. Indications for swimmer’s view

The swimmer’s (cervicothoracic lateral) projection demonstrates the **C7–T1 junction**, which may be hidden by the shoulders on a standard lateral cervical-spine image. It is indicated when the lower cervical spine/cervicothoracic junction is not adequately seen, especially in trauma assessment or suspected fracture/dislocation. One arm is raised and the opposite shoulder depressed to separate the shoulders; do not move the arms if spinal injury precautions prohibit it.

## 12. AP “wagging jaw” method

The AP “wagging jaw” method is a modified cervical-spine projection used to demonstrate the upper cervical vertebrae, particularly C1–C2, when the mandible obscures them. The patient gently moves the jaw continuously during a relatively long exposure; jaw movement blurs the mandible while the cervical vertebrae remain sharp. Position the patient AP with the neck as straight as safely possible and centre to the cervical spine according to the department’s protocol. It is not suitable when jaw movement is painful or when trauma precautions prohibit movement; use an approved alternative in those situations.

## 13. Cranial bones

The eight bones of the neurocranium are: **frontal (1), parietal (2), temporal (2), occipital (1), sphenoid (1), and ethmoid (1)**. The facial skeleton is separate and has 14 bones; do not include those when asked specifically for cranial bones.

## 14. Lumbar spine erect lateral positioning

- Stand the patient in a true lateral position with the side of interest against the detector, shoulders and hips superimposed, and the midsagittal plane parallel to the detector. Flex knees slightly and bring arms forward to avoid superimposition.
- Centre at approximately L3 (about 4 cm above the iliac crest), with the CR perpendicular; use a horizontal beam if the patient must remain erect. Collimate to include T12 through the sacrum.
- Suspend breathing for exposure. A radiopaque side marker is used. The image should show open intervertebral spaces as far as anatomy permits, with posterior vertebral margins superimposed.

Erect lateral imaging can demonstrate alignment under load; follow the clinical request and local protocol.

## 15. Indications for chest lateral view

A lateral chest view is usually obtained with the PA chest to localise abnormalities in depth and reveal regions obscured on the frontal image. Indications include suspected or known lung/mediastinal lesion, retrosternal or retrocardiac disease, pleural effusion, and assessment of heart size or lower lobes. The patient is usually erect with arms raised; the left side is placed against the detector for a routine lateral to reduce cardiac magnification, unless the clinical question specifies otherwise.

## 16. Intercondylar fossa of the knee

The intercondylar fossa (notch) is demonstrated by a tangential projection. Common methods include **Camp–Coventry** (prone, knee flexed; CR angled caudad), **Beclere** (supine, knee flexed; CR angled cephalad), and **Holmblad** (kneeling/flexed-knee method). Exact flexion and tube angle depend on the method and patient mobility.

Centre the CR to the knee joint/intercondylar region and align it with the chosen method so the femoral condyles do not obscure the notch. These views assess osteochondral defects, loose bodies, and the intercondylar region. Do not force flexion in acute injury.

## 17. Views demonstrating posterior elements of vertebrae

Oblique projections are the principal views for the **zygapophyseal (facet) joints** and pars interarticularis: cervical obliques show the intervertebral foramina; lumbar obliques show the facets and pars (the “Scottie dog” appearance). AP views show pedicles and spinous/transverse processes, while lateral views show posterior vertebral alignment. The requested projection depends on the particular posterior element and spinal region.

## 18. Indications for Towne’s view

Towne’s is an AP axial skull projection. It is used to demonstrate the occipital bone, foramen magnum, dorsum sellae and posterior clinoid processes, and the petrous pyramids in the foramen magnum. It may be requested for suspected occipital injury or assessment of these structures. The standard CR angle is commonly 30° caudad to the OML (or 37° to the IOML); use the protocol specified by the department. Avoid positioning that risks movement of an unstable cervical spine.

## 19. Cephalometry

Cephalometry is the standardised radiographic measurement of the skull and facial bones, commonly using a lateral cephalogram and sometimes a PA view. A cephalostat holds the head reproducibly, with the teeth in a specified position and the central ray aligned to the ear-rod region. Tracings and measurements assess craniofacial growth, jaw relationships, and orthodontic treatment before and after intervention. Consistent positioning and magnification are essential for comparison.

## 20. Mastoid views

Traditional projections for the mastoid and temporal bone include:

- **Schüller / modified Law:** oblique lateral projection, commonly used to show mastoid air cells and the external auditory canal.
- **Stenvers:** oblique projection of the petrous temporal bone; historically used for the petrous pyramid and inner-ear region.
- **Mayer:** AP axial oblique projection of the petrous temporal bone/mastoid region.

These are conventional radiographic methods; high-resolution CT is generally more informative for detailed temporal-bone assessment. Follow local naming and positioning standards because eponyms/angles vary.

## 21. Projections for Hill–Sachs lesion

A Hill–Sachs lesion is an impaction defect of the posterolateral humeral head associated with anterior shoulder dislocation. Useful projections include **AP internal rotation** (profiles the posterolateral humeral head), **Stryker notch** (tangential view of the humeral head), and **Garth / apical oblique**. The axillary view helps assess dislocation; CT or MRI may be needed to define lesion size. Do not force rotation in an acute painful shoulder.

## 22. Ankle mortise view

The mortise view is an AP oblique ankle projection that opens the distal tibiofibular joint and ankle mortise.

- Patient supine or seated with leg extended; internally rotate the entire leg and foot about 15–20° until the intermalleolar line is parallel to the detector.
- Centre the CR midway between the malleoli, perpendicular to the detector; include distal tibia/fibula and proximal talus.
- The mortise should appear open and symmetrical, with minimal talar overlap by the malleoli. It is used for ankle fracture and joint-space assessment.

## 23. Projections for congenital clubfoot

Conventional assessment uses **AP (dorsoplantar) and lateral foot views**, often in **plantar-flexion and dorsiflexion** to assess talocalcaneal alignment and flexibility. In infants, views may be non-weight-bearing; older children may have weight-bearing views if safe and requested. Position gently—never force correction—and use paediatric dose optimisation. Ultrasound or other imaging may be used depending on age and clinical question.

## 24. Special views for the shoulder

Common special projections include:

- **Grashey (true AP oblique):** glenohumeral joint space.
- **Scapular Y:** shoulder alignment/dislocation.
- **Axillary / inferosuperior axial:** relationship of humeral head to glenoid.
- **Transthoracic lateral:** humeral shaft/proximal humerus when movement is limited.
- **Stryker notch:** Hill–Sachs defect.
- **Garth (apical oblique):** shoulder trauma, including Hill–Sachs and glenoid rim.
- **West Point:** anteroinferior glenoid rim (bony Bankart).
- **Neer outlet / supraspinatus outlet:** subacromial outlet and impingement assessment.
- **Zanca:** AC joint, using reduced exposure and slight cephalad CR angle.

Select views according to the clinical question and avoid painful or unsafe movement.

## 25. Occlusal radiography

Occlusal radiography is an intraoral technique in which a large image receptor is placed between the occlusal surfaces of the teeth and the patient gently bites to hold it. The beam is directed through the jaw according to the maxillary or mandibular projection. It shows a relatively broad area and is useful for impacted/supernumerary teeth, jaw expansion, fractures, salivary calculi, and localisation of lesions or foreign bodies. It complements, rather than replaces, other dental views.

## 26. Projection to visualise the sella turcica

The **lateral skull projection** demonstrates the sella turcica in profile and is the standard conventional view for its outline. A well-positioned lateral should show the sella, dorsum sellae, and clinoid processes without significant rotation. CT or MRI is preferred for detailed evaluation of pituitary and sellar disease.

## 27. Ulnar deviation view

For the PA ulnar-deviation wrist view, place the wrist PA on the detector and gently deviate the hand toward the ulna while keeping the forearm stable. Centre to the carpal region and include the wrist. Ulnar deviation elongates the scaphoid and reduces foreshortening, helping assess suspected scaphoid fracture. Do not force deviation if a fracture or severe pain is present; use a modified position as needed.

## 28. Jones method positioning

The Jones method is a modified AP elbow examination used when the elbow is acutely flexed and cannot be extended. Support the arm and obtain separate projections of the distal humerus and proximal forearm as required, directing the CR to the elbow joint and adjusting the angle to the flexion. Do not force extension. Because descriptions of “Jones method” vary, follow the specific departmental/textbook protocol and obtain orthogonal views if safely possible.

## 29. Indications for carpal tunnel view

The tangential carpal-canal view (often **Gaynor–Hart**) is used to show the carpal tunnel, especially the hook of hamate and pisiform. It may be requested for suspected hook-of-hamate fracture, carpal tunnel bony abnormality, or a foreign body. The wrist is dorsiflexed and the hand pulled gently; the CR is angled tangentially to the canal (commonly about 25–30° to the long axis of the hand) and centred at the base of the third metacarpal. Avoid forced hyperextension in trauma or severe pain.

## 30. Scotty dog projection

This is a lumbar oblique projection used to assess the pars interarticularis and facet joints. Position the patient in an oblique rotation, commonly about 45° according to body habitus and protocol, and centre to the lumbar spine. On the image, the oblique lumbar posterior elements resemble a Scottie dog: the **neck** corresponds to the pars; a break in the neck (“collar”) may indicate spondylolysis. CT/MRI may be required if suspicion persists.

## 31. Views for calcaneal spur

Use a **lateral calcaneus/foot view** to demonstrate plantar or posterior calcaneal spurs. An **axial plantodorsal calcaneus view** (Harris–Beath) may be added to assess the calcaneus and subtalar region. Centre and angle the CR according to the selected projection; include the calcaneus. Weight-bearing lateral imaging may be requested for alignment if the patient can stand safely.

## 32. Water’s view

Water’s (occipitomental) view is used mainly to demonstrate the maxillary sinuses and facial bones. Position erect, nose and chin against the detector, with the chin extended so the OML is about 37° to the detector; align the midsagittal plane straight. The CR is perpendicular, exiting at the acanthion. It is used for sinus disease and facial trauma; a horizontal-beam/erect view can show fluid levels. CT is preferred for significant facial trauma.

## 33. Indications for C-spine open-mouth projection

The AP open-mouth (odontoid) view demonstrates the dens and the lateral masses of C1 in relation to C2. It is requested for suspected C1/C2 fracture or dislocation, atlantoaxial alignment, and selected inflammatory conditions. Centre through the open mouth with the teeth and skull base aligned so they do not obscure the dens. In trauma, do not make the patient move the neck; use only when permitted by the trauma protocol and obtain CT when indicated.

## 34. Settegast method

Settegast is a tangential patellar projection for the patellofemoral joint. Position prone, flex the knee to approximately 90° only if tolerated, and direct the CR tangentially to the patellofemoral joint, adjusting the angle to the degree of flexion. It can show patellar facets and osteochondral defects. It is unsuitable when acute injury, severe pain, or instability makes flexion unsafe; use a less flexed alternative.

## 35. Rosenberg method positioning

Rosenberg is a bilateral or unilateral **PA weight-bearing knee** projection with the knees flexed about 45°. The patient stands facing the detector, knees flexed, and the CR is angled caudad (commonly about 10°) to the joint spaces. It demonstrates tibiofemoral joint-space narrowing, especially posterior compartment osteoarthritis, under load. Use support and do not perform weight-bearing flexion if unsafe.

## 36. Knee horizontal-beam view

The horizontal-beam lateral knee is used particularly in trauma when the patient cannot safely stand or be repositioned. Keep the patient supine, place the detector beside the knee, and direct a horizontal CR to the knee joint; flex only slightly if permitted. It can show an effusion and a fat–fluid level (lipohaemarthrosis), which may suggest an intra-articular fracture. Do not flex or rotate a suspected unstable injury unnecessarily.

## 37. SI-joint views

Common conventional views are **AP axial** and **oblique** sacroiliac-joint projections. AP axial is performed supine with the pelvis straight and CR angled cephalad (commonly about 30° for men and 35° for women in traditional protocols) to project the SI joints. Posterior obliques, often about 25–30°, can demonstrate the joint spaces; select the obliquity according to which joint is required and local protocol. These views assess sacroiliitis, degenerative change, and trauma; CT/MRI may be more sensitive.

## 38. Indications and contraindications for Rhese view

Rhese (parieto-orbital oblique) is an optic-foramen projection, traditionally used to assess the optic canal/foramen and optic nerve region.

- **Indications:** suspected optic-canal lesion or fracture, optic-foramen asymmetry, and selected orbital/optic-nerve assessments.
- **Contraindications/precautions:** suspected unstable cervical-spine or facial trauma, inability to rotate/position safely, or severe pain. Do not force the head into the required obliquity; use CT/MRI or an approved alternative where appropriate.

The classic position places the affected side against the detector with the head rotated and extended so the orbitomeatal line and midsagittal plane form the prescribed angle (commonly 53° to the detector). Exact setup varies by text and department.

## 39. Sphenoid sinus views

The **lateral skull/sinus view** demonstrates the sphenoid sinus in profile; a **submentovertex (SMV)** projection can also show the sphenoid region and skull base. Some sinus protocols include an erect lateral or occipitomental view depending on the clinical question. Positioning must be adapted if trauma is suspected. CT is generally more sensitive for sinus disease and anatomy.

### Quick exam-answer pattern

For any positioning question, state: **(1) indication/anatomy shown, (2) patient and part position, (3) CR/centering and angle, (4) image-evaluation point or safety precaution.** For a 3-mark response, prioritise the three details specifically requested and use the exact protocol taught in your course.
