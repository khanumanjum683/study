# Cervical Spine Radiography

## 1. Important exam idea
Cervical spine is an important chapter in radiographic positioning. The most common views are:
- AP open mouth (C1 and C2)
- AP axial
- Oblique cervical spine
- Lateral cervical spine
- Erect cervical spine
- Trauma lateral (horizontal beam)
- Swimmer's view (cervicothoracic junction)
- Special views:
  - Lateral hyperflexion and hyperextension
  - AP (Fuchs method) or PA (Judd method)
  - AP wagging jaw (Ottonello method)

In the exam, always mention:
- Indication
- Patient position
- Central ray (CR)
- Structures shown
- Why the view is taken

---

## 2. Related radiological anatomy
The cervical spine has 7 vertebrae:
- C1 = Atlas
- C2 = Axis
- C3 to C7 = typical cervical vertebrae

Important anatomy:
- Odontoid process (dens)
- Atlas and axis articulation
- Intervertebral foramina
- Facet joints
- Spinous processes
- Vertebral bodies
- Cervicothoracic junction

Important structures in cervical radiographs:
- C1 and C2
- Occipital condyles
- Atlantoaxial joint
- Intervertebral disc spaces
- Pedicles and laminae
- Spinous processes

## 3. AP open mouth view (C1 and C2)

### Indication
- Odontoid fracture
- Atlantoaxial instability
- Trauma to upper cervical spine
- Neck pain after injury

### Patient position
- Patient is supine or erect
- Mouth is open widely
- The line from lower incisors to mastoid tip is perpendicular to the film
- The neck is not flexed too much

### Central ray
- Directed perpendicular to the cassette
- Centered through the open mouth, midway between the upper incisors and mastoid tip

### Structures shown
- C1 (atlas)
- C2 (axis)
- Odontoid process (dens)
- Atlantoaxial joint

### Key points
- This view is very important for odontoid process and C1-C2 region
- The mouth must be open so the teeth do not cover the dens
- This is a very common exam question

### Simple diagram
```text
       X-ray tube
           |
           V
   Patient mouth open
     Lower incisors to mastoid tip aligned
          C1  C2  Dens seen
      AP Open Mouth view
```

### Exam answer
"AP open mouth view is taken to visualize the atlas and axis, especially the odontoid process. The patient is positioned with the mouth opened widely and the line from the lower incisors to the mastoid tip placed perpendicular to the film. The central ray is directed perpendicular to the cassette through the open mouth. This projection shows C1, C2 and the odontoid process clearly and is useful in suspected fracture or atlantoaxial instability."

## 4. AP axial cervical spine view

### Indication
- Cervical trauma
- Spondylosis
- Degenerative disease
- Tumor or infection

### Patient position
- Patient is supine or erect
- Neck is extended slightly
- Chin is raised to avoid jaw overlap
- The spine is centered to the film

### Central ray
- Directed 15° to 20° cephalad
- Centered to the thyroid cartilage or C4 region

### Structures shown
- C3 to C7 vertebral bodies
- Intervertebral disc spaces
- Spinous processes
- Lower cervical vertebrae

### Key points
- This view gives a good general view of the cervical spine
- It helps to see the lower cervical vertebrae clearly
- The chin must be raised to prevent the mandible from hiding the cervical vertebrae

### Simple diagram
```text
      X-ray tube
          |
          V
   Patient supine/erect
   Neck slightly extended
   CR 15°-20° cephalad
   C3-C7 seen clearly
       AP Axial cervical spine
```

### Exam answer
"AP axial cervical spine view is taken to visualize the lower cervical vertebrae. The patient is positioned with the neck slightly extended and chin raised to avoid overlap of the mandible. The central ray is directed 15° to 20° cephalad and centered to the lower cervical region. This view shows the vertebral bodies, intervertebral spaces and lower cervical vertebrae clearly."

---

## 5. Oblique cervical spine view

### Indication
- Foraminal stenosis
- Nerve root compression
- Facet joint disease
- Trauma
- Cervical spondylosis

### Patient position
- Patient is erect or supine
- Body is rotated 45°
- The head is turned in the same direction as the body
- One side is closer to the film

### Central ray
- Directed 15° to 20° cephalad
- Centered to C4

### Structures shown
- Intervertebral foramina
- Pedicles
- Facet joints
- Vertebral bodies

### Key points
- Oblique view is used to see the intervertebral foramina clearly
- It helps to identify foraminal narrowing and nerve root involvement
- Right and left oblique views are taken separately

### Simple diagram
```text
         Patient rotated 45°
       Side near film shows foramina
      Open foramina  and facet joints
             Oblique cervical view
```

### Exam answer
"Oblique cervical spine view is taken to visualize the intervertebral foramina and facet joints. The patient is rotated 45° with the head turned in the same direction. The central ray is directed 15° to 20° cephalad and centered to C4. This view is useful in evaluating foraminal narrowing, nerve root compression and facet joint pathology."

---

## 6. Lateral cervical spine view

### Indication
- Neck pain
- Trauma
- Fracture
- Dislocation
- Infection
- Tumor

### Patient position
- Patient is erect or supine
- Side of the neck is against the cassette
- The neck is in true lateral position
- Shoulder depression is done if needed
- Full inspiration or suspended breathing may be used

### Central ray
- Directed horizontal to the cervical spine
- Centered to C4

### Structures shown
- Vertebral bodies
- Intervertebral disc spaces
- Spinous processes
- Facet joints
- Lower cervical vertebrae

### Key points
- This is the most important cervical spine view
- Best view to see alignment and fracture
- Shoulders must be depressed to avoid covering lower cervical vertebrae

### Simple diagram
```text
       X-ray tube
           |
           V
    Patient in true lateral position
   Shoulder depression to show lower C-spine
      C1 to T1 seen in lateral view
         Lateral cervical spine
```

### Exam answer
"Lateral cervical spine view is the most important projection for cervical trauma and alignment. The patient is positioned in true lateral with the cervical spine against the cassette and the shoulders depressed to visualize the lower cervical vertebrae. The central ray is directed horizontal to C4. This view demonstrates vertebral alignment, intervertebral spaces, spinous processes and suspected fracture or dislocation clearly."

## 7. Erect cervical spine view

### Indication
- Postural study
- Cervical alignment
- Degenerative disease
- Pain in standing position

### Patient position
- Patient stands upright
- Side against cassette
- Neck in neutral position or slight extension

### Central ray
- Directed horizontal
- Centered to C4

### Structures shown
- Natural cervical curvature
- Vertebral alignment
- Intervertebral spaces

### Key points
- It helps to assess cervical posture and alignment
- It is often used when patient is stable and can stand

### Exam answer
"Erect cervical spine view is taken to study the natural cervical alignment in the upright position. The patient stands and the side of the neck is placed against the cassette. The central ray is directed horizontal to C4. This view helps in evaluating posture and alignment of cervical vertebrae."

---

## 8. Trauma lateral view (horizontal beam)

### Indication
- Cervical trauma
- Suspected fracture or dislocation
- Condition where patient cannot move

### Patient position
- Patient is supine or lying down
- Horizontal beam is used
- No movement of the neck
- The chest and shoulders are handled carefully

### Central ray
- Directed horizontal beam to C4

### Structures shown
- Cervical vertebral alignment
- Fracture or dislocation
- Soft tissue swelling

### Key points
- This is an emergency view in trauma
- Horizontal beam is used because the patient cannot move
- It is done without disturbing the neck

### Exam answer
"Trauma lateral cervical spine view is done in patients with suspected cervical injury when movement is dangerous. A horizontal beam is used because the patient cannot move freely. The central ray is directed horizontally to C4. This view helps in detecting fracture, dislocation or abnormal alignment without moving the patient."

---

## 9. Swimmer's view (cervicothoracic junction)

### Indication
- Upper thoracic and lower cervical junction pathology
- Fracture at cervicothoracic junction
- Poor visualization of C7-T1 junction

### Patient position
- Patient is lateral
- One arm is raised above head
- The other arm is down
- This helps to move the humeral head away from the vertebrae

### Central ray
- Directed perpendicular or slightly angled
- Centered to cervicothoracic junction

### Structures shown
- C7 and T1 vertebrae
- Cervicothoracic junction
- Lower cervical spine

### Key points
- This view is used when C7-T1 region is not seen in lateral cervical spine
- It is also called cervical thoracic junction view

### Exam answer
"Swimmer's view is taken to demonstrate the cervicothoracic junction, especially the C7-T1 region. The patient is placed in lateral position and one arm is raised above the head to move the humeral head away from the vertebrae. The central ray is directed to the cervicothoracic region. This view is useful when the lower cervical vertebrae are not clearly seen in the standard lateral view."

## 10. Special views

### 10.1 Lateral hyperflexion and hyperextension

#### Indication
- Ligament injury
- Instability
- Cervical spine mobility study

#### Patient position
- Patient is lateral
- Hyperflexion: patient bends neck forward
- Hyperextension: patient bends neck backward

#### Central ray
- Directed horizontal to C4

#### Structures shown
- Intersegmental movement
- Ligamentous instability
- Spinal alignment in flexion and extension

#### Exam answer
"Lateral hyperflexion and hyperextension views are taken to assess cervical spine mobility and ligamentous instability. In hyperflexion, the patient bends the neck forward, while in hyperextension, the neck is bent backward. The central ray is directed horizontal to C4. These views show abnormal movement or instability between vertebrae."

---

### 10.2 AP (Fuchs method) or PA (Judd method)

#### Indication
- Odontoid process evaluation
- Atlantoaxial joint study
- When open mouth view is not possible

#### Patient position
- Fuchs method: patient is supine, chin raised, vertex aligned
- Judd method: patient is prone, head extended

#### Central ray
- Centered to the tip of the mastoid or odontoid region

#### Structures shown
- Odontoid process
- Foramen magnum
- Atlantoaxial articulation

#### Key points
- These are special views to visualize the dens when open mouth projection is not adequate

---

### 10.3 AP wagging jaw view (Ottonello method)

#### Indication
- Cervical vertebral bodies without mandible overlap
- To see lower cervical spine clearly

#### Patient position
- Patient is AP position
- The patient moves the jaw continuously during exposure

#### Central ray
- Directed to C4

#### Structures shown
- Cervical vertebrae
- Avoids mandible overlap

#### Key points
- The jaw is moved to blur the mandible and reveal the cervical spine

### Exam answer
"AP wagging jaw view is used to blur the mandible so that the cervical vertebrae can be seen clearly. The patient is positioned in AP projection and the jaw is moved continuously during exposure. The central ray is directed to C4. This view is useful when the upper cervical vertebrae are obscured by the mandible."

## 13. Easy 5-mark answer
"Cervical spine radiography includes AP open mouth, AP axial, oblique, lateral, trauma lateral and special views such as swimmer's view and hyperflexion/hyperextension. AP open mouth view is taken with the mouth open to see the atlas, axis and odontoid process. AP axial view is used to show the lower cervical spine with the central ray directed 15° to 20° cephalad. Oblique view is taken at 45° rotation to view the intervertebral foramina. Lateral cervical spine is the most important view and is used to detect fractures, dislocation and alignment. Swimmer's view is used to see the cervicothoracic junction when C7-T1 is not visible."

---

## 14. Final revision summary
Cervical spine is one of the most common exam topics. The basic views are AP open mouth, AP axial, oblique, lateral and trauma lateral. Special views are used for dens, instability and cervicothoracic junction. Always remember the patient position, CR direction, and the main structures shown. If you write these points correctly, you can score well.
